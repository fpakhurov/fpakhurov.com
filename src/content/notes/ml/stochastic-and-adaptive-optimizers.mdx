The [gradient descent note](/notes/ml/gradient-descent) ended with two costs. Every step reads all $n$ examples, and the number of steps grows with the condition number $\kappa$. This note attacks both. The first cost goes away when the gradient is estimated from a small random sample of examples: the steps become cheap and noisy, and the noise changes how the step size must behave. The second is reduced by remembering past gradients (momentum) and by giving each coordinate its own step size (AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam). Adam trains almost every neural network in the Deep Learning, NLP and Generative Models courses, so it is worth knowing exactly what each of its lines does.

**Notation.** The loss is an average over examples, $Q(w) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell_i(w)$, where $\ell_i$ is the loss on example $i$; for least squares $\ell_i(w) = \frac12(\langle w, x_i\rangle - y_i)^2$ in this note. A **minibatch** $S$ is a random set of $B$ indices, $g = \frac1B\sum_{i \in S}\nabla\ell_i(w)$ is the **stochastic gradient**, and one **epoch** is $\lceil n/B\rceil$ steps, one pass over the data. $\eta_k$ is the step size at step $k$, $\beta$ the momentum coefficient, and $m$, $v$ running averages of gradients and squared gradients. Products and squares of vectors in the adaptive methods are taken coordinate by coordinate. $H$, $\mu$, $L$ and $\kappa$ are the Hessian, its extreme eigenvalues and their ratio, as before.

## A gradient from a sample

The gradient of an average is the average of the gradients, $\nabla Q(w) = \frac1n\sum_i\nabla\ell_i(w)$. Pick one index $i$ uniformly at random: then $E[\nabla\ell_i(w)] = \nabla Q(w)$, so a single example's gradient is an **unbiased estimate** of the full one. Averaging $B$ independently drawn examples keeps the mean and divides the covariance by $B$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[g] &= \nabla Q(w), \\
\operatorname{Cov}(g) &= \frac1B\,\Sigma(w), \\
\Sigma(w) &= \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \big(\nabla\ell_i(w) - \nabla Q(w)\big)\big(\nabla\ell_i(w) - \nabla Q(w)\big)^\top .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the [Monte Carlo estimate](/notes/probability/law-of-large-numbers#monte-carlo-estimation) of an average, and its error falls like $1/\sqrt B$. **Stochastic gradient descent** (SGD) takes the step $w \leftarrow w - \eta g$ with that estimate. A step costs $O(Bd)$ instead of $O(nd)$. In practice the examples are shuffled once per epoch and taken in consecutive batches, sampling without replacement. At a fixed weight vector its batch covariance is smaller by the finite-population factor $(n-B)/(n-1)$. Across an epoch, batches and current weights become dependent, so the IID proof above does not apply unchanged.

We use a newly specified **synthetic sensor fixture**, not observations from people or equipment. Generate 240 independent rows from eight standard-normal draws $z_1,\ldots,z_8$. Set $x_1=z_1$, $x_2=z_1+0.08z_2$ and $x_j=z_j$ for $j=3,\ldots,8$. The target is $y=20+4x_1+4x_2-3x_3+2x_4+4\varepsilon$, with independent $\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)$. The columns are `sensor_a`, `sensor_b`, `control`, `ambient` and four auxiliary signals. The redundant pair tests coefficient stability; the last four columns carry no true signal. Seed 20261113 fixes this teaching experiment. The data generator and new calculations were composed for this course with Codex assistance; no external dataset or benchmark scores are inputs.

Standardize all eight inputs and centre the target for this fixed optimization problem. At $w=0$, sample 6000 batches independently with replacement, using seed 20261114. For $B=1,8,64$, the measured gradient RMSE is $28.99,10.05,3.54$; $\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma/B}$ predicts $28.47,10.07,3.56$. The sample estimates fluctuate around the exact finite-dataset variance. Increasing the batch size eightfold reduces the theoretical error by $\sqrt 8$.

Why accept noise at all? Because early in training the full gradient is overkill: a sample of a few hundred examples already points in roughly the right direction, and one epoch of SGD takes $n/B$ steps for the price of one full step. Nor is there a reason to optimize the training loss far more precisely than the training data determine the true minimizer; the statistical error of $\hat w$ is already of order $1/\sqrt n$ ([Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016](#references), section 8.1.3).

## A constant step stalls in a noise ball

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> When the gradient noise does not vanish at the minimum, a constant step does not converge: the iterate settles into a cloud around the minimum whose variance, and so the expected excess loss, is proportional to the step; its radius scales like the square root of the step. Convergence needs steps that decrease, but not too fast.</aside>

**One dimension.** Take $Q(w) = \frac\lambda2 w^2$ and a stochastic gradient $\lambda w + \xi$ with independent noise $\xi$ of mean 0 and variance $s^2$. The update is $w_{k+1} = (1 - \eta\lambda)w_k - \eta\xi_k$. If $0 < \eta\lambda < 2$, the variance $V_k = \operatorname{Var}(w_k)$ obeys $V_{k+1} = (1 - \eta\lambda)^2 V_k + \eta^2 s^2$ and converges to the fixed point

$$
V_\infty = \frac{\eta^2 s^2}{1 - (1 - \eta\lambda)^2} = \frac{\eta s^2}{\lambda(2 - \eta\lambda)} \approx \frac{\eta s^2}{2\lambda} \quad (\eta\lambda \ll 1) .
$$

The expected excess loss $\frac\lambda2 V_\infty$ is about $\eta s^2/4$: proportional to the step. With $\lambda = s = 1$, $V_\infty = 0.1/1.9 = 0.0526$ for $\eta = 0.1$ and $0.01/1.99 = 0.0050$ for $\eta = 0.01$; 20,000 simulated runs give $0.053$ and $0.005$ (the Code section). A ten times smaller step gives a ten times smaller variance, a ball about $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2$ times narrower, but approaches it ten times more slowly, since the mean shrinks by $1 - \eta\lambda$ per step.

**Least squares.** Near the minimum the noise of a single example's gradient $x_i(\langle w, x_i\rangle - y_i)$ comes mainly from the residual noise: its covariance is about $\sigma^2 H$ when the residuals have variance $\sigma^2$ and are independent of $x$. Applying the one-dimensional result to each eigendirection, where the noise variance is $\sigma^2\lambda_i$, gives the excess loss $\sum_i \eta\sigma^2\lambda_i/4 = \eta\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(H)/4$ for small steps.

<NoiseBall />

The figure isolates initial bias and gradient noise in one dimension. It propagates $E[w_k]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(w_k)$ exactly from $w_0=2$, with gradient noise variance 1. The expected loss is $(E[w_k]^2+\operatorname{Var}(w_k))/2$. Constant steps leave a nonzero limit; a decreasing step continues to lower it. This expectation calculation does not claim that every sampled path decreases. The classical step conditions of [Robbins and Monro (1951)](#references) are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k} \eta_k &= \infty, \\
\sum_{k} \eta_k^2 &< \infty :
\end{aligned}
$$

the steps must add up to an infinite distance, so that any starting point can be left behind, while the accumulated noise, whose variance adds up as $\sum\eta_k^2 s^2$, stays finite. Steps $\eta_k \propto 1/k$ satisfy both. For a $\mu$-strongly convex loss with bounded stochastic gradients, $\eta_k = 2/(\mu(k + 1))$ makes a weighted average of the iterates converge at the rate $O(1/k)$ ([Bubeck, 2015](#references), Theorem 6.2), much slower than the linear rate of exact gradient descent: the price of the noise.

A finite run needs a concrete schedule rather than only an asymptotic condition. On the new fixture, start all weights at zero; train for 40 epochs in shuffled batches of 24, with five permutation seeds 400–404 shared across methods. The minimum training loss is $8.456$, and $\kappa=568.9$. SGD at $\eta=0.15$ ends with mean excess loss $0.116$; reducing the step tenfold after 20 epochs gives $0.029$. With $\eta=0.03$, the corresponding values are $0.035$ and $0.035$: an earlier reduction does not automatically help when progress in a flat direction is still slow. These are training-loss comparisons for the stated budget, not test performance or a general ranking of optimizers.

Averaging the iterates also shrinks the ball without touching the step. **Polyak averaging** reports $\bar w_k = \frac1k\sum_{j \le k} w_j$ instead of the last iterate; the exponential moving average of the weights used to train diffusion models is its modern form ([DDPM training in practice](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective#training-in-practice-averaged-weights-and-clipped-gradients)).

## Momentum: remember the direction

The zigzag of the previous note had a simple cause: along the steep direction the gradient flips sign at every step, along the flat one it keeps pointing the same way, but with a small magnitude. Averaging recent gradients cancels the first and accumulates the second. **Momentum**, or the heavy-ball method ([Polyak, 1964](#references)), keeps a velocity $v$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{k+1} &= \beta v_k + \nabla Q(w_k), \\
w_{k+1} &= w_k - \eta\, v_{k+1} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Unrolled, $v_{k+1} = \sum_{j \ge 0}\beta^j\nabla Q(w_{k-j})$, an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients. When the gradient is constant, the velocity grows to $\nabla Q/(1 - \beta)$, so with $\beta = 0.9$ the effective step along a consistent direction is ten times $\eta$; components that alternate in sign largely cancel.

On a quadratic the method can be analysed exactly like gradient descent, now as a two-term recursion per eigendirection ([Goh, 2017](#references)). With the best choice $\eta = 4/(\sqrt L + \sqrt\mu)^2$ and $\beta = \big((\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1)\big)^2$, the error shrinks asymptotically, per step, by

$$
\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1} \quad \text{instead of} \quad \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1} :
$$

the number of steps grows like $\sqrt\kappa$ instead of $\kappa$. For $\kappa = 100$ the factors are $0.818$ and $0.980$. The momentum factor is the spectral radius of the two-term recursion: it sets the rate over many steps, not a contraction at every step. Unlike gradient descent, the heavy ball can first move away from the minimum: from the starting point of the figure below, its error norm grows from $2.88$ to $8.98$ in four steps before it falls. On the quadratic of the figure below, with eigenvalues $4.5$ and $0.045$, a thousandfold reduction of the loss takes gradient descent 173 steps at its best constant step and the optimally tuned heavy ball 39.

**Nesterov's method** evaluates the gradient at the point the velocity is about to carry the iterate to, $v_{k+1} = \beta v_k + \nabla Q(w_k - \eta\beta v_k)$, in the form of [Sutskever et al. (2013)](#references). The look-ahead corrects the velocity before it overshoots. With $\eta = 1/L$ and $\beta = (\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1)$ it needs 17 steps on the same problem, and unlike the heavy ball it keeps the $\sqrt\kappa$ rate on every smooth strongly convex function, not only on quadratics ([Bubeck, 2015](#references), Theorem 3.18). This scaling is optimal in the first-order black-box setting: Theorem 3.15 gives a lower bound proportional to $((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1))^{2(k-1)}$ times $\mu\lVert w_1-w^*\rVert^2/2$ for a worst-case quadratic in a Hilbert space. This is a worst-case loss bound, not a claim that every example needs that many steps.

The optimal quadratic parameters need $\mu$ and $L$. With stochastic gradients, the effective step $\eta/(1-\beta)$ is useful intuition for a sustained direction, not an exact universal formula for the stationary noise. On the new fixture, momentum $\beta=0.9$, $\eta=0.015$ reaches mean excess loss $0.054$ after the same 40 epochs; the halfway cut gives $0.030$. Learning rate, momentum and schedule must be considered together.

## Per-coordinate steps: AdaGrad and RMSProp

Momentum treats all coordinates alike. Features on different scales, or rare features that appear in few examples, call for different step sizes per coordinate, which is what standardization achieved in the gradient descent note by changing the features. Adaptive methods achieve it by changing the steps, using the gradients seen so far.

**AdaGrad** ([Duchi, Hazan and Singer, 2011](#references)) divides each coordinate's step by the root of the sum of its squared gradients:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G_{k} &= G_{k-1} + g_k^2, \\
w_{k+1} &= w_k - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{G_k} + \varepsilon}\, g_k .
\end{aligned}
$$

A coordinate that has seen large gradients gets small steps; a coordinate of a rare feature, whose gradient is mostly zero, keeps a large step for the few examples where it appears. This suits sparse problems: GloVe word vectors are trained with AdaGrad ([GloVe](/notes/nlp/glove#weighted-least-squares)). The weakness is that $G_k$ only grows, so all steps shrink towards zero even when the loss surface stops changing.

**RMSProp** ([Hinton, Srivastava and Swersky, 2012](#references), lecture 6e) replaces the sum by an exponential moving average, $v_k = \rho v_{k-1} + (1 - \rho)g_k^2$ with $\rho = 0.9$, and divides by $\sqrt{v_k}$: the step adapts to the recent size of the gradient instead of its whole history.

## Adam

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Adam combines momentum (a running mean of gradients) with RMSProp (a running mean of squared gradients), corrects both for their start at zero, and steps by their ratio. Each coordinate moves by about $\eta$ when its gradient is consistent, whatever the gradient's scale.</aside>

**Adam** ([Kingma and Ba, 2015](#references), Algorithm 1) keeps two moving averages and steps by their ratio:

$$
\begin{aligned}
m_k &= \beta_1 m_{k-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\, g_k, & \hat m_k &= m_k / (1 - \beta_1^k), \\
v_k &= \beta_2 v_{k-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\, g_k^2, & \hat v_k &= v_k / (1 - \beta_2^k), \\
w_{k} &= w_{k-1} - \eta\, \hat m_k / \big(\sqrt{\hat v_k} + \varepsilon\big), & &
\end{aligned}
$$

with $m_0 = v_0 = 0$ and the defaults $\eta = 10^{-3}$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$.

**Where the bias correction comes from** ([Kingma and Ba, 2015](#references), section 3). Unrolling the recursion, $m_k = (1 - \beta_1)\sum_{i=1}^k \beta_1^{k-i} g_i$. If the gradients have a constant mean $E[g]$, then $E[m_k] = (1 - \beta_1)\sum_{i=1}^k\beta_1^{k-i}\,E[g] = (1 - \beta_1^k)\,E[g]$, by the geometric sum. The average started at zero underestimates the mean by the factor $1 - \beta_1^k$, which is $0.1$ at the first step, and dividing by it removes the bias; the same holds for $v_k$ with $\beta_2$. Ignoring $\varepsilon$, without the correction the first step would be $\eta\cdot 0.1\,g/\sqrt{0.001\,g^2} = 3.16\,\eta$ per coordinate, and with $\beta_2 = 0.999$ the second-moment estimate would stay too small for hundreds of steps.

**Worked example.** A first gradient $g_1 = (100, 0.01)$. Then $\hat m_1 = g_1$ and $\hat v_1 = g_1^2$, so, neglecting the small $\varepsilon$, the first step is approximately $\eta\,(1, 1)$: each coordinate moves by about $\eta$ against the sign of its gradient, although the two gradients differ by a factor of $10^4$. Plain gradient descent would move the first coordinate $10^4$ times farther than the second.

The figure shows what that buys and what it does not. When the valley runs along the coordinate axes, as it does when features have different scales but are uncorrelated, dividing by $\sqrt{\hat v}$ equalizes the curvatures much as standardization did, and Adam needs 13 steps against 173 for gradient descent. When the same valley is rotated, as it is for correlated features, no rescaling of the axes can make it round, and Adam needs 171 steps. Gradient descent and momentum depend only on the geometry of the loss, so they take exactly as many steps in both panels; Adam depends on the coordinate system. Its scaling uses past squared gradients, not an estimate of the full Hessian. The coordinate dependence in this experiment is a limitation of that normalization, not a general guarantee for diagonal curvature.

Three caveats from practice and theory:

- **The step size still matters.** On the new fixture, Adam at $\eta=0.003$ ends with excess loss $21.155$, while $\eta=0.1$ gives $0.045$, under the same 40-epoch budget. The smaller step has not reached the optimum; dividing it tenfold halfway makes the result worse, $28.620$. A schedule cannot replace sufficient progress.
- **Adam need not converge.** [Reddi, Kale and Kumar (2018)](#references) construct convex problems on which Adam does not converge to the optimum, for any fixed $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$ in a range that includes the usual $0.9$ and $0.999$ (Theorems 1–3), because the moving average $v$ forgets a rare large gradient; their AMSGrad uses the running maximum of $v$ instead.
- **Different solutions.** On problems with many minimizers, adaptive methods can converge to different ones than SGD; [Wilson et al. (2017)](#references) found several tasks where they generalized worse. Weight decay also interacts with the division by $\sqrt{\hat v}$, which is why AdamW applies it separately ([weight decay under Adam](/notes/ml/training-deep-networks#weight-decay-is-not-an-l2-penalty-under-adam)).

The code checks the analytic gradient against central finite differences and checks the first bias-corrected Adam step explicitly. The optimizer comparison is implemented in NumPy, with shared permutations and no external framework parity claim.

## What changed

| | Gradient descent | SGD | SGD with momentum | Adam |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gradient | exact, $O(nd)$ | from $B$ examples, $O(Bd)$ | from $B$ examples | from $B$ examples |
| Constant step | converges for a stable step on a smooth convex problem | persistent noise generally leaves a nonzero error | stability and noise depend jointly on step, momentum and problem | no general convergence guarantee |
| Dependence on $\kappa$ (quadratic, exact gradients) | $\kappa$ | $\kappa$ | $\sqrt\kappa$ with tuned $\eta, \beta$ | no general rate guaranteed from $\kappa$ alone |
| Memory per weight | none | none | one velocity | two moments |
| Typical use | small smooth problems | convex models, with a schedule | vision networks, with a schedule | Transformers, most deep learning (as AdamW) |

## Limitations

- **Noise needs a schedule.** When the gradient noise stays nonzero at the minimum, no constant step converges; the schedule adds hyperparameters. The exception is interpolation: if one weight vector fits every example exactly, as in least squares with more features than examples, every per-example gradient vanishes there and constant-step SGD can converge to it. Overparametrized models that fit their training set are close to this regime, the setting of the [double-descent caveat](/notes/ml/bias-variance#the-double-descent-caveat).
- **Momentum's optimal tuning needs $\mu$ and $L$.** The $\sqrt\kappa$ rate holds for the best parameters; with $\beta = 0.9$ and a guessed $\eta$ the gain is smaller, and too much momentum oscillates.
- **Adaptive methods see only the diagonal.** Correlated features leave a rotated valley that per-coordinate steps cannot straighten.
- **No method removes the need to tune $\eta$.** Defaults work for some model families and fail by orders of magnitude for others.
- **Theory and practice diverge for networks.** The guarantees are for convex losses; for neural networks the choices rest on experiments.

## What you should remember

- A minibatch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient with covariance $\Sigma/B$: error $\propto 1/\sqrt B$, cost $\propto B$.
- With a constant step, SGD settles in a noise ball: excess loss about $\eta s^2/4$ in one dimension, $\eta\sigma^2\operatorname{tr}(H)/4$ for least squares. Decreasing steps with $\sum\eta_k = \infty$, $\sum\eta_k^2 < \infty$ converge; in practice, schedules.
- Momentum, $v \leftarrow \beta v + g$, $w \leftarrow w - \eta v$, accumulates consistent directions and cancels oscillating ones; on quadratics it needs $\sqrt\kappa$ instead of $\kappa$ steps. Nesterov's look-ahead keeps that rate on all smooth strongly convex functions.
- AdaGrad divides by the root of the accumulated squared gradients (good for sparse features, steps shrink forever); RMSProp by a moving average of them.
- Adam = momentum + RMSProp + bias correction $1/(1 - \beta^k)$; its first step is approximately $\eta\,\mathrm{sign}(g)$ when $\varepsilon$ is negligible. Coordinate normalization can help with scale differences but does not whiten correlated directions; the step size still needs tuning.

```python
            indices = rng.permutation(len(y))
            for start in range(0, len(y), 24):
                idx = indices[start:start + 24]
                g = X[idx].T @ (X[idx] @ w - y[idx]) / len(idx)
                step += 1
                if method == "sgd":
                    w -= rate * g
                elif method == "momentum":
                    velocity = 0.9 * velocity + g
                    w -= rate * velocity
                elif method == "adam":
                    m = 0.9 * m + 0.1 * g
                    v = 0.999 * v + 0.001 * g * g
                    w -= rate * (m / (1 - 0.9 ** step)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - 0.999 ** step)) + 1e-8)
                else:
                    raise ValueError(method)
        out.append(float(np.mean((X @ w - y) ** 2) / 2 - minimum))
    return {"mean": float(np.mean(out)), "seed_results": out}

w_hat = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
print(round(loss(w_hat), 3), round(float(np.linalg.cond(X.T @ X)), 1))  # 8.456 568.9
for method, rate in [('sgd', 0.03), ('sgd', 0.15), ('momentum', 0.015), ('adam', 0.003), ('adam', 0.1)]:
    print(method, rate, round(train(X, y, method, rate)['mean'], 3), round(train(X, y, method, rate, True)['mean'], 3))
# sgd 0.03 0.035 0.035 | sgd 0.15 0.116 0.029 | momentum 0.015 0.054 0.030
# adam 0.003 21.155 28.620 | adam 0.1 0.045 0.036
```

## Next

Least squares can now be fitted for any $n$ and $d$, by formula or by descent. Being able to fit it does not make the fit good. With many features, or features that nearly repeat each other, the [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#when-the-solution-is-unstable) showed coefficients that swing with every draw of the noise and a training $R^2$ that rises while the validation $R^2$ collapses. The [next note](/notes/ml/regularization) adds a penalty on the size of the weights: ridge and the lasso, the priors behind them, and how to choose the penalty's strength by validation.

The optimizers of this note train the networks of the other courses: the [Deep Learning course](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks) adds warm-up, decoupled weight decay and gradient clipping to them, Adam and AdamW are the defaults of the [Generative Models training runs](/notes/generative-models/training-background#a-training-run-in-practice), and the next section of this course uses them for [neural networks](/notes/ml/neural-networks).
