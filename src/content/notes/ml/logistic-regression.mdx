The [linear classifiers note](/notes/ml/linear-classifiers) ended with a table of what each surrogate loss estimates. The hinge recovers only the side of the boundary; the logistic loss recovers the log-odds $\log\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}$, from which the probability $\eta$ itself follows. Many decisions need that probability, not just the side: an advertiser compares the chance of a click with its price, a clinic weighs a patient's risk against the cost of a test, and the [next note](/notes/ml/classification-metrics) chooses thresholds from costs. **Logistic regression** is the linear model built for this. This note derives it from a probability model of the label, fits it by [maximum likelihood](/notes/probability/maximum-likelihood), works out its gradient and its failure on separable data, and asks when its probabilities can be taken at face value.

**Notation.** In this note the labels are $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$, which suits the Bernoulli distribution; the margin form of the previous note uses $\pm 1$ labels, $2y_i - 1$. The features $x_i \in \mathbb R^d$ include a constant 1, so the bias is one of the weights $w \in \mathbb R^d$. The **score** (or logit) is $z_i = \langle w, x_i\rangle$, the **sigmoid** is $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$, and the model's probability is $p_i = \sigma(z_i)$. $X$ is the $n \times d$ matrix with rows $x_i$, $y$ and $p$ are the vectors of labels and probabilities, and $\eta(x) = P(y = 1 \mid x)$ is the true probability.

## Why the log-odds?

A linear function $\langle w, x\rangle$ takes every real value; a probability lives in $[0, 1]$. Modelling $p$ itself as linear produces "probabilities" of $1.3$ and $-0.2$. The **odds** $p/(1 - p)$ live in $(0, \infty)$ and the **log-odds** in $(-\infty, \infty)$, so the log-odds is a quantity that a linear model can describe without leaving its range:

$$
\log\frac{P(y = 1 \mid x)}{1 - P(y = 1 \mid x)} = \langle w, x\rangle
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
P(y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\langle w, x\rangle}} = \sigma(\langle w, x\rangle) .
$$

The sigmoid is just the inverse of the log-odds. Two of its properties are used below: $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$, and $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\,(1 - \sigma(z))$.

**Why linear log-odds are natural.** By [Bayes' formula in odds form](/notes/probability/conditional-probability-and-bayes#bayes-formula), the posterior log-odds are the prior log-odds plus the log likelihood ratio $\log\frac{p(x \mid y = 1)}{p(x \mid y = 0)}$. When the two classes are Gaussian with a common covariance $\Sigma$ and means $\mu_1, \mu_0$, the quadratic terms of the two log-densities cancel and

$$
\log\frac{p(x \mid y = 1)}{p(x \mid y = 0)} = (\mu_1 - \mu_0)^\top \Sigma^{-1} x + \text{const},
$$

which is linear in $x$ ([Bishop, 2006, Section 4.2.1](#references); the Gaussian itself is in the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian)). The same happens for many other class-conditional families. Logistic regression keeps the conclusion, linear log-odds, and drops the assumption: it models $P(y \mid x)$ directly and never describes how $x$ is distributed.

**Worked example.** Classes $N(1, 1)$ and $N(-1, 1)$ on a line, equally likely. The log likelihood ratio is $\big[(x + 1)^2 - (x - 1)^2\big]/2 = 2x$, so the log-odds are exactly $2x$ and at $x = 0.5$ the probability of class 1 is $\sigma(1) = 0.731$. If class 1 is rarer, with prior $0.2$, the prior log-odds $\log(0.2/0.8) = -1.386$ are added: $\sigma(1 - 1.386) = 0.405$. A change of base rate shifts every log-odds by the same constant, the rule the probability course derived for deployment under a different prevalence.

**Reading the weights.** Increasing feature $j$ by one unit adds $w_j$ to the log-odds, which multiplies the odds by $e^{w_j}$, whatever the other features are: a weight of $0.7$ doubles the odds ($e^{0.7} = 2.01$). This is the **odds ratio** that medical and social-science papers report. It describes the model, and it is causal only if the data allow a causal reading.

## Maximum likelihood gives the log-loss

Each label is a Bernoulli variable with success probability $p_i = \sigma(\langle w, x_i\rangle)$, and the labels are independent given the features. The likelihood of the training labels is $\prod_i p_i^{y_i} (1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}$, and its negative logarithm is

$$
\mathcal L(w) = -\sum_{i=1}^n \Big[ y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i) \log(1 - p_i) \Big] ,
$$

the **log-loss**, or **binary cross-entropy**. That cross-entropy losses are negative log-likelihoods of a categorical model is derived in the [maximum likelihood note](/notes/probability/maximum-likelihood#loss-functions-are-likelihoods); the information-theoretic reading, the surprise of the observed labels under the model's beliefs, is in the [entropy note of the generative models course](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#cross-entropy-surprise-under-a-wrong-belief).

**It is the logistic loss.** For $y_i = 1$ the term is $-\log\sigma(z_i) = \log(1 + e^{-z_i})$. For $y_i = 0$ it is $-\log(1 - \sigma(z_i)) = -\log\sigma(-z_i) = \log(1 + e^{z_i})$. With the margin $M_i = (2y_i - 1)\, z_i$ both cases read

$$
\mathcal L(w) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log\big(1 + e^{-M_i}\big) ,
$$

the logistic surrogate of the previous note, with the natural logarithm. So the surrogate chosen there for its shape is exactly maximum likelihood under the model of linear log-odds.

**Worked example.** One feature, $w = 1$, no bias, three points: $x = (2, -1, 0.5)$ with labels $y = (1, 0, 0)$. The probabilities are $p = \sigma(x) = (0.881, 0.269, 0.622)$. The losses are $-\log 0.881 = 0.127$ for the confident correct point, $-\log(1 - 0.269) = 0.313$ for the moderately correct one, and $-\log(1 - 0.622) = 0.974$ for the third point, which the model places on the wrong side; the total is $1.414$. A model that said $p = 0.99$ for the third point would pay $-\log 0.01 = 4.6$ for it: the log-loss punishes confident mistakes without limit.

## The gradient: residuals times inputs

Differentiate one term with respect to its score. For $y_i = 1$, $\frac{d}{dz}\big[-\log\sigma(z)\big] = -(1 - \sigma(z))$; for $y_i = 0$, $\frac{d}{dz}\big[-\log(1 - \sigma(z))\big] = \sigma(z)$; both cases equal $\sigma(z_i) - y_i$. By the chain rule, with $\partial z_i / \partial w = x_i$,

$$
\nabla \mathcal L(w) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big(\sigma(z_i) - y_i\big)\, x_i = X^\top (p - y) .
$$

The form is the one of least squares, $X^\top(Xw - y)$: prediction minus target, times the input. A point pulls the weights in proportion to how wrong its probability is, and a correctly and confidently classified point, with $p_i \approx y_i$, hardly pulls at all. In the worked example $p - y = (-0.119, 0.269, 0.622)$, the gradient with respect to $w$ is $-0.119 \cdot 2 + 0.269 \cdot (-1) + 0.622 \cdot 0.5 = -0.196$, and with respect to a bias it would be $\sum_i (p_i - y_i) = 0.772$: the predictions are too high on average (their mean is $0.591$, the share of positives $1/3$), and a gradient step lowers the bias.

**Convexity.** Differentiating once more, with $\sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$, gives the Hessian

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^2 \mathcal L(w) &= \sum_{i=1}^n p_i (1 - p_i)\, x_i x_i^\top = X^\top S X, \\
S &= \operatorname{diag}\big(p_i (1 - p_i)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

For any vector $v$, $v^\top X^\top S X v = \sum_i p_i(1 - p_i) \langle x_i, v\rangle^2 \ge 0$, so the log-loss is convex, and strictly convex when $X$ has full column rank (every $p_i$ is strictly between 0 and 1 at finite $w$). Every local minimum is global. The [matrix calculus note](/notes/math/matrix-calculus#the-check-against-logistic-regression) derives the same gradient and Hessian with differentials.

**No closed form, but Newton's method.** Setting the gradient to zero gives $X^\top(\sigma(Xw) - y) = 0$, equations that are nonlinear in $w$, unlike the normal equations of least squares. They are solved iteratively. [Gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent) works; with a few hundred features, **Newton's method** ([derived in the mathematics course](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization#newtons-method)) converges in a handful of steps:

$$
w \leftarrow w - (X^\top S X)^{-1} X^\top (p - y) .
$$

Each step is a weighted least-squares problem with weights $S$ and a working response $Xw + S^{-1}(y - p)$, which is why it is called **iteratively reweighted least squares** ([Bishop, 2006, Section 4.3.3](#references)). On the breast cancer data with two standardized features, 25 Newton steps reach a gradient of $10^{-15}$ and the same weights as scikit-learn (Code section).

**What the optimum guarantees.** The bias is the weight of the constant feature, so the gradient equation for it reads $\sum_i (p_i - y_i) = 0$: at the maximum likelihood fit, the average predicted probability equals the share of positives in the training set. The same holds within any group marked by a binary feature of the model. On the breast cancer fit, the predicted probabilities of malignancy add up to $212.0$, the number of malignant tumours.

## Separable data: the weights run away

If some $w_0$ classifies the training set perfectly, every margin $M_i = (2y_i - 1)\langle w_0, x_i\rangle$ is positive, and scaling $w_0$ by $c > 1$ multiplies them all by $c$. Each term $\log(1 + e^{-cM_i})$ decreases towards zero as $c$ grows, while $\mathcal L(w) > 0$ for every finite $w$. The infimum, zero, is not attained: the maximum likelihood estimate does not exist. It exists exactly when the classes overlap, neither completely nor quasi-completely separated ([Albert and Anderson, 1984](#references)).

Gradient descent on separable data keeps going. Its direction settles down, towards the maximum-margin separator of the [support vector machines note](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines) ([Soudry et al., 2018](#references)), while its norm grows without bound, only logarithmically in the number of steps, and all predicted probabilities drift to 0 and 1. On four points $x = -2, -1, 1, 2$ with the two right ones positive, gradient descent with step $0.5$ from zero has slope $5.55$ after $10^3$ steps, $7.83$ after $10^4$ and $10.13$ after $10^5$: about $\log(t/4)$, so every tenfold increase in steps adds $\log 10 = 2.30$ to the weight and divides the loss, about $2/t$, by ten.

Separation is not exotic. As the previous note showed, random labels on $n$ points in $d$ dimensions are separable with probability $1/2$ when $n = 2(d + 1)$, and real data are far from random. The breast cancer data with all 30 standardized features are linearly separable: as the penalty is relaxed, the fitted weight norm goes from $3.8$ to $1{,}891$ and the training log-loss from $0.053$ to $0.004$ (Code section).

**The penalty fixes it.** Minimizing

$$
\mathcal L(w) + \frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2
$$

adds $\lambda I$ to the Hessian, so the objective is strictly convex and grows without bound in every direction: a unique, finite minimizer exists for any data. The penalty is a Gaussian prior $w \sim N(0, \lambda^{-1} I)$, and the penalized fit is its [MAP estimate](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#map-estimation-and-regularization); the bias is usually left unpenalized. The Bayesian methods course replaces the MAP point by a Gaussian approximation of the whole posterior ([Bayesian logistic regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation#bayesian-logistic-regression)). Larger $\lambda$ keeps $\lVert w\rVert$ small and the probability contours wide apart. scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` applies this penalty by default, with `C = 1` the inverse of its strength; `C = np.inf` gives plain maximum likelihood.

## Calibration: do the probabilities mean what they say?

A model is **calibrated** if, among the examples to which it assigns probability $p$, a share $p$ is positive:

$$
P\big(y = 1 \mid \hat p(x) = p\big) = p \qquad \text{for every } p .
$$

A weather forecaster who says "70% chance of rain" is calibrated if it rains on 70% of such days. Calibration is checked with a **reliability diagram**: split the test predictions into bins (ten equal-width bins are common), and plot the mean prediction in each bin against the share of positives in it. A calibrated model's points lie on the diagonal. The **expected calibration error** summarizes the distance,

$$
\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b} \frac{n_b}{n} \,\big\lvert \bar y_b - \bar p_b \big\rvert ,
$$

with $n_b$ examples, mean prediction $\bar p_b$ and share of positives $\bar y_b$ in bin $b$. The number depends on the bins and on the sample size. Each bin's share of positives is noisy, so even perfectly calibrated predictions, with labels drawn as $y \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$, have a positive ECE: on 10,000 examples it averages $0.010$ with ten bins and $0.022$ with fifty. A reliability diagram should therefore show the count of each bin, and the ECE should be read next to the log-loss and the Brier score below, which need no bins. Calibration alone is not quality: predicting the base rate for everyone is perfectly calibrated and useless. A good probabilistic classifier is calibrated *and* separates the classes, which the next note measures.

**Why the log-loss aims at calibrated probabilities.** At a single point with true probability $\eta$, a prediction $p$ has expected log-loss $-\eta\log p - (1 - \eta)\log(1 - p)$. Its derivative $-\eta/p + (1 - \eta)/(1 - p)$ vanishes at $p = \eta$: the expected loss is minimized by telling the truth. Losses with this property are called **proper scoring rules** ([Gneiting and Raftery, 2007](#references)). The squared error $(y - p)^2$, the **Brier score** ([Brier, 1950](#references)), is proper too; the absolute error $\lvert y - p\rvert$ is not, since its expected value $\eta(1 - p) + (1 - \eta)p$ is linear in $p$ and minimized at $0$ or $1$. Propriety is a statement about the best possible predictor. A fitted logistic regression is calibrated in the large on its training set, by the bias equation above, and calibrated everywhere only to the extent that the true log-odds are linear and the sample is large. Overfitting makes it overconfident; a strong penalty makes it underconfident.

**Other models are not even aiming.** A hinge-trained score is not a probability at all. Naive Bayes multiplies the evidence of correlated features as if it were independent, counts the same signal several times, and tends to push its probabilities towards 0 and 1. Max-margin methods and boosted trees tend to push them away from 0 and 1, giving S-shaped reliability curves ([Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, 2005](#references)). Modern deep networks trained to zero training error tend to be overconfident ([Guo et al., 2017](#references)). These are observed tendencies of model families, not guarantees. In the Code section, naive Bayes on Gaussian classes with correlated features has ECE $0.236$ and log-loss $1.133$, while logistic regression, the correct model there, has $0.012$ and $0.633$. On these 10,000 test examples, ECE values near $0.01$ are at the level of perfectly calibrated predictions, so they cannot rank well-calibrated models against each other.

**Recalibration.** A score that ranks well but is miscalibrated can be repaired by a monotone map fitted on data not used for training (a held-out set or cross-validation, as in the [validation note](/notes/ml/validation-and-overfitting)):

- **Platt scaling** ([Platt, 1999](#references)) fits a one-feature logistic regression on the score: $\hat p = \sigma(a s + b)$, where $s$ is the model's score or the logit of its probability, by maximum likelihood. To limit overfitting on small sets, Platt replaced the targets $1$ and $0$ by $(N_+ + 1)/(N_+ + 2)$ and $1/(N_- + 2)$, with $N_\pm$ the class counts. **Temperature scaling** of neural networks is the special case $b = 0$, a single factor on the logits ([Guo et al., 2017](#references)).
- **Isotonic regression** ([Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002](#references)) fits the best non-decreasing step function of the score in squared error, by the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm ([Ayer et al., 1955](#references)): walk through the examples sorted by score and, whenever a block's mean exceeds the next block's, merge them. For six labels in score order, $0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1$, the second label exceeds the third, the merged block $(1, 0)$ with mean $0.5$ exceeds the fourth label, and the result is $0, \tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13, 1, 1$. It can fix any monotone distortion, but it needs more data: with fewer than a few hundred to a thousand calibration examples Platt scaling usually does better ([Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, 2005](#references)).

Platt's map is strictly increasing (for $a > 0$) and leaves the ranking, and therefore the ROC curve of the next note, unchanged. The isotonic map is only non-decreasing: it can merge neighbouring scores into ties, which changes the AUC little. Both statements are about one fixed score. `CalibratedClassifierCV` with `cv=5`, as in the Code section, fits the model and its map on each of five folds and averages the five calibrated outputs; an average of different models can rank the examples differently from any one of them. In the Code section the AUC of naive Bayes is $0.695$ before and after either recalibration.

## Several classes: the softmax

With $K$ classes, each class gets its own weight vector $w_k$ and score $z_k = \langle w_k, x\rangle$, and the scores are turned into probabilities by the **softmax**:

$$
P(y = k \mid x) = \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}} .
$$

Maximum likelihood gives the cross-entropy loss $-\sum_i \log P(y_i \mid x_i)$, and its gradient has the same residual form, $X^\top(P - Y)$, with $P$ the $n \times K$ matrix of predicted probabilities and $Y$ the one-hot labels. Only differences of scores matter: adding the same vector to every $w_k$ changes nothing, so one class can be fixed at $w_K = 0$. For $K = 2$ the softmax probability of class 1 is $e^{z_1}/(e^{z_1} + e^{z_2}) = \sigma(z_1 - z_2)$, which is binary logistic regression with $w = w_1 - w_2$. The [multiclass note](/notes/ml/multiclass-and-multilabel) develops this model and its alternatives.

## Where logistic regression reappears

- The first text classifier of the NLP course is multinomial logistic regression on word counts, the [bag-of-words model](/notes/nlp/bag-of-words-and-tf-idf#a-linear-classifier-on-counts); it reaches 0.918 accuracy on AG News and remains the baseline in the [text classification note](/notes/nlp/text-classification).
- [Negative sampling in word2vec](/notes/nlp/word2vec#negative-sampling) trains a logistic regression that tells observed (word, context) pairs from random ones, and a language model's output layer is a softmax over the vocabulary trained with cross-entropy, as in the [n-gram note](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models#perplexity-from-probability-to-a-number).
- The discriminator of a GAN is a classifier trained with exactly this log-loss ([GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#where-the-value-function-comes-from-binary-cross-entropy)), and noise-contrastive estimation learns an unnormalized density by logistic regression of data against noise ([energy-based models note](/notes/generative-models/energy-based-models#noise-contrastive-estimation-learning-z-by-classification)).

## What changed

| | Linear classifier with a surrogate loss | Logistic regression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output | a side of the hyperplane | a probability $\sigma(\langle w, x\rangle)$ |
| Loss | chosen for its shape | derived: the negative Bernoulli log-likelihood |
| What the score means | depends on the loss | the log-odds; $e^{w_j}$ is an odds ratio |
| Fitting | gradient methods | the same, plus Newton's method (IRLS) with $\nabla = X^\top(p - y)$ |
| Separable data | many solutions | no maximum likelihood estimate; the penalty (a Gaussian prior) restores one |
| Probabilities | not available | calibrated if the model is right; checked by a reliability diagram, repaired by Platt or isotonic maps |

## Limitations

- **Linear log-odds.** Interactions and nonlinear effects must be supplied as features; otherwise the model is miscalibrated where the true log-odds bend.
- **Separation and near-separation.** Unpenalized fits diverge or give huge, unstable coefficients; the penalty strength then matters and must be chosen by validation, with features on comparable scales.
- **Coefficients are fragile to interpret.** Correlated features share and trade weight, and an odds ratio is conditional on all the other features in the model.
- **Calibration is not discrimination.** A calibrated model can still separate the classes poorly, and calibration measured on one population breaks when the base rate or the feature distribution shifts.
- **Rare classes.** The probabilities can be right while the default threshold of 0.5 almost never fires; choosing the threshold is a separate decision.

## What you should remember

- Logistic regression models the log-odds as linear: $P(y = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(\langle w, x\rangle)$. Gaussian classes with a common covariance give exactly this form.
- Maximum likelihood under the Bernoulli model gives the log-loss, which is the logistic surrogate $\sum_i \log(1 + e^{-M_i})$.
- The gradient is $X^\top(p - y)$, the Hessian $X^\top S X$ with $S = \operatorname{diag}(p_i(1 - p_i))$; the loss is convex, has no closed-form minimizer, and Newton's method is IRLS.
- At the optimum with a bias, the mean predicted probability equals the share of positives.
- On separable data the weights grow without bound and the probabilities go to 0 and 1; an L2 penalty, a Gaussian prior, gives a unique finite fit.
- The log-loss is a proper scoring rule. Check calibration with a reliability diagram that shows its bin counts, and with ECE next to the log-loss or Brier score; repair it with Platt scaling or isotonic regression fitted on held-out data. Platt scaling keeps the ranking; isotonic regression can only add ties.
- The softmax with cross-entropy is the $K$-class version; for $K = 2$ it reduces to the sigmoid.

## Code

The Gaussian log-odds, Newton's method, separation, calibration of naive Bayes, pool adjacent violators and the two-class softmax:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import expit as sigmoid, softmax
from scipy.stats import norm
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.naive_bayes import GaussianNB
from sklearn.calibration import CalibratedClassifierCV
from sklearn.isotonic import IsotonicRegression
from sklearn.metrics import log_loss, roc_auc_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. Gaussian classes N(+1, 1) and N(-1, 1): the log-odds are exactly 2x; a prior of 0.2 adds log(0.25)
x0 = 0.5
log_odds = norm.logpdf(x0, 1) - norm.logpdf(x0, -1)
print(log_odds, round(sigmoid(log_odds), 4), round(sigmoid(log_odds + np.log(0.2 / 0.8)), 4))   # 1.0 0.7311 0.4046

# 2. maximum likelihood by Newton's method (IRLS) on two standardized breast cancer features
data = load_breast_cancer()
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(data.data[:, :2])        # mean radius, mean texture
y = 1.0 - data.target                                       # 1 = malignant
Xb = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X]
w = np.zeros(3)
for _ in range(25):
    p = sigmoid(Xb @ w)
    grad = Xb.T @ (p - y)                                   # the gradient X^T (sigma - y)
    H = Xb.T @ (Xb * (p * (1 - p))[:, None])                # the Hessian X^T S X
    w -= np.linalg.solve(H, grad)
sk = LogisticRegression(C=np.inf, solver='newton-cholesky').fit(X, y)
print(w.round(4), np.r_[sk.intercept_, sk.coef_[0]].round(4))   # [-0.7076  3.722   0.9374] twice
print(f'{np.abs(grad).max():.1e}', round(p.sum(), 4), y.sum())  # 1.2e-15 212.0 212.0: mean prediction = share of positives

# 3. all 30 features: the training set is separable, and the unpenalized loss runs to zero
X30 = StandardScaler().fit_transform(data.data)
for C in (1.0, 1e2, 1e4, 1e6):
    m = LogisticRegression(C=C, max_iter=100_000).fit(X30, y)
    p = m.predict_proba(X30)[:, 1]
    print(C, round(np.linalg.norm(m.coef_), 1), f'{log_loss(y, p):.2g}', int((m.predict(X30) != y).sum()))
# C = 1: |w| 3.8, loss 0.053, 7 errors | 1e2: 21.4, 0.03, 4 | 1e4: 113.8, 0.022, 4 | 1e6: 1891.1, 0.0042, 0

# 4. gradient descent on four separable points: the weight grows like log(t/4), the loss falls like 2/t
Xs, ys = np.array([[1., -2], [1, -1], [1, 1], [1, 2]]), np.array([0, 0, 1, 1.])
w = np.zeros(2)
for t in range(1, 100_001):
    w -= 0.5 * Xs.T @ (sigmoid(Xs @ w) - ys) / 4
    if t in (1_000, 10_000, 100_000):
        print(t, round(w[1], 3), f'{np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, -(2 * ys - 1) * (Xs @ w))):.2e}')
# 1000 5.548 1.95e-03 | 10000 7.828 1.99e-04 | 100000 10.127 2.00e-05

# 5. calibration: Gaussian classes with correlated features; naive Bayes double-counts the correlated evidence
d, rho = 8, 0.7
L = np.linalg.cholesky(rho * np.ones((d, d)) + (1 - rho) * np.eye(d))
yc = rng.integers(0, 2, 20_000)
Xc = rng.normal(size=(20_000, d)) @ L.T + np.where(yc[:, None] == 1, 0.3, -0.3)
tr, te = slice(0, 10_000), slice(10_000, None)
def ece(p, y, bins=10):
    k = np.minimum((p * bins).astype(int), bins - 1)
    return sum((k == b).mean() * abs(p[k == b].mean() - y[k == b].mean()) for b in range(bins) if (k == b).any())
models = {'naive Bayes': GaussianNB(), '+ Platt': CalibratedClassifierCV(GaussianNB(), method='sigmoid', cv=5),
          '+ isotonic': CalibratedClassifierCV(GaussianNB(), method='isotonic', cv=5), 'logistic regression': LogisticRegression()}
for name, m in models.items():
    p = m.fit(Xc[tr], yc[tr]).predict_proba(Xc[te])[:, 1]
    print(name, round(ece(p, yc[te]), 3), round(log_loss(yc[te], p), 3), round(roc_auc_score(yc[te], p), 3))
# ECE, log-loss, AUC: naive Bayes 0.236 1.133 0.695 | + Platt 0.008 0.64 0.695 | + isotonic 0.01 0.634 0.695
#                     logistic regression 0.012 0.633 0.695

# 6. isotonic regression (pool adjacent violators) on six labels sorted by score
print(IsotonicRegression().fit_transform(np.arange(6), [0, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1]).round(3))   # [0. 0.333 0.333 0.333 1. 1.]

# 7. two-class softmax = sigmoid of the score difference
z = np.array([1.3, -0.4])
print(softmax(z)[0].round(4), sigmoid(z[0] - z[1]).round(4))    # 0.8455 0.8455

# 8. ECE of perfectly calibrated predictions (y ~ Bernoulli(p)) on 10,000 examples, 200 repetitions, 10 and 50 bins
p0 = rng.random((200, 10_000)); y0 = (rng.random((200, 10_000)) < p0).astype(float)
print([round(float(np.mean([ece(p, y, b) for p, y in zip(p0, y0)])), 3) for b in (10, 50)])   # [0.01, 0.022]
```

## Next

Logistic regression returns a probability, and a probability is not yet a decision. Somewhere a threshold turns it into "flag" or "pass", and the right threshold depends on how rare the positives are and on what each kind of error costs. Judging the model needs a number too, and accuracy, the obvious one, says almost nothing when positives are rare: a model that never flags anything scores 99% when 1% are positive. The [next note](/notes/ml/classification-metrics) builds the vocabulary for these questions: the confusion matrix, precision and recall, the ROC and precision–recall curves, and thresholds chosen from costs.
