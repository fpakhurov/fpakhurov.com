Here are 100 points in ten dimensions. The eigenvalues of their sample covariance are $62.23$, $12.00$ and $4.45$, then $1.52$, $1.35$ and a slow decline to $0.39$. How many principal components do the data contain? [Probabilistic PCA](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#probabilistic-pca) answers in terms of likelihood, and the answer is unhelpful: the maximized likelihood grows with every component added.

A Bayesian model can do better. Put a prior on the loadings with one precision per column, as the [relevance vector machine](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine) did for weights, and let the evidence switch unneeded columns off. The evidence is now an integral over the loadings with no closed form, and the tool that handles it is the [EM algorithm](/notes/ml/em-algorithm) of the Machine Learning course ([Dempster, Laird and Rubin, 1977](#references)), restated as a bound that the rest of this part of the course builds on.

## Probabilistic PCA cannot choose its dimension

Probabilistic PCA explains each observation $x_i \in \mathbb R^d$, centred so that the data have mean zero, by a $k$-dimensional code $z_i$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_i &\sim \mathcal N(0, I_k), \\
x_i \mid z_i &\sim \mathcal N(W z_i, \sigma^2 I_d), \\
x_i &\sim \mathcal N(0,\; W W^\top + \sigma^2 I_d) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $W \in \mathbb R^{d \times k}$ is the loading matrix with columns $w_1, \dots, w_k$ and $\sigma^2$ the noise variance. The maximum likelihood solution is known in closed form. Let $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d$ be the eigenvalues of the sample covariance $S = \frac1n \sum_i x_i x_i^\top$. The columns of $W$ span the $k$ leading eigenvectors of $S$, the directions of [PCA](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition#pca-is-the-svd-of-the-centred-data). In the solution with orthogonal columns, column $j$ has length $\sqrt{\lambda_j - \sigma^2}$, and $\sigma^2$ is the average of the $d - k$ discarded eigenvalues.

<details><summary>Why</summary>

The log-likelihood depends on the data only through $S$: with $C = W W^\top + \sigma^2 I_d$, it is $-\frac n2\big(\log\det C + \operatorname{tr}(C^{-1} S)\big)$ plus a constant. Suppose $C$ has the eigenvectors of $S$, with eigenvalues $c_j$. The determinant is the product of the $c_j$ and the trace is $\sum_j \lambda_j / c_j$, so the log-likelihood splits into one term per direction, $-\frac n2 (\log c_j + \lambda_j/c_j)$, and its derivative in $c_j$, $-\frac n2 (1/c_j - \lambda_j/c_j^2)$, vanishes at $c_j = \lambda_j$. A rank-$k$ $W$ sets $k$ of them freely above $\sigma^2$, $c_j = \lVert w_j \rVert^2 + \sigma^2 = \lambda_j$, which gives the lengths $\sqrt{\lambda_j - \sigma^2}$; the other $d - k$ equal $\sigma^2$. Setting the derivative of $\sum_{j \gt k} (\log\sigma^2 + \lambda_j/\sigma^2)$ in $\sigma^2$ to zero gives their average. That the optimal $C$ has the eigenvectors of $S$, and keeps the largest eigenvalues, is proved in [Tipping and Bishop (1999)](#references), Appendices A.1–A.2.

</details>

What maximum likelihood cannot do is choose $k$. The models are nested: a model with $k + 1$ columns can set one of them to zero, so the maximized log-likelihood never decreases with $k$. The data of the opening come from a model with three latent dimensions and unit noise:

| $k$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| log-likelihood | $-2052.4$ | $-1885.9$ | $-1813.9$ | $-1806.6$ | $-1799.7$ | $-1793.2$ | $-1790.6$ | $-1786.4$ | $-1783.4$ |

The gains are $166.5$ and $72.0$ up to $k = 3$, then $7.3$, $6.9$ and less, but the likelihood keeps growing. With $k = 9$ every column is used. The lengths $\sqrt{\lambda_j - \sigma^2}$ are $7.86$, $3.41$ and $2.02$ for the signal and $1.07$ down to $0.49$ for six directions that carry only noise. The noise variance comes out as $\sigma^2 = 0.388$, well below the true value 1, because the extra columns have absorbed most of the noise. Choosing $k$ by BIC or by held-out likelihood means fitting every $k$ separately.

## EM as coordinate ascent on the evidence lower bound

Let $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ be the observations, $Z = \{z_1, \dots, z_n\}$ the latent variables and $\theta$ the parameters. For any distribution $q(Z)$ that is positive wherever the posterior is,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(X \mid \theta) &= \mathcal L(q, \theta) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X, \theta)\big), \\
\mathcal L(q, \theta) &= \mathbb E_q\big[\log p(X, Z \mid \theta)\big] - \mathbb E_q\big[\log q(Z)\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The [EM note](/notes/ml/em-algorithm#where-the-lower-bound-comes-from) derives the identity: write $\log p(X \mid \theta) = \log p(X, Z \mid \theta) - \log p(Z \mid X, \theta)$, add and subtract $\log q(Z)$, and average over $q$. The KL divergence is non-negative, so $\mathcal L$, the evidence lower bound (ELBO), is a lower bound on the log evidence, tight exactly when $q$ is the posterior. The Code section checks the identity for a randomly drawn $q$: both sides equal $-14.691242$.

EM is [coordinate ascent](/notes/ml/em-algorithm#em-is-coordinate-ascent-on-the-bound) on $\mathcal L$. The **E-step** maximizes over $q$ with $\theta$ fixed. Since $\log p(X \mid \theta)$ does not depend on $q$, this minimizes the KL divergence: $q$ becomes the posterior and the gap closes. The **M-step** maximizes over $\theta$ with $q$ fixed. The entropy of $q$ does not depend on $\theta$, so the M-step maximizes the expected complete-data log-likelihood $\mathbb E_q[\log p(X, Z \mid \theta)]$.

Why does adding unknowns help? The log-likelihood of a mixture of Gaussians is a sum over points of the logarithm of a sum over components. It does not split into separate fits, and it has many local maxima: relabelling the $K$ components alone turns one maximum into $K!$. If we knew which component produced each point, the fit would split into one Gaussian fit per component. Latent variables are chosen so that the complete-data distribution $p(X, Z \mid \theta)$ is in the [exponential family](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#the-exponential-family). Then $\log p(X, Z \mid \theta)$ is a linear function of the natural parameters minus the log-partition function, which is [convex because its Hessian is a covariance](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#derivatives-of-the-log-partition-function); so it is concave. Its average over $q$ is concave too, because a sum of concave functions with non-negative weights is concave. The M-step is a concave maximization, solved by [moment matching](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#derivatives-of-the-log-partition-function) with the expected sufficient statistics, in closed form or by a few steps of a convex solver.

A prior on the parameters gives **MAP-EM**. The log posterior of the parameters is $\log p(\theta \mid X) = \log p(X \mid \theta) + \log p(\theta) - \log p(X)$, and the last term does not depend on $\theta$. Adding $\log p(\theta)$ to both sides of the decomposition,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(\theta \mid X) + \log p(X) ={}& \mathcal L(q, \theta) + \log p(\theta) \\
&+ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X, \theta)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The E-step is unchanged. The M-step maximizes $\mathbb E_q[\log p(X, Z \mid \theta)] + \log p(\theta)$, and the same argument shows that the log posterior never decreases. A prior on the covariances of a Gaussian mixture, one of the defences against collapsing components, works this way: it turns the M-step into a regularized fit.

The roles can also be swapped. Make the weights of a linear model the latent variable and the prior precisions the parameters, and EM maximizes the evidence over the precisions. For the relevance vector machine this gives the update $\alpha_i = 1/(\mu_i^2 + \Sigma_{ii})$, which [the RVM note](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine#why-mackays-update-converges-faster-than-em) compares with MacKay's fixed point.

Neither step has to be solved exactly. A **generalized EM** only increases $\mathcal L$ in the M-step, for example by a few gradient steps or by maximizing over one group of parameters at a time, and each cycle still increases the likelihood. The E-step can be partial too: an incremental EM recomputes the posterior of one data point at a time and updates the sufficient statistics of the M-step accordingly ([Neal and Hinton, 1998](#references); [Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 9.4). What matters is that every step raises the bound.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> $\log p(X) = \mathcal L(q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, \text{posterior})$. EM's exact E-step closes the gap. A step that only raises $\mathcal L$ still climbs, but a restricted $q$ leaves a gap and biases the fixed point.</aside>

## EM for crowd labels

Training labels are often bought on crowdsourcing platforms: each image goes to a few annotators, and their answers disagree. A majority vote treats every annotator as equally reliable and every image as equally hard. [Whitehill et al. (2009)](#references) model both, in a model they call GLAD, for generative model of labels, abilities and difficulties. EM fits it, with the true labels as the latent variables and the abilities and difficulties as parameters.

Item $i = 1, \dots, n$ has an unknown true label $z_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and an **easiness** $b_i > 0$; its difficulty is $1/b_i$. Annotator $j = 1, \dots, m$ has a **skill** $a_j \in \mathbb R$. The label $l_{ij}$ that annotator $j$ gives item $i$ is correct with probability

$$
p(l_{ij} = z_i \mid z_i, a_j, b_i) = \sigma(a_j b_i) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-a_j b_i}} .
$$

The paper writes $\alpha$ and $\beta$; in this course those letters are precisions. A skill of 0 is a coin flip, and a negative skill belongs to an annotator who tends to give the wrong label, by malice or by misunderstanding. An item with $b_i \to 0$ is a coin flip for everyone. The labels are independent given the true label, and an annotator need not label every item: write $J_i$ for those who labelled item $i$.

The E-step is a weighted vote. By Bayes' formula with the prior $p(z_i = 1) = 1/2$, the posterior log-odds of the true label is the sum of the log-likelihood ratios of its labels, since they are independent given $z_i$. A label $l_{ij} = 1$ is correct under $z_i = 1$ and wrong under $z_i = 0$, so it contributes $\log \sigma(a_j b_i) - \log \sigma(-a_j b_i)$; a label $l_{ij} = 0$ contributes the negative. Finally $\sigma(x)/\sigma(-x) = (1 + e^{x})/(1 + e^{-x}) = e^x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log \frac{p(z_i = 1 \mid l, a, b)}{p(z_i = 0 \mid l, a, b)}
&= \sum_{j \in J_i} (2 l_{ij} - 1) \log \frac{\sigma(a_j b_i)}{\sigma(-a_j b_i)} \\
&= b_i \sum_{j \in J_i} a_j\, (2 l_{ij} - 1) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Each label votes $+1$ or $-1$ with the weight of its annotator's skill. With equal skills this is the majority vote, and an annotator with a negative skill votes for the opposite of what they said. The easiness multiplies the whole sum: it changes how confident the posterior is, never which side wins.

For the M-step, let $c_{ij}$ be the posterior probability that label $l_{ij}$ is correct, $q_i(z_i = l_{ij})$. The complete-data log-likelihood is a sum over labels of $\log \sigma(a_j b_i)$ for a correct label and $\log \sigma(-a_j b_i)$ for a wrong one. Its expectation under $q$ replaces the indicator of a correct label by $c_{ij}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(a, b) = \sum_{i} \sum_{j \in J_i} \Big[&c_{ij} \log \sigma(a_j b_i) \\
&+ (1 - c_{ij}) \log \sigma(-a_j b_i)\Big] + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\log \sigma(x) = -\log(1 + e^{-x})$, its derivative is $e^{-x}/(1 + e^{-x}) = \sigma(-x)$, and that of $\log\sigma(-x)$ is $-\sigma(x)$. With $\sigma(x) + \sigma(-x) = 1$, each term has the derivative $c_{ij}\,\sigma(-x) - (1 - c_{ij})\,\sigma(x) = c_{ij} - \sigma(x)$ with respect to $x = a_j b_i$, and the chain rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial a_j} &= \sum_{i:\, j \in J_i} b_i \big(c_{ij} - \sigma(a_j b_i)\big), \\
\frac{\partial Q}{\partial b_i} &= \sum_{j \in J_i} a_j \big(c_{ij} - \sigma(a_j b_i)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the gradient of a [logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression): observed minus predicted probability of a correct label. There is no closed form, so the M-step takes a few gradient steps, a generalized EM. Whitehill et al. add the priors $a_j \sim \mathcal N(1, 1)$ and $\log b_i \sim \mathcal N(1, 1)$, which keeps $b_i$ positive and turns the method into MAP-EM.

**Worked example.** Take 1000 items, each labelled by 3 of 7 annotators chosen at random, with skills $(3, 2, 1.5, 1, 0.5, 0, -1)$: experts, average annotators, a guesser and an adversary. The easiness is $e^{\mathcal N(1, 1)}$, as in the paper's simulations. EM starts from equal skills, so its first E-step is the majority vote:

| | majority vote | GLAD, 24 EM iterations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| wrong labels of 1000 | 133 | 61 |
| skills | equal | $(1.45, 1.40, 1.43, 1.13, 0.83, 0.09, -1.03)$ |

GLAD fixes 91 items on which the majority was wrong and breaks 19 on which it was right. It finds the guesser and the adversary, whose votes now count reversed, but with three labels per item it does not separate the three best annotators.

Two symmetries show what the priors do. Changing the sign of every skill and flipping every true label leaves the likelihood unchanged: every correct label becomes wrong, and $\sigma(a_j b_i)$ and $\sigma(-a_j b_i)$ swap places, so each factor stays the same. Doubling the skills while halving the easiness leaves every product $a_j b_i$, and so the likelihood, unchanged. Only the prior mean of 1 on $a_j$ says that annotators are mostly helpful and fixes the scale.

A model with skills alone, every $b_i = 1$, also makes 61 errors on these data. Given the skills, the easiness never changes the winner of a vote; it acts through the M-step, where the gradient in $a_j$ weights item $i$ by $b_i$, so hard items count less. Here the two fits find nearly proportional skills, $(3.14, 3.06, 3.08, 2.20, 1.52, 0.11, -2.01)$ without the easiness, and the votes come out alike. Whitehill et al. found that this pays when half the items are hard: 4.5% errors against 8.4% for an EM model of skills alone and 11.2% for the majority vote.

## Variational EM: when the E-step cannot be exact

The exact E-step needs the posterior $p(Z \mid X, \theta)$. For a mixture it is a table of responsibilities. For most models with many interacting latent variables it is a distribution over exponentially many configurations, or a continuous density with no closed form. **Variational EM** ([Neal and Hinton, 1998](#references)) keeps the bound and restricts the E-step to a family $\mathcal Q$ of distributions it can handle:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q^{t+1} &= \arg\max_{q \in \mathcal Q}\, \mathcal L(q, \theta^t) \\
&= \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal Q}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X, \theta^t)\big), \\
\theta^{t+1} &= \arg\max_\theta\, \mathcal L(q^{t+1}, \theta) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The two forms of the E-step are the same problem, because $\log p(X \mid \theta^t)$ does not depend on $q$. Each step still increases $\mathcal L$, so the bound never decreases. What is lost is the tightness after the E-step: the gap is the smallest KL divergence within $\mathcal Q$, which is positive unless $\mathcal Q$ contains the posterior. Write $\ell(\theta) = \log p(X \mid \theta)$. The monotonicity chain of exact EM is

$$
\ell(\theta^{t+1}) \ge \mathcal L(q^{t+1}, \theta^{t+1}) \ge \mathcal L(q^{t+1}, \theta^t) = \ell(\theta^t) ,
$$

and with a restricted $q$ its last link becomes $\le$. The log-likelihood can go down, and the fixed point maximizes the bound rather than the likelihood. The bound is the likelihood minus a gap that depends on $\theta$, so its maximizer is pulled towards parameters at which the posterior is easy to approximate by a member of $\mathcal Q$, a bias that can be large ([Turner and Sahani, 2011](#references)).

**Worked example: hard EM is variational EM.** Take $\mathcal Q$ to be the point masses, $q_i(z_i) = [z_i = \hat z_i]$. The entropy of a point mass is zero, so $\mathcal L = \sum_i \log p(x_i, \hat z_i \mid \theta)$, maximized by giving each point its most probable component. The gap for one point is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_i \,\|\, p(z_i \mid x_i, \theta)\big) \\
&\quad= \sum_{z} [z = \hat z_i] \log \frac{[z = \hat z_i]}{p(z \mid x_i, \theta)} \\
&\quad= -\log p(\hat z_i \mid x_i, \theta) .
\end{aligned}
$$

This E-step is the assignment step of k-means and of the [hard EM](/notes/ml/em-algorithm#k-means-is-the-hard-equal-variance-limit) of the EM note, which therefore minimizes the same KL divergence over the narrowest family, point masses on the assignments ([Vetrov and Kropotov](#references), lecture 7). Take that note's toy model, the mixture $\tfrac12 \mathcal N(0, 1) + \tfrac12 \mathcal N(\theta, 1)$ on seven points, and start at $\theta_0 = 0.6$. The first three points go to the first component and the last four to the second:

| $x_i$ | $-0.8$ | $-0.2$ | $0.3$ | $0.9$ | $2.1$ | $2.8$ | $3.4$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $p(\hat z_i \mid x_i, \theta_0)$ | $0.659$ | $0.574$ | $0.500$ | $0.589$ | $0.746$ | $0.818$ | $0.865$ |
| gap $-\log p(\hat z_i \mid x_i, \theta_0)$ | $0.417$ | $0.554$ | $0.693$ | $0.529$ | $0.292$ | $0.201$ | $0.145$ |

The gaps add up to $2.83$. The M-step moves $\theta$ to the mean of the four points, $2.300$, where the log-likelihood is $-12.06$ and the next E-step leaves a gap of $0.77$. Hard EM converges to $\theta = 2.767$ with $\ell = -12.05$ and a gap of $0.45$. Exact EM converges to the maximum likelihood estimate $\theta = 2.540$ with $\ell = -12.00$ and no gap. Hard EM even climbs faster at first, $-12.06$ against $-12.83$ after one M-step, and still stops at the wrong place. It maximizes its own bound, which at $\theta = 2.767$ is $-12.50$, higher than its value $-12.57$ at the maximum likelihood estimate.

Point masses are the crudest family. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference) uses factorized distributions, $q(Z) = \prod_j q_j(Z_j)$, for which the E-step has a closed-form coordinate update. It also lets $q$ cover the parameters, so that the M-step disappears and the whole posterior is approximated ([Beal, 2003](#references)).

## Bayesian PCA: a precision for every column

Bayesian PCA ([Bishop, 1999a](#references)) keeps probabilistic PCA and adds a prior on the loadings with one precision $\alpha_j$ per column:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(W \mid \alpha) &= \prod_{j=1}^{k} \mathcal N\big(w_j \mid 0,\ \alpha_j^{-1} I_d\big), \\
k &= d - 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is automatic relevance determination applied to latent dimensions instead of input features. A large $\alpha_j$ forces column $w_j$ towards zero, and a zero column removes latent dimension $j$ from the model, since $W z$ no longer depends on $z_j$. $k = d - 1$ is as many columns as can be useful: with all of them kept, $W W^\top + \sigma^2 I$ can already represent any covariance matrix. Take $\sigma^2$ equal to its smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_d$, and as columns the other $d - 1$ eigenvectors $v_j$ scaled to $\sqrt{\lambda_j - \lambda_d}\, v_j$ ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 12.2.3).

The ideal would be to choose $\alpha$ and $\sigma^2$ by maximizing the evidence $p(X \mid \alpha, \sigma^2) = \int p(X \mid W, \sigma^2)\, p(W \mid \alpha)\, dW$. But $W$ enters the covariance of $x$ quadratically, and the integral has no closed form. Bishop's scheme, also the one of [Vetrov and Kropotov](#references), approximates it in the spirit of the [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) and alternates two steps:

1. with $\alpha$ fixed, find the MAP estimate of $W$ and $\sigma^2$ by MAP-EM, with the codes $z_i$ as latent variables;
2. with $W$ fixed, set $\alpha_j = d / \lVert w_j \rVert^2$.

The second update is the evidence fixed point of the relevance vector machine, $\alpha_j = \gamma_j / \lVert w_j \rVert^2$, applied to a whole column of $d$ entries, each of them counted as well determined by the data, so that $\gamma_j = d$. It is also the value of $\alpha_j$ that maximizes $\log p(w_j \mid \alpha_j) = \frac d2 \log \alpha_j - \frac{\alpha_j}{2} \lVert w_j \rVert^2$ at the current $w_j$.

The E-step is exact, because $z_i$ and $x_i$ are jointly Gaussian. Keep the terms of $\log p(x_i, z_i)$ that contain $z_i$ and [complete the square](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#3-completing-the-square):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(z_i \mid x_i)
&= -\tfrac12 z_i^\top z_i - \frac{\lVert x_i - W z_i \rVert^2}{2\sigma^2} + \text{const} \\
&= -\tfrac12 z_i^\top \frac{M}{\sigma^2} z_i + z_i^\top \frac{W^\top x_i}{\sigma^2} + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The precision is $M/\sigma^2$, the mean is the precision's inverse times the linear term, and $\mathbb E[z z^\top]$ is the covariance plus the outer product of the mean:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= W^\top W + \sigma^2 I_k, \\
\mathbb E[z_i] &= M^{-1} W^\top x_i, \\
\mathbb E[z_i z_i^\top] &= \sigma^2 M^{-1} + \mathbb E[z_i]\, \mathbb E[z_i]^\top .
\end{aligned}
$$

The M-step maximizes $\sum_i \mathbb E\big[\log p(x_i \mid z_i, W, \sigma^2)\big] + \log p(W \mid \alpha)$. The terms with $W$ are $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_i \mathbb E\lVert x_i - W z_i \rVert^2 - \frac12 \sum_j \alpha_j \lVert w_j \rVert^2$. Write $A = \operatorname{diag}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_k)$ and collect the two sums of E-step statistics,

$$
\begin{aligned}
G &= \sum_i x_i\, \mathbb E[z_i]^\top, \\
R &= \sum_i \mathbb E[z_i z_i^\top] .
\end{aligned}
$$

Expand $\mathbb E\lVert x - W z \rVert^2 = \lVert x \rVert^2 - 2\, \mathbb E[z]^\top W^\top x + \operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbb E[z z^\top] W^\top W\big)$. By the [trace rules](/notes/math/matrix-calculus#traces-of-matrix-products), the gradient in $W$ of $\mathbb E[z]^\top W^\top x$ is $x\, \mathbb E[z]^\top$ and that of $\operatorname{tr}(R\, W^\top W)$, for a symmetric $R$, is $2 W R$; the prior term gives $-W A$. Summed over the points, the gradient and its zero are

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &= \frac{1}{\sigma^2} \big(G - W R\big) - W A \\
\Longrightarrow\quad G &= W \big(R + \sigma^2 A\big) \\
\Longrightarrow\quad W &= G \big(R + \sigma^2 A\big)^{-1} .
\end{aligned}
$$

With $A = 0$ this is the EM algorithm for probabilistic PCA ([Tipping and Bishop, 1999](#references)); the prior adds $\sigma^2 A$, a ridge penalty of its own size for every column. The prior does not involve the noise variance. Setting the derivative of $-\frac{nd}{2} \log \sigma^2 - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_i \mathbb E\lVert x_i - W z_i \rVert^2$ to zero, with the new $W$, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{nd} \sum_{i=1}^n \Big(&\lVert x_i \rVert^2 - 2\, \mathbb E[z_i]^\top W^\top x_i \\
&+ \operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbb E[z_i z_i^\top]\, W^\top W\big)\Big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

One iteration costs $O(ndk + k^3)$ and never forms the $d \times d$ covariance, which makes EM cheaper than an eigendecomposition when $d$ is large and $k$ small. EM for probabilistic PCA also handles values missing at random: the E-step then averages over the missing coordinates as well as over the codes ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 12.2.2).

### Why a column switches off

Take a fixed point at which the kept columns are orthogonal and lie along eigenvectors of $S$. EM reaches such a point here: in the Code section the largest principal angle between the kept columns and the three leading eigenvectors is below $0.01°$. Then $C = W W^\top + \sigma^2 I$ has the eigenvectors of $S$. Along the eigenvector of column $j$, with eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $S$ and $u = \lVert w_j \rVert^2$, $C$ has the eigenvalue $c = u + \sigma^2$, and as in the maximum likelihood solution above the log-likelihood splits into one term per direction. The MAP objective for $u$ and its stationary point are

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(u) &= -\frac n2 \Big(\log c + \frac{\lambda}{c}\Big) - \frac{\alpha_j}{2}\, u, \\
f'(u) = 0 \iff \alpha_j &= \frac{n(\lambda - c)}{c^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituting the precision update $\alpha_j = d/u$ and $c = u + \sigma^2$, then expanding both sides and collecting powers of $u$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
n(\lambda - \sigma^2 - u)\, u &= d\,(u + \sigma^2)^2 \\
(n + d)\, u^2 - \big[n(\lambda - \sigma^2) - 2d\sigma^2\big]\, u + d\sigma^4 &= 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The product of the roots, $d\sigma^4/(n + d)$, is positive, so both roots have the same sign, that of their sum, the bracket divided by $n + d$. The roots are real when the discriminant is non-negative. So a positive root exists only if the bracket is positive and its square is at least $4(n + d)\, d\sigma^4$, that is $n(\lambda - \sigma^2) - 2d\sigma^2 \ge 2\sigma^2\sqrt{d(n + d)}$, or

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\lambda}{\sigma^2} \;\ge\; 1 + \frac{2\big(d + \sqrt{d(n + d)}\big)}{n}\;}
$$

Below the threshold the only fixed point is $w_j = 0$ with $\alpha_j = \infty$: the column is switched off. Maximum likelihood keeps a direction whenever $\lambda > \sigma^2$. The prior asks for extra evidence that shrinks as $n$ grows, about $2\sqrt{d/n}$ in relative terms when $n \gg d$. Above the threshold the larger root is the squared length $u = \lVert w_j \rVert^2$ that EM reaches here. It is smaller than the maximum likelihood value $\lambda - \sigma^2$, because it is below the sum of the roots, $\big[n(\lambda - \sigma^2) - 2d\sigma^2\big]/(n + d)$.

**Worked example.** Return to the data of the opening, with $n = 100$ and $d = 10$. Bayesian PCA with $k = 9$ columns removes six of them, at EM iterations $55$, $67$, $76$, $124$, $217$ and $218$ (a column is removed when $\alpha_j$ exceeds $10^8$), and ends with $\sigma^2 = 0.977$, close to the true noise variance. The threshold and the third column are

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| threshold on $\lambda/\sigma^2$ | $1 + 2(10 + \sqrt{1100})/100 = 1.863$ |
| threshold on $\lambda$ | $1.863 \cdot 0.977 = 1.82$ |
| quadratic for $\lambda_3 = 4.45$ | $110\, u^2 - 327.8\, u + 9.545 = 0$ |
| larger root, $\lVert w_3 \rVert$ | $u = 2.950$, $\lVert w_3 \rVert = 1.717$ |
| maximum likelihood, $\sqrt{\lambda_3 - \sigma^2}$ | $1.86$ |

The third eigenvalue, $4.45$, is above the threshold and the fourth, $1.52$, below it, so exactly three columns survive. The length of the third is the one EM finds. The other two kept columns have lengths $7.450$ and $3.136$, again the larger roots of their quadratics, and $\alpha = (0.180, 1.017, 3.391)$.

## Where it breaks

The figure also shows the first failure: EM for Bayesian PCA is cheap per iteration and slow to converge. Pruning is fast, but the three surviving columns then turn slowly towards the eigenvectors, and only after a few thousand iterations do their lengths settle at the roots of the quadratic. When several columns sit near the threshold, the result after a fixed number of iterations depends on when one stops, and the starting point can decide which columns survive.

The second is that the evidence is only approximated. The update $\alpha_j = d/\lVert w_j \rVert^2$ treats every entry of a column as well determined and uses a point estimate of $W$, so the threshold above is a property of this scheme. A variational treatment of the same model, with a factorized posterior over $W$, $z$ and $\alpha$, maximizes a true lower bound on the evidence instead ([Bishop, 1999b](#references)).

The third is the bias of variational EM, worked out in the hard EM example. Its fixed points maximize a bound whose gap depends on the parameters, so the estimates move towards parameters with easy posteriors, $2.767$ instead of $2.540$ there. Comparing models by their bounds is worse still, because each bound carries its own unknown gap. Finally, the model is linear and Gaussian, like probabilistic PCA: it sees only second moments, and curved data need mixtures of probabilistic PCA or nonlinear models such as the VAE.

## What you should remember

- $\log p(X \mid \theta) = \mathcal L(q, \theta) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X, \theta))$ for every $q$; EM is coordinate ascent on $\mathcal L$.
- A prior adds $\log p(\theta)$ to the M-step (MAP-EM); partial steps are fine if they raise $\mathcal L$.
- Crowd labels (GLAD): the E-step is a vote weighted by skill, the M-step a logistic-regression gradient; priors fix sign and scale.
- Variational EM restricts $q$; it raises the bound, the likelihood can fall, and its fixed point is biased. Hard EM is the point-mass case.
- Bayesian PCA: one precision per column, M-step $W = G(R + \sigma^2 A)^{-1}$, then $\alpha_j = d/\lVert w_j \rVert^2$.
- A direction survives only if $\lambda/\sigma^2 \ge 1 + 2(d + \sqrt{d(n + d)})/n$.

## Code

Exact and hard EM on the seven-point mixture, the decomposition $\log p = \mathcal L + D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ for an arbitrary $q$, maximum likelihood probabilistic PCA for every $k$, and Bayesian PCA by EM with the fixed-point check of the text. NumPy and SciPy only; seeds are fixed, and exact numbers can differ slightly across platforms and library versions. The script runs in about a second.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm
from scipy.linalg import subspace_angles

# the seven-point mixture 0.5 N(0, 1) + 0.5 N(theta, 1): exact EM and variational EM with point masses (hard EM)
x = np.array([-0.8, -0.2, 0.3, 0.9, 2.1, 2.8, 3.4])
loglik = lambda th: float(np.log(0.5 * norm.pdf(x) + 0.5 * norm.pdf(x - th)).sum())
def bound(th, q):                        # L(q, theta), q = probability of the second component for each point
    with np.errstate(divide='ignore', invalid='ignore'):
        a = np.where(q < 1, (1 - q) * np.log(0.5 * norm.pdf(x) / (1 - q)), 0.0)
        b = np.where(q > 0, q * np.log(0.5 * norm.pdf(x - th) / q), 0.0)
    return float((a + b).sum())
exact = lambda th: norm.pdf(x - th) / (norm.pdf(x) + norm.pdf(x - th))   # E-step: the posterior
hard = lambda th: (np.abs(x - th) < np.abs(x)).astype(float)             # E-step restricted to point masses
r0, h0 = exact(0.6), hard(0.6)
print(np.where(h0 == 1, r0, 1 - r0).round(3), round(loglik(0.6) - bound(0.6, h0), 2))
# [0.659 0.574 0.5   0.589 0.746 0.818 0.865] 2.83: the probability of the chosen component, and the gap
for name, estep in (('exact', exact), ('hard', hard)):
    th, out = 0.6, []
    for it in range(200):
        q = estep(th)
        if it < 2: out.append((round(float(th), 3), round(loglik(th), 2), round(bound(th, q), 2)))
        th = (q * x).sum() / q.sum()                                     # M-step: q-weighted mean
    q = estep(th)
    print(name, out, round(float(th), 3), round(loglik(th), 2), round(bound(th, q), 2))
# exact [(0.6, -16.35, -16.35), (1.662, -12.83, -12.83)] 2.54 -12.0 -12.0
# hard [(0.6, -16.35, -19.18), (2.3, -12.06, -12.82)] 2.767 -12.05 -12.5
print(round(bound(2.540, hard(2.540)), 2))                              # -12.57: the hard bound at the maximum likelihood estimate

# log p = L + KL holds for any q, not only for the posterior
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
q = rng.uniform(size=7); r = exact(1.0)
kl = (q * np.log(q / r) + (1 - q) * np.log((1 - q) / (1 - r))).sum()
print(round(loglik(1.0), 6), round(bound(1.0, q) + kl, 6))           # -14.691242 -14.691242

# Bayesian PCA: 100 points in d = 10 dimensions from a model with three latent dimensions and unit noise
d, k, n = 10, 3, 100
W_true = rng.normal(size=(d, k)) * np.array([3.0, 2.0, 1.0])
X = rng.normal(size=(n, k)) @ W_true.T + rng.normal(size=(n, d))
X = X - X.mean(axis=0)
lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(X.T @ X / n); lam, V = lam[::-1], V[:, ::-1]
print(lam.round(2))
# [62.23 12.    4.45  1.52  1.35  1.18  0.89  0.84  0.63  0.39]

# maximum likelihood probabilistic PCA: the log-likelihood only grows with the number of columns
def ppca_loglik(q):
    s2 = lam[q:].mean(); c = np.concatenate([lam[:q], np.full(d - q, s2)])
    return -n / 2 * (d * np.log(2 * np.pi) + np.log(c).sum() + (lam / c).sum())
print([round(float(ppca_loglik(q)), 1) for q in range(1, 10)])
# [-2052.4, -1885.9, -1813.9, -1806.6, -1799.7, -1793.2, -1790.6, -1786.4, -1783.4]
s2 = lam[9:].mean(); print(round(float(s2), 3), np.sqrt(lam[:9] - s2).round(2))   # nine columns: every one is used
# 0.388 [7.86 3.41 2.02 1.07 0.98 0.89 0.71 0.67 0.49]

# EM for the MAP loadings with one ARD precision per column, alpha_j = d / ||w_j||^2; columns with alpha_j > 1e8 are removed
q = d - 1
W, s2, alpha = rng.normal(size=(d, q)), 1.0, np.ones(q)
active = np.arange(q); pruned = []
for it in range(1000):
    Wa = W[:, active]; m = len(active)
    Minv = np.linalg.inv(Wa.T @ Wa + s2 * np.eye(m))
    Ez = X @ Wa @ Minv                                   # E[z_i], n x m
    R = n * s2 * Minv + Ez.T @ Ez                        # sum_i E[z_i z_i^T]
    Wa = (X.T @ Ez) @ np.linalg.inv(R + s2 * np.diag(alpha[active]))   # W = G (R + s2 A)^-1
    s2 = (np.sum(X ** 2) - 2 * np.sum(Ez * (X @ Wa)) + np.trace(R @ Wa.T @ Wa)) / (n * d)
    W[:] = 0; W[:, active] = Wa
    alpha[active] = d / (Wa ** 2).sum(axis=0)
    for j in active[alpha[active] > 1e8]: pruned.append((j, it + 1))
    active = active[alpha[active] <= 1e8]
print(len(active), [it for _, it in pruned], round(float(s2), 3))
# 3 [55, 67, 76, 124, 217, 218] 0.977
print(np.sort(np.linalg.norm(W[:, active], axis=0))[::-1].round(3), np.sort(alpha[active]).round(3))
# [7.45  3.136 1.717] [0.18  1.017 3.391]
print(np.degrees(subspace_angles(W[:, active], V[:, :3])).max() < 0.01)   # True: the kept columns span the leading eigenvectors

# the fixed point of one column along an eigenvector with eigenvalue l: (n + d) u^2 - [n (l - s2) - 2 d s2] u + d s2^2 = 0, u = ||w||^2
B = n * (lam[:4] - s2) - 2 * d * s2; disc = B ** 2 - 4 * (n + d) * d * s2 ** 2
print(np.where(disc > 0, np.sqrt((B + np.sqrt(np.maximum(disc, 0))) / (2 * (n + d))), 0).round(3), round(float(1 + 2 * (d + np.sqrt(d * (n + d))) / n), 3))
# [7.45  3.136 1.717 0.   ] 1.863
```

EM for crowd labels: the simulation of the worked example, GLAD against the majority vote and against a model of skills alone, and the two symmetries of the likelihood. It runs in under a second.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.special import expit, log_expit

# EM for crowd labels (GLAD): annotator j labels item i correctly with probability sigmoid(a_j b_i)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, m = 1000, 7                                       # 1000 items, 7 annotators, 3 labels per item
a_true = np.array([3.0, 2.0, 1.5, 1.0, 0.5, 0.0, -1.0])   # skills: experts, average, a guesser, an adversary
b_true = np.exp(rng.normal(1, 1, n))                 # easiness b_i > 0; the difficulty is 1/b_i
z_true = rng.integers(0, 2, n)
item = np.repeat(np.arange(n), 3)
ann = np.concatenate([rng.choice(m, 3, replace=False) for _ in range(n)])
ok = rng.uniform(size=3 * n) < expit(a_true[ann] * b_true[item])
lab = np.where(ok, z_true[item], 1 - z_true[item])
vote = 2 * lab - 1                                   # +1 for class 1, -1 for class 0

def e_step(a, b):                                    # log-odds of z_i = 1 is the weighted vote sum_j a_j b_i (2 l_ij - 1)
    return expit(np.bincount(item, a[ann] * b[item] * vote, minlength=n))

def m_step(q, a, s, fit_b):
    """A few L-BFGS steps on Q(a, b) + log prior, a_j ~ N(1, 1), s_i = log b_i ~ N(1, 1)."""
    c = np.where(lab == 1, q[item], 1 - q[item])     # posterior probability that each label is correct
    def neg(p):
        aj, si = p[:m], (p[m:] if fit_b else s)
        b = np.exp(si); x = aj[ann] * b[item]
        f = (c * log_expit(x) + (1 - c) * log_expit(-x)).sum() - ((aj - 1) ** 2).sum() / 2
        r = c - expit(x)                             # observed minus predicted probability of being correct
        g = [np.bincount(ann, r * b[item], minlength=m) - (aj - 1)]
        if fit_b:
            f -= ((si - 1) ** 2).sum() / 2
            g.append(np.bincount(item, r * aj[ann], minlength=n) * b - (si - 1))
        return -f, -np.concatenate(g)
    p = minimize(neg, np.concatenate([a, s]) if fit_b else a, jac=True, method='L-BFGS-B', options=dict(maxiter=25)).x
    return p[:m], (p[m:] if fit_b else s)

def em(fit_b):
    a, s = np.ones(m), np.zeros(n)                   # equal skills: the first E-step is a majority vote
    q = e_step(a, np.exp(s))
    for it in range(1, 500):
        a, s = m_step(q, a, s, fit_b)
        q, q_old = e_step(a, np.exp(s)), q
        if np.abs(q - q_old).max() < 1e-5: return q, a, s, it

majority = np.bincount(item, vote, minlength=n) > 0
q, a, s, it = em(fit_b=True)
glad = q > 0.5
print((majority != z_true).sum(), (glad != z_true).sum(), it)       # 133 61 24: errors of majority vote and of GLAD
print(((majority != z_true) & (glad == z_true)).sum(), ((majority == z_true) & (glad != z_true)).sum())   # 91 19
print(a.round(2))                                    # [ 1.45  1.4   1.43  1.13  0.83  0.09 -1.03]
print((np.bincount(ann, lab == z_true[item]) / np.bincount(ann)).round(2))   # [0.98 0.94 0.94 0.87 0.79 0.53 0.11]: each annotator's true accuracy
print(round(np.corrcoef(s, np.log(b_true))[0, 1], 2))                # 0.43: easiness is only roughly recovered
q1, a1, *_ = em(fit_b=False)                        # skills only, every b_i = 1
print(((q1 > 0.5) != z_true).sum(), a1.round(2))    # 61 [ 3.14  3.06  3.08  2.2   1.52  0.11 -2.01]

# the likelihood cannot tell (a, z) from (-a, 1 - z), nor (a, b) from (2a, b/2): only the priors fix sign and scale
def loglik(a, b, z):
    x = a[ann] * b[item]
    return np.where(lab == z[item], log_expit(x), log_expit(-x)).sum()
print(np.isclose(loglik(a_true, b_true, z_true), loglik(-a_true, b_true, 1 - z_true)),
      np.isclose(loglik(a_true, b_true, z_true), loglik(2 * a_true, b_true / 2, z_true)))   # True True
```
