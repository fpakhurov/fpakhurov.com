The [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) replaced a posterior that has no formula by a Gaussian at its mode. The models of this section have a second kind of unknown: besides the parameters shared by all observations, every observation has its own **latent variable**, a cluster label or a low-dimensional code. The [EM algorithm](/notes/ml/em-algorithm) of the Machine Learning course ([Dempster, Laird and Rubin, 1977](#references)) fits such models when the posterior over the latent variables can be computed exactly. This note restates EM in the form the rest of the section builds on, a lower bound on the log evidence climbed one block at a time, and then changes what the blocks are. With a prior on the parameters, EM returns a MAP estimate or tunes hyperparameters. When the posterior over the latent variables is out of reach, restricting the E-step to a tractable family gives **variational EM**. And with a precision for every column of the loading matrix, as in the [relevance vector machine](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine), probabilistic PCA chooses for itself how many principal components it needs: **Bayesian PCA**.

**Notation.** $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ are the observations in $\mathbb R^d$, centred so that their mean is zero, and $Z = \{z_1, \dots, z_n\}$ the latent variables. $\theta$ collects the parameters, $q(Z)$ is any distribution over the latent variables, $\mathcal L(q, \theta)$ the evidence lower bound (ELBO) and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ the Kullback–Leibler divergence. For PCA, $k$ is the number of latent dimensions, $W \in \mathbb R^{d \times k}$ the loading matrix with columns $w_1, \dots, w_k$, $\sigma^2$ the noise variance, $\alpha_j$ the prior precision of column $w_j$ and $A = \operatorname{diag}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_k)$. $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d$ are the eigenvalues of the sample covariance $S = \frac1n \sum_i x_i x_i^\top$.

## Baseline: probabilistic PCA with a fixed number of components

[Probabilistic PCA](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#probabilistic-pca) explains each observation by a $k$-dimensional code,

$$
z_i \sim \mathcal N(0, I_k), \qquad x_i \mid z_i \sim \mathcal N(W z_i, \sigma^2 I_d), \qquad x_i \sim \mathcal N(0,\; W W^\top + \sigma^2 I_d) .
$$

Its maximum likelihood solution is known in closed form: the columns of $W$ span the $k$ leading eigenvectors of $S$; in the solution with orthogonal columns, column $j$ has length $\sqrt{\lambda_j - \sigma^2}$, and $\sigma^2$ is the average of the $d - k$ discarded eigenvalues. What maximum likelihood cannot do is choose $k$. The models are nested: a model with $k + 1$ columns can set one of them to zero, so the maximized log-likelihood never decreases with $k$.

**Worked example.** In the Code section, 100 points in $d = 10$ dimensions are drawn from a model with three latent dimensions and unit noise. The eigenvalues of $S$ are $62.23, 12.00, 4.45$, then $1.52, 1.35, \dots, 0.39$. The maximized log-likelihood for $k = 1, \dots, 9$ is

$$
-2052.4,\ -1885.9,\ -1813.9,\ -1806.6,\ -1799.7,\ -1793.2,\ -1790.6,\ -1786.4,\ -1783.4 .
$$

It grows by $166.5$ and $72.0$ up to $k = 3$ and then by $7.3$, $6.9$ and less, but it keeps growing. With $k = 9$ every column is used: the column lengths $\sqrt{\lambda_j - \sigma^2}$ are $7.86, 3.41, 2.02$ for the signal and $1.07, \dots, 0.49$ for six directions that carry only noise, and the noise variance comes out as $\sigma^2 = 0.388$, well below the true value 1, because the extra columns have absorbed most of the noise. Choosing $k$ by BIC or held-out likelihood means fitting every $k$ separately. The Bayesian answer, after a short detour through EM, is to fit one model with the largest $k$ and let a prior remove the columns it does not need.

## EM as coordinate ascent on the evidence lower bound

For any distribution $q(Z)$, positive wherever the posterior is,

$$
\log p(X \mid \theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p(X, Z \mid \theta)\big] - \mathbb E_q\big[\log q(Z)\big]}_{\mathcal L(q, \theta)} \;+\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(Z) \,\big\|\, p(Z \mid X, \theta)\big) .
$$

The identity is derived in the [EM note](/notes/ml/em-algorithm#where-the-lower-bound-comes-from): write $\log p(X \mid \theta) = \log p(X, Z \mid \theta) - \log p(Z \mid X, \theta)$, add and subtract $\log q(Z)$, and average over $q$. Because the KL divergence is non-negative, $\mathcal L$ is a lower bound on the log evidence, tight exactly when $q$ is the posterior. EM is [coordinate ascent](/notes/ml/em-algorithm#em-is-coordinate-ascent-on-the-bound) on $\mathcal L$: the **E-step** maximizes over $q$ with $\theta$ fixed, which sets $q$ to the posterior and closes the gap; the **M-step** maximizes over $\theta$ with $q$ fixed, which maximizes the expected complete-data log-likelihood $\mathbb E_q[\log p(X, Z \mid \theta)]$. The Code section checks the identity for a $q$ drawn at random, not a posterior: both sides equal $-14.691242$.

The decomposition is what this section keeps; the three uses below change what the blocks are.

**A prior on the parameters: MAP-EM.** Add $\log p(\theta)$ to both sides. The posterior of the parameters satisfies $\log p(\theta \mid X) = \log p(X \mid \theta) + \log p(\theta) - \log p(X)$, and the last term does not depend on $\theta$, so

$$
\log p(\theta \mid X) + \log p(X) = \mathcal L(q, \theta) + \log p(\theta) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X, \theta)\big) .
$$

The E-step is unchanged; the M-step maximizes $\mathbb E_q[\log p(X, Z \mid \theta)] + \log p(\theta)$, and the same monotonicity argument shows that the log posterior never decreases. A prior on the covariances of a Gaussian mixture, one of the defences against collapsing components, works this way: it turns the M-step into a regularized fit.

**Parameters as latent variables: hyperparameters by EM.** In the [relevance vector machine](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine) the weights $w$ have the prior $\mathcal N(0, \operatorname{diag}(\alpha)^{-1})$, and the precisions $\alpha_j$ are chosen by maximizing the evidence $p(t \mid \alpha, \beta)$. Treat $w$ as the latent variable and $\alpha$ as the parameter. The E-step is the Gaussian posterior $\mathcal N(w \mid \mu, \Sigma)$ of the weights. In the M-step only the prior depends on $\alpha$:

$$
\mathbb E_q\big[\log p(w \mid \alpha)\big] = \sum_j \Big(\tfrac12 \log \alpha_j - \tfrac12\, \alpha_j\, \mathbb E_q[w_j^2]\Big) + \text{const}, \qquad \alpha_j^{\text{new}} = \frac{1}{\mathbb E_q[w_j^2]} = \frac{1}{\mu_j^2 + \Sigma_{jj}} .
$$

EM thus gives a second set of evidence updates, guaranteed to increase the evidence at every step. [Tipping (2001](#references), Appendix A) derives both and reports that the [fixed-point updates of MacKay](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine#type-ii-maximum-likelihood-mackays-fixed-point), $\alpha_j = \gamma_j/\mu_j^2$ with $\gamma_j = 1 - \alpha_j \Sigma_{jj}$, converge much faster in practice, which is why the RVM uses those.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> One identity organizes this section: $\log p(X) = \mathcal L(q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, \text{posterior})$. EM can afford the exact posterior, so its E-step closes the gap. Variational EM restricts $q$ to a family it can handle and lives with the gap that remains. Bayesian PCA keeps the exact E-step and puts a prior with one precision per column on the loadings; the evidence then has no reason to keep a column that only fits noise, and the column is switched off.</aside>

## Variational EM: when the E-step cannot be exact

The exact E-step needs the posterior $p(Z \mid X, \theta)$. For a mixture it is a table of responsibilities; for most models with many interacting latent variables it is a distribution over exponentially many configurations or a continuous density with no closed form. **Variational EM** ([Neal and Hinton, 1998](#references)) keeps the bound and restricts the E-step to a family $\mathcal Q$ of distributions that can be handled:

$$
q^{t+1} = \arg\max_{q \in \mathcal Q}\, \mathcal L(q, \theta^t) = \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal Q}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X, \theta^t)\big), \qquad \theta^{t+1} = \arg\max_\theta\, \mathcal L(q^{t+1}, \theta) .
$$

The two forms of the E-step are the same problem, because $\log p(X \mid \theta^t)$ does not depend on $q$. Each step still increases $\mathcal L$, so the bound never decreases. What is lost is the tightness after the E-step: the gap is the smallest KL within $\mathcal Q$, not zero, and the third link of the monotonicity chain $\ell(\theta^{t+1}) \ge \mathcal L(q^{t+1}, \theta^{t+1}) \ge \mathcal L(q^{t+1}, \theta^t) = \ell(\theta^t)$ becomes an inequality in the wrong direction. The log-likelihood can go down, and the fixed point maximizes the bound, not the likelihood. Since the bound is the likelihood minus a gap, its maximizer is pulled towards parameters at which the posterior is easy to approximate by a member of $\mathcal Q$, a bias that can be large ([Turner and Sahani, 2011](#references)).

**Worked example: hard EM is variational EM.** Take $\mathcal Q$ to be the point masses, $q_i(z_i) = [z_i = \hat z_i]$. The entropy of a point mass is zero, so $\mathcal L = \sum_i \log p(x_i, \hat z_i \mid \theta)$, maximized by the most probable component of each point; the gap is

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_i \,\|\, p(z_i \mid x_i, \theta)\big) = \sum_{z} [z = \hat z_i] \log \frac{[z = \hat z_i]}{p(z \mid x_i, \theta)} = -\log p(\hat z_i \mid x_i, \theta) .
$$

This E-step is the assignment step of k-means and of the hard EM of the [EM note](/notes/ml/em-algorithm#k-means-is-the-hard-equal-variance-limit). On that note's toy model, the mixture $\tfrac12 \mathcal N(0, 1) + \tfrac12 \mathcal N(\theta, 1)$ on the seven points $-0.8, -0.2, 0.3, 0.9, 2.1, 2.8, 3.4$, start at $\theta_0 = 0.6$. The posterior probabilities of the second component are $0.341, 0.426, 0.500, 0.589, 0.746, 0.818, 0.865$; the first three points go to the first component and the last four to the second, and the gap is

$$
-\big(\log 0.659 + \log 0.574 + \log 0.500 + \log 0.589 + \log 0.746 + \log 0.818 + \log 0.865\big) = 2.83 .
$$

The M-step moves $\theta$ to the mean of the four points, $2.300$, where the log-likelihood is $-12.06$ and the next E-step leaves a gap of $0.77$. Hard EM converges to $\theta = 2.767$ with $\ell = -12.05$ and a gap of $0.45$; exact EM converges to the maximum likelihood estimate $\theta = 2.540$ with $\ell = -12.00$ and no gap. Hard EM even climbs faster at first ($-12.06$ against $-12.83$ after one M-step), and still stops at the wrong place: it maximizes its own bound, which at $\theta = 2.767$ is $-12.50$, higher than its value $-12.57$ at the maximum likelihood estimate.

Point masses are the crudest family. The next note uses the family of factorized distributions, $q(Z) = \prod_j q_j(Z_j)$, for which the E-step has a closed-form coordinate update, and lets $q$ cover the parameters too, so that the M-step disappears and the whole posterior is approximated ([Beal, 2003](#references)).

## Bayesian PCA: a precision for every column

Bayesian PCA ([Bishop, 1999a](#references)) keeps probabilistic PCA and adds a prior on the loadings with one precision per column:

$$
p(W \mid \alpha) = \prod_{j=1}^{k} \Big(\frac{\alpha_j}{2\pi}\Big)^{d/2} \exp\Big(-\frac{\alpha_j}{2}\, \lVert w_j \rVert^2\Big), \qquad k = d - 1 .
$$

This is automatic relevance determination applied to latent dimensions instead of input features. A large $\alpha_j$ forces column $w_j$ towards zero, and a zero column removes latent dimension $j$ from the model, since $W z$ no longer depends on $z_j$. $k = d - 1$ is the largest useful value: with $k = d$ the covariance $W W^\top + \sigma^2 I$ could absorb the noise into $W$.

The ideal would be to choose $\alpha$ and $\sigma^2$ by maximizing the evidence $p(X \mid \alpha, \sigma^2) = \int p(X \mid W, \sigma^2)\, p(W \mid \alpha)\, dW$, but $W$ enters the covariance of $x$ quadratically and the integral has no closed form. Bishop's scheme, also the one of the [lectures of Vetrov and Kropotov](#references), approximates it in the spirit of the [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) and alternates two steps:

1. **MAP loadings.** With $\alpha$ fixed, find the MAP estimate of $W$ and $\sigma^2$ by MAP-EM with the codes $z_i$ as latent variables.
2. **Precisions.** With $W$ fixed, set $\alpha_j = d / \lVert w_j \rVert^2$.

The second update is MacKay's evidence fixed point of the relevance vector machine applied to a whole column, $\alpha_j = \gamma_j / \lVert w_j \rVert^2$ with $\gamma_j = d - \alpha_j \operatorname{tr} \Sigma_{w_j}$ and $\Sigma_{w_j}$ the posterior covariance of the column, and with every one of the $d$ entries of the column counted as well determined by the data, $\gamma_j = d$. It is also the value of $\alpha_j$ that maximizes $\log p(w_j \mid \alpha_j) = \frac d2 \log \alpha_j - \frac{\alpha_j}{2} \lVert w_j \rVert^2$ at the current $w_j$.

**The E-step** is exact, because $z_i$ and $x_i$ are jointly Gaussian. By the [conditioning formula](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals),

$$
M = W^\top W + \sigma^2 I_k, \qquad \mathbb E[z_i] = M^{-1} W^\top x_i, \qquad \mathbb E[z_i z_i^\top] = \sigma^2 M^{-1} + \mathbb E[z_i]\, \mathbb E[z_i]^\top .
$$

**The M-step** maximizes $\sum_i \mathbb E\big[\log p(x_i \mid z_i, W, \sigma^2)\big] + \log p(W \mid \alpha)$. The terms with $W$ are $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_i \mathbb E\lVert x_i - W z_i \rVert^2 - \frac12 \sum_j \alpha_j \lVert w_j \rVert^2$, and with $\mathbb E\lVert x - W z \rVert^2 = \lVert x \rVert^2 - 2\, \mathbb E[z]^\top W^\top x + \operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbb E[z z^\top] W^\top W\big)$ the gradient in $W$ is

$$
\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \Big(\sum_i x_i\, \mathbb E[z_i]^\top - W \sum_i \mathbb E[z_i z_i^\top]\Big) - W A = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
W = \Big(\sum_i x_i\, \mathbb E[z_i]^\top\Big) \Big(\sum_i \mathbb E[z_i z_i^\top] + \sigma^2 A\Big)^{-1} .
$$

With $A = 0$ this is the EM algorithm for probabilistic PCA ([Tipping and Bishop, 1999](#references)); the prior adds $\sigma^2 A$, a ridge penalty of its own size for every column. The noise variance does not appear in the prior, and setting the derivative of $-\frac{nd}{2} \log \sigma^2 - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_i \mathbb E\lVert x_i - W z_i \rVert^2$ to zero gives

$$
\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{nd} \sum_{i=1}^n \Big(\lVert x_i \rVert^2 - 2\, \mathbb E[z_i]^\top W^\top x_i + \operatorname{tr}\big(\mathbb E[z_i z_i^\top]\, W^\top W\big)\Big),
$$

with the new $W$. One iteration costs $O(ndk + k^3)$ and never forms the $d \times d$ covariance.

### Why a column switches off

At a fixed point the kept columns lie along eigenvectors of $S$: in the Code section the largest principal angle between the kept columns and the three leading eigenvectors is below $0.01°$. Along one such eigenvector, with eigenvalue $\lambda$ and $u = \lVert w_j \rVert^2$, the covariance of $x$ has eigenvalue $c = u + \sigma^2$, and the log-likelihood of probabilistic PCA splits into one term per direction. The MAP objective for $u$ is

$$
-\frac n2 \Big(\log c + \frac{\lambda}{c}\Big) - \frac{\alpha_j}{2}\, u, \qquad \text{stationary at} \quad \frac{n(\lambda - c)}{c^2} = \alpha_j .
$$

Substituting the precision update $\alpha_j = d/u$ gives $n(\lambda - c)\,u = d\,c^2$, a quadratic in $u$:

$$
(n + d)\, u^2 - \big[n(\lambda - \sigma^2) - 2d\sigma^2\big]\, u + d\sigma^4 = 0 .
$$

It has a positive root only if the bracket is positive and the discriminant non-negative, $n(\lambda - \sigma^2) - 2d\sigma^2 \ge 2\sigma^2\sqrt{d(n + d)}$, that is

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\lambda}{\sigma^2} \;\ge\; 1 + \frac{2\big(d + \sqrt{d(n + d)}\big)}{n}\;}
$$

Below the threshold the only fixed point is $w_j = 0$ with $\alpha_j = \infty$: the column is switched off. Maximum likelihood keeps a direction whenever $\lambda > \sigma^2$; the prior asks for extra evidence that shrinks as $n$ grows, about $2\sqrt{d/n}$ in relative terms when $n \gg d$. Above the threshold the larger root is the squared length $u = \lVert w_j \rVert^2$ that EM reaches, a little smaller than the maximum likelihood value $\lambda - \sigma^2$.

**Worked example.** For the data of the baseline, $n = 100$ and $d = 10$ give the threshold $1 + 2(10 + \sqrt{1100})/100 = 1.863$. Bayesian PCA with $k = 9$ columns removes six of them, at EM iterations $55, 67, 76, 124, 217, 218$ (a column is removed when $\alpha_j$ exceeds $10^8$), and ends with $\sigma^2 = 0.977$, close to the true noise variance. The threshold is then $1.863 \cdot 0.977 = 1.82$: the third eigenvalue, $4.45$, is above it, the fourth, $1.52$, below, so exactly three columns survive. For $\lambda = 4.45$ the quadratic is

$$
110\, u^2 - 327.8\, u + 9.545 = 0, \qquad u = \frac{327.8 + \sqrt{327.8^2 - 4 \cdot 110 \cdot 9.545}}{220} = 2.950, \qquad \lVert w_3 \rVert = 1.717,
$$

the length EM finds, against $\sqrt{\lambda - \sigma^2} = \sqrt{4.45 - 0.977} = 1.86$ for maximum likelihood at the same $\sigma^2$. The other two kept columns have lengths $7.450$ and $3.136$, again given by the larger roots of their quadratics, and $\alpha = (0.180, 1.017, 3.391)$.

## What changed

| | Probabilistic PCA, maximum likelihood | Bayesian PCA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of components | chosen beforehand, or by fitting every $k$ | $k = d - 1$, unused columns switched off |
| Prior on $W$ | none | $\mathcal N(w_j \mid 0, \alpha_j^{-1} I_d)$ for each column |
| A direction is kept when | $\lambda > \sigma^2$ | $\lambda/\sigma^2 \ge 1 + 2(d + \sqrt{d(n + d)})/n$ |
| Column length | $\sqrt{\lambda - \sigma^2}$ | the larger root of a quadratic, slightly shorter |
| Fitting | eigendecomposition, or EM | EM with a ridge term $\sigma^2 A$, alternated with $\alpha_j = d/\lVert w_j \rVert^2$ |
| Noise variance in the example | $0.388$ with $k = 9$ (true 1) | $0.977$ |

| | EM | Variational EM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E-step | the exact posterior | the best $q$ in a family $\mathcal Q$ |
| Gap after the E-step | zero | the smallest KL in $\mathcal Q$ |
| What never decreases | the log-likelihood | the bound $\mathcal L$ |
| Fixed point | a stationary point of the likelihood | a stationary point of the bound, biased towards easy posteriors |

## Limitations

- **The evidence is approximated, not maximized.** The update $\alpha_j = d/\lVert w_j \rVert^2$ treats every entry of a column as well determined and uses a point estimate of $W$; the threshold above is a property of this scheme. A variational treatment of the same model, with a factorized posterior over $W$, $z$ and $\alpha$ instead of point estimates, maximizes a true lower bound on the evidence ([Bishop, 1999b](#references)).
- **Slow convergence.** Pruning is fast, but the surviving columns align with the eigenvectors over thousands of iterations; with many columns near the threshold, the result after a fixed number of iterations depends on when one stops.
- **Linear and Gaussian.** Like probabilistic PCA, the model sees only second moments and linear structure; curved manifolds need mixtures of probabilistic PCA or nonlinear models such as the VAE.
- **Variational EM is biased.** Its fixed points maximize a bound whose gap depends on the parameters, so parameter estimates and, worse, comparisons of models by the bound can be distorted, and the size of the distortion is not known from the bound alone.
- **Local optima remain.** Neither MAP-EM nor variational EM is guaranteed to find the global maximum of its objective; the starting point decides which columns survive when several are near the threshold.

## What you should remember

- For every $q$, $\log p(X \mid \theta) = \mathcal L(q, \theta) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X, \theta))$; EM is coordinate ascent on $\mathcal L$, and its exact E-step closes the gap.
- A prior on $\theta$ adds $\log p(\theta)$ to the M-step (MAP-EM); treating weights as latent and hyperparameters as parameters turns EM into evidence maximization, with $\alpha_j = 1/(\mu_j^2 + \Sigma_{jj})$ for ARD.
- Variational EM restricts the E-step to a family $\mathcal Q$; it increases the bound, not the likelihood, and converges to a biased point. Hard EM is the special case of point masses, with gap $-\log p(\hat z_i \mid x_i, \theta)$ per point.
- Bayesian PCA gives every column of $W$ its own precision, fits $W$ and $\sigma^2$ by MAP-EM with the extra term $\sigma^2 A$, and re-estimates $\alpha_j = d/\lVert w_j \rVert^2$.
- A direction with sample eigenvalue $\lambda$ survives only if $\lambda/\sigma^2 \ge 1 + 2(d + \sqrt{d(n + d)})/n$; otherwise its column goes to zero and the dimension is chosen automatically.

## Code

Exact and hard EM on the seven-point mixture, the decomposition $\log p = \mathcal L + D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ for an arbitrary $q$, maximum likelihood probabilistic PCA for every $k$, and Bayesian PCA by EM with the fixed-point check of the text. NumPy and SciPy only; seeds are fixed, and exact numbers can differ slightly across platforms and library versions. The script runs in about a second.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm
from scipy.linalg import subspace_angles

# the seven-point mixture 0.5 N(0, 1) + 0.5 N(theta, 1): exact EM and variational EM with point masses (hard EM)
x = np.array([-0.8, -0.2, 0.3, 0.9, 2.1, 2.8, 3.4])
loglik = lambda th: float(np.log(0.5 * norm.pdf(x) + 0.5 * norm.pdf(x - th)).sum())
def bound(th, q):                        # L(q, theta), q = probability of the second component for each point
    with np.errstate(divide='ignore', invalid='ignore'):
        a = np.where(q < 1, (1 - q) * np.log(0.5 * norm.pdf(x) / (1 - q)), 0.0)
        b = np.where(q > 0, q * np.log(0.5 * norm.pdf(x - th) / q), 0.0)
    return float((a + b).sum())
exact = lambda th: norm.pdf(x - th) / (norm.pdf(x) + norm.pdf(x - th))   # E-step: the posterior
hard = lambda th: (np.abs(x - th) < np.abs(x)).astype(float)             # E-step restricted to point masses
for name, estep in (('exact', exact), ('hard', hard)):
    th, out = 0.6, []
    for it in range(200):
        q = estep(th)
        if it < 2: out.append((round(float(th), 3), round(loglik(th), 2), round(bound(th, q), 2)))
        th = (q * x).sum() / q.sum()                                     # M-step: q-weighted mean
    q = estep(th)
    print(name, out, round(float(th), 3), round(loglik(th), 2), round(bound(th, q), 2))
# exact [(0.6, -16.35, -16.35), (1.662, -12.83, -12.83)] 2.54 -12.0 -12.0
# hard [(0.6, -16.35, -19.18), (2.3, -12.06, -12.82)] 2.767 -12.05 -12.5

# log p = L + KL holds for any q, not only for the posterior
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
q = rng.uniform(size=7); r = exact(1.0)
kl = (q * np.log(q / r) + (1 - q) * np.log((1 - q) / (1 - r))).sum()
print(round(loglik(1.0), 6), round(bound(1.0, q) + kl, 6))           # -14.691242 -14.691242

# Bayesian PCA: 100 points in d = 10 dimensions from a model with three latent dimensions and unit noise
d, k, n = 10, 3, 100
W_true = rng.normal(size=(d, k)) * np.array([3.0, 2.0, 1.0])
X = rng.normal(size=(n, k)) @ W_true.T + rng.normal(size=(n, d))
X = X - X.mean(axis=0)
lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(X.T @ X / n); lam, V = lam[::-1], V[:, ::-1]
print(lam.round(2))
# [62.23 12.    4.45  1.52  1.35  1.18  0.89  0.84  0.63  0.39]

# maximum likelihood probabilistic PCA: the log-likelihood only grows with the number of columns
def ppca_loglik(q):
    s2 = lam[q:].mean(); c = np.concatenate([lam[:q], np.full(d - q, s2)])
    return -n / 2 * (d * np.log(2 * np.pi) + np.log(c).sum() + (lam / c).sum())
print([round(float(ppca_loglik(q)), 1) for q in range(1, 10)])
# [-2052.4, -1885.9, -1813.9, -1806.6, -1799.7, -1793.2, -1790.6, -1786.4, -1783.4]
s2 = lam[9:].mean(); print(round(float(s2), 3), np.sqrt(lam[:9] - s2).round(2))   # nine columns: every one is used
# 0.388 [7.86 3.41 2.02 1.07 0.98 0.89 0.71 0.67 0.49]

# EM for the MAP loadings with one ARD precision per column, alpha_j = d / ||w_j||^2; columns with alpha_j > 1e8 are removed
q = d - 1
W, s2, alpha = rng.normal(size=(d, q)), 1.0, np.ones(q)
active = np.arange(q); pruned = []
for it in range(1000):
    Wa = W[:, active]; m = len(active)
    Minv = np.linalg.inv(Wa.T @ Wa + s2 * np.eye(m))
    Ez = X @ Wa @ Minv                                   # E[z_i], n x m
    Szz = n * s2 * Minv + Ez.T @ Ez                      # sum_i E[z_i z_i^T]
    Wa = (X.T @ Ez) @ np.linalg.inv(Szz + s2 * np.diag(alpha[active]))
    s2 = (np.sum(X ** 2) - 2 * np.sum(Ez * (X @ Wa)) + np.trace(Szz @ Wa.T @ Wa)) / (n * d)
    W[:] = 0; W[:, active] = Wa
    alpha[active] = d / (Wa ** 2).sum(axis=0)
    for j in active[alpha[active] > 1e8]: pruned.append((j, it + 1))
    active = active[alpha[active] <= 1e8]
print(len(active), [it for _, it in pruned], round(float(s2), 3))
# 3 [55, 67, 76, 124, 217, 218] 0.977
print(np.sort(np.linalg.norm(W[:, active], axis=0))[::-1].round(3), np.sort(alpha[active]).round(3))
# [7.45  3.136 1.717] [0.18  1.017 3.391]
print(np.degrees(subspace_angles(W[:, active], V[:, :3])).max() < 0.01)   # True: the kept columns span the leading eigenvectors

# the fixed point of one column along an eigenvector with eigenvalue l: (n + d) u^2 - [n (l - s2) - 2 d s2] u + d s2^2 = 0, u = ||w||^2
B = n * (lam[:4] - s2) - 2 * d * s2; disc = B ** 2 - 4 * (n + d) * d * s2 ** 2
print(np.where(disc > 0, np.sqrt((B + np.sqrt(np.maximum(disc, 0))) / (2 * (n + d))), 0).round(3), round(float(1 + 2 * (d + np.sqrt(d * (n + d))) / n), 3))
# [7.45  3.136 1.717 0.   ] 1.863
```

## Next

Variational EM approximated the posterior over the latent variables and kept a point estimate of the parameters. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference) approximates the posterior over everything unknown at once, latent variables and parameters alike, with a factorized $q$. It derives the general update of each factor, $\log q_j^* = \mathbb E_{-j}[\log p] + \text{const}$, works it out for a Gaussian with unknown mean and precision, and shows what the factorization gets wrong.
