The [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors) met the evidence $p(X)$ as a by-product: the normalizer of the posterior, a ratio of two normalizing constants, the product of the one-step predictions of the data. This note makes it the main object. Every model so far was fixed in advance: a likelihood, a prior and the values of its hyperparameters. In practice we must also choose among models, the degree of a polynomial, the number of components, the width of a prior, and the training likelihood cannot make that choice, because the largest model always fits best. The evidence can. It measures how well a model predicted the data before seeing them, it penalizes needless flexibility without any penalty added by hand, and maximizing it over hyperparameters, **empirical Bayes**, replaces a grid search on a validation set. The price is a strong dependence on the prior, which the last section examines.

**Notation.** $\mathcal M$ is a model: a likelihood $p(X \mid \theta, \mathcal M)$ and a prior $p(\theta \mid \mathcal M)$. Its **evidence** or **marginal likelihood** is $p(X \mid \mathcal M) = \int p(X \mid \theta, \mathcal M)\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal M)\, d\theta$. $\hat\theta$ is the maximum likelihood or MAP estimate and $d$ the number of parameters. For regression, $\Phi$ is the $N \times (M+1)$ design matrix of a polynomial of degree $M$ (columns $1, x, \dots, x^M$), $w$ its weights with prior $\mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$, and $\beta$ the noise precision. $K_{10}$ is a Bayes factor.

## Baseline: choosing the degree by the training error

Twenty points $x_i$ equally spaced on $[-1, 1]$, with $y_i = \sin(\pi x_i)$ plus Gaussian noise of standard deviation $0.2$. Fit polynomials of degree $M = 0, \dots, 9$ by [least squares](/notes/ml/least-squares). The root mean squared error on the training points falls at every step: $0.618, 0.411, 0.406, 0.132, 0.131, 0.121, 0.120, 0.119, 0.116, 0.116$. The maximum of the likelihood never decreases when a parameter is added, because the larger model contains the smaller one, so the training fit always picks the largest model.

The usual fix is a [validation set or cross-validation](/notes/ml/validation-and-overfitting#cross-validation-every-example-validates-once): hold out data, compare models on it. It works, but it spends data on the comparison, it is noisy with twenty points, and its cost grows with the number of hyperparameters: a grid over ten of them is out of reach. A criterion computed from the training data alone, that does not prefer bigger models, would avoid all three problems.

## The evidence of a model

Apply Bayes' formula one level up. Models are hypotheses like any other, with prior probabilities $p(\mathcal M)$, and the data update them:

$$
p(\mathcal M \mid X) = \frac{p(X \mid \mathcal M)\, p(\mathcal M)}{\sum_{\mathcal M'} p(X \mid \mathcal M')\, p(\mathcal M')} .
$$

The likelihood of a model is its evidence, the same integral that normalized the posterior over its parameters. Inference then has two levels ([MacKay, 1992](#references)): at the first, the posterior $p(\theta \mid X, \mathcal M)$ within a model; at the second, the posterior over models, through their evidences. With equal prior probabilities, models are ranked by evidence alone. A prediction can also use all models at once, **Bayesian model averaging**:

$$
p(x_* \mid X) = \sum_{\mathcal M} p(x_* \mid X, \mathcal M)\, p(\mathcal M \mid X) .
$$

The evidence is not the likelihood at the best parameter. It is the likelihood averaged over the prior, and by the product rule, as the previous note found for the coin,

$$
p(X \mid \mathcal M) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}, \mathcal M) ,
$$

the probability with which the model predicted each point from the ones before it, starting from the prior. A model is rewarded for predicting the data, not for fitting them after the fact. This is the sense in which the evidence is a form of out-of-sample validation that uses every point both for fitting and for testing ([Fong and Holmes, 2020](#references)).

## Why the evidence prefers simple models

The evidence is a probability distribution over datasets: $\sum_X p(X \mid \mathcal M) = 1$, or an integral for continuous data. A model that can explain many different datasets must spread this unit of probability over all of them, and so gives each one less. A simple model concentrates its probability on the few datasets it can explain. When the data are among them, the simple model wins; when they are not, the flexible one does. This is the Bayesian **Occam's razor** ([MacKay, 2003](#references), Section 28.1): no penalty is added, it follows from normalization.

**Worked example.** Ten flips of a coin. Model $\mathcal M_0$ says the coin is fair. Model $\mathcal M_1$ says its probability of heads $f$ is unknown, with a uniform prior. The probability each model gives to the number of heads $k$ is

$$
P(k \mid \mathcal M_0) = \binom{10}{k} 2^{-10}, \qquad P(k \mid \mathcal M_1) = \int_0^1 \binom{10}{k} f^k (1 - f)^{10-k}\, df = \frac{1}{11} ,
$$

the second because $\binom{n}{k} B(k + 1, n - k + 1) = 1/(n + 1)$: under the uniform prior every count is equally likely.

<OccamCoin />

1. **The simple model** puts its mass near $k = 5$: $P(5 \mid \mathcal M_0) = 252/1024 = 0.246$.
2. **The flexible model** spreads it evenly: $1/11 = 0.091$ for every $k$.
3. **Five heads.** $\mathcal M_0$ predicted this outcome better: the ratio of evidences is $0.246/0.091 = 2.71$ in favour of the fair coin, although $\mathcal M_1$ contains $f = 0.5$ and fits the data exactly as well.
4. **Nine heads.** $P(9 \mid \mathcal M_0) = 10/1024 = 0.0098$, and the ratio is $9.31$ in favour of $\mathcal M_1$.

### Best-fit likelihood times the Occam factor

When the posterior is a narrow peak, the evidence integral is about the height of the integrand at its peak times its width. In one dimension, with posterior width $\sigma_{\theta \mid X}$ and a uniform prior of width $\Delta\theta$, so that $p(\hat\theta) = 1/\Delta\theta$,

$$
p(X \mid \mathcal M) \approx p(X \mid \hat\theta, \mathcal M) \cdot p(\hat\theta \mid \mathcal M) \cdot \sqrt{2\pi}\, \sigma_{\theta \mid X} = \underbrace{p(X \mid \hat\theta, \mathcal M)}_{\text{best-fit likelihood}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\, \sigma_{\theta \mid X}}{\Delta\theta}}_{\text{Occam factor}} .
$$

The Occam factor is the fraction of the prior range that survives the data, at most about 1. A model with many parameters, or with broad priors, pays a small factor for each of them. For 9 heads in 10 under $\mathcal M_1$: $\hat f = 0.9$, the best-fit likelihood is $0.9^9 \cdot 0.1 = 0.0387$, forty times $\mathcal M_0$'s $2^{-10} = 0.00098$. The posterior width is $\sqrt{\hat f(1 - \hat f)/n} = 0.095$ on a prior range of 1, so the Occam factor is $\sqrt{2\pi} \cdot 0.095 = 0.238$, and the evidence is about $0.0387 \cdot 0.238 = 0.0092$; the exact value is $B(10, 2) = 1/110 = 0.0091$. The fair coin has no parameter and pays no Occam factor. Of $\mathcal M_1$'s fortyfold advantage in fit, a factor 4.2 is paid back for its flexibility, leaving the ratio $9.3$.

## Bayes factors

The ratio of the evidences of two models is their **Bayes factor**,

$$
K_{10} = \frac{p(X \mid \mathcal M_1)}{p(X \mid \mathcal M_0)}, \qquad \frac{p(\mathcal M_1 \mid X)}{p(\mathcal M_0 \mid X)} = K_{10} \cdot \frac{p(\mathcal M_1)}{p(\mathcal M_0)} :
$$

posterior odds are the Bayes factor times the prior odds. Unlike a p-value, it can support the simpler model as well as reject it. [Kass and Raftery (1995)](#references), following Jeffreys, read it on this scale:

| $K_{10}$ | $2 \log K_{10}$ | Evidence against $\mathcal M_0$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 3 | 0 to 2 | not worth more than a bare mention |
| 3 to 20 | 2 to 6 | positive |
| 20 to 150 | 6 to 10 | strong |
| above 150 | above 10 | very strong |

Nine heads in ten give $K_{10} = 9.3$, positive evidence for a biased coin. Five heads in ten give $K_{01} = 2.71$ for the fair coin, barely worth a mention. Evidence for the simpler hypothesis accumulates slowly: 50 heads in 100 flips give $K_{01} = 8.04$, still only positive. The flexible model is never refuted by data it can also explain; it only loses its Occam factor, which shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$.

## The evidence of a linear-Gaussian model

For regression the evidence has a closed form whenever the weights have a Gaussian prior and the noise is Gaussian. Write the model as $y = \Phi w + \varepsilon$ with $w \sim \mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$ and $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \beta^{-1} I)$ independent. Then $y$ is an [affine function of Gaussian vectors](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps), hence Gaussian, with mean 0 and covariance $\Phi \operatorname{Cov}(w) \Phi^\top + \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)$:

$$
p(y \mid \alpha, \beta) = \mathcal N\big(y \mid 0,\ C\big), \qquad C = \alpha^{-1} \Phi \Phi^\top + \beta^{-1} I ,
$$

$$
\log p(y \mid \alpha, \beta) = \underbrace{-\tfrac12\, y^\top C^{-1} y}_{\text{data fit}}\ \underbrace{-\tfrac12 \log \det C}_{\text{complexity}}\ - \tfrac N2 \log 2\pi .
$$

The two terms are the razor in matrix form. Adding a column to $\Phi$ adds a rank-one term to $C$: the model can now produce data in one more direction, the fit term can only improve, and $\det C$ grows, because the same unit of probability is spread over a larger volume of datasets. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression) derives the posterior of $w$ and the equivalent form in weight space,

$$
\log p(y \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{M + 1}{2} \log \alpha + \frac N2 \log \beta - E(m) - \frac12 \log \det A - \frac N2 \log 2\pi ,
$$

with $A = \alpha I + \beta \Phi^\top \Phi$, $m = \beta A^{-1} \Phi^\top y$ the posterior mean, which is the [ridge solution](/notes/ml/regularization#ridge-a-penalty-on-the-size-of-the-weights) with $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$, and $E(m) = \frac\beta2 \lVert y - \Phi m \rVert^2 + \frac\alpha2 \lVert m \rVert^2$ ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 3.5.1). The Code section checks that the two forms agree.

The evidence jumps by 31 nats between degrees 2 and 3, where the cubic first captures the shape of the sine; the even degree 2 adds nothing, because $\sin(\pi x)$ is odd, and is penalized for it. After degree 3 every extra weight improves the fit a little and costs an Occam factor, and the evidence falls slowly. The peak is at $M = 3$. Two cautions on reading it. The differences beyond the peak are small: degree 5 is $e^{-1.0}$ times as probable as degree 3, $2 \log K = 2.0$, not worth more than a bare mention. And the evidence answers "which model is most probable", not "which predicts best": the error against the true curve is slightly lower at degrees 5 to 7, because the sine is not a polynomial and more terms approximate it better. The evidence prefers the simplest model that explains the data to within the noise.

## Empirical Bayes: fitting hyperparameters by the evidence

The hyperparameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ were fixed above. A Bayesian could give them a prior too, a **hyperprior**, and integrate them out:

$$
p(y) = \iint p(y \mid \alpha, \beta)\, p(\alpha, \beta)\, d\alpha\, d\beta .
$$

This hierarchical model is rarely tractable. **Empirical Bayes**, or **type-II maximum likelihood**, replaces the integral by its peak: choose the hyperparameters that maximize the evidence,

$$
(\hat\alpha, \hat\beta) = \arg\max_{\alpha, \beta}\ p(y \mid \alpha, \beta) = \arg\max_{\alpha, \beta} \int p(y \mid w, \beta)\, p(w \mid \alpha)\, dw ,
$$

then use the posterior $p(w \mid y, \hat\alpha, \hat\beta)$. This is maximum likelihood one level up, with the weights integrated out. It is justified when the evidence is sharply peaked in the hyperparameters, which is usual: there are few of them and all the data inform them. Maximizing over $w$ and $\alpha$ together instead would fail. The joint density $p(y \mid w, \beta)\, p(w \mid \alpha)$ at $w = 0$ grows without bound as $\alpha \to \infty$, because the prior $\mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$ becomes an ever higher spike at zero; integrating $w$ out is what makes the problem well posed.

For the linear-Gaussian model the maximum has a fixed-point form ([MacKay, 1992](#references); [Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 3.5.2). With $\lambda_j$ the eigenvalues of $\Phi^\top \Phi$, set the gradient of the log evidence to zero:

$$
\gamma = \sum_j \frac{\beta \lambda_j}{\alpha + \beta \lambda_j}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\gamma}{m^\top m}, \qquad \frac1\beta = \frac{\lVert y - \Phi m \rVert^2}{N - \gamma} ,
$$

and iterate, recomputing $m$ each time. Each term of $\gamma$ is between 0 and 1: near 1 for a direction in weight space that the data determine, near 0 for one left to the prior. So $\gamma$ is the **effective number of parameters**, and the noise estimate divides by $N - \gamma$, the degrees of freedom left, like the $n - 1$ of the [sample variance](/notes/probability/estimators#the-sample-variance-and-n-1). For the cubic, the iteration reaches $\hat\alpha = 0.418$, $\hat\beta = 46.0$ (a noise standard deviation of $0.147$), $\gamma = 3.97$ of 4 weights, and the log evidence rises from $-4.18$ to $-2.13$. For degree 9, $\gamma = 6.28$ of 10: the prior switches off the directions that the data cannot determine, and the log evidence is $-5.86$, still below the cubic. The [relevance vector machine](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine) gives every weight its own $\alpha_j$ and runs the same iteration; most $\alpha_j$ go to infinity and their features are removed.

Empirical Bayes uses the data twice, to set the prior and then to update it, and so understates uncertainty about the hyperparameters. With many observations per hyperparameter the error is small; with a hyperparameter per weight it is not, and the evidence can then overfit.

## The BIC approximation

When the evidence has no closed form, it can be approximated by the same peak-times-width argument in $d$ dimensions. Expand the log of the integrand to second order around its maximum $\hat\theta$, with $H$ the Hessian of $-\log\big(p(X \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\big)$ at $\hat\theta$; the integral of the resulting Gaussian is the **Laplace approximation**,

$$
\log p(X \mid \mathcal M) \approx \log p(X \mid \hat\theta) + \log p(\hat\theta) + \frac d2 \log 2\pi - \frac12 \log \det H .
$$

For $n$ i.i.d. points the Hessian grows linearly, $H \approx n\, I_1(\hat\theta)$ with $I_1$ the [Fisher information](/notes/probability/maximum-likelihood#fisher-information) of one point, so $\log \det H = d \log n + \log \det I_1$. Keeping only the terms that grow with $n$,

$$
\log p(X \mid \mathcal M) = \log p(X \mid \hat\theta) - \frac d2 \log n + O(1), \qquad \mathrm{BIC} = -2 \log p(X \mid \hat\theta) + d \log n ,
$$

the **Bayesian information criterion** of [Schwarz (1978)](#references), used in the [Machine Learning course](/notes/ml/em-algorithm#how-many-components-bic) to choose the number of mixture components; smaller is better. The derivation shows what it drops: the prior and the curvature, terms of order 1 that do not grow with $n$. For 9 heads in 10 under $\mathcal M_1$, the exact log evidence is $\log(1/110) = -4.700$, the Laplace approximation gives $-4.687$, and the BIC approximation $-3.251 - \frac12 \log 10 = -4.402$. For the polynomials, with $d = M + 2$ (the weights and the noise variance), the BIC also picks degree 3. It needs only the maximum likelihood fit and can be used for any model; it is reliable when $n$ is large compared with $d$, and it ignores the prior entirely, which is both its robustness and its limitation. The [Laplace approximation note](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) uses the full expression, curvature included.

## How the evidence depends on the prior

The posterior forgets the prior as data accumulate; the evidence does not. The Occam factor is the ratio of the posterior width to the prior width, so a prior twice as broad halves the evidence of every parameter it governs, however many data there are. The same twenty points under broader priors on the weights:

| Prior precision $\alpha$ | $M = 1$ | $M = 3$ | $M = 5$ | $M = 9$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | −34.5 | −4.2 | −5.2 | −6.8 |
| $0.01$ | −38.8 | −8.8 | −13.6 | −18.3 |
| $0.0001$ | −43.4 | −18.0 | −27.3 | −38.6 |

The cubic fit hardly changes between these priors, since the data determine its four weights. But once the prior is broad compared with the posterior, each tenfold broadening of its standard deviation costs $\log 10 = 2.3$ nats per weight: from $\alpha = 0.01$ to $0.0001$ the cubic loses $9.2 = 4 \cdot 2.3$ nats and degree 9 loses $20.3$. The gap between degrees 3 and 9 widens from 2.6 to 20.6 nats. In the limit of a flat prior, $\alpha \to 0$, the evidence of every model with parameters goes to zero and the model without parameters always wins: the **Jeffreys–Lindley paradox** ([Lindley, 1957](#references)). An improper prior, such as a uniform density on the whole real line, makes the evidence undefined, while the posterior is often still fine.

Three practices follow. Priors used for model comparison must be proper and set on the scale of the problem: here, inputs scaled to $[-1, 1]$ and outputs of order 1 make $\alpha = 1$ reasonable. Hyperparameters that set the scale are better fitted by empirical Bayes than guessed. And conclusions should be reported with their sensitivity to the prior; when they change, held-out likelihood or cross-validation, which depend on the prior only through the posterior, are the safer criterion.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> The evidence $p(X \mid \mathcal M)$ is a distribution over datasets, so a flexible model must spread it thin. Comparing evidences compares how well each model predicted the data before seeing them: the best-fit likelihood times an Occam factor, the fraction of the prior range the data leave. It chooses models and fits hyperparameters from the training data alone, at the cost of depending on the prior width.</aside>

| --- | --- | --- |
| Criterion | $\max_\theta p(X \mid \theta)$, or error on held-out data | $\int p(X \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta$ |
| Larger models | always fit better | pay an Occam factor per parameter |
| Polynomial degree, 20 points | training error picks 9 | the evidence picks 3 |
| Data used | a part held out | all of it |
| Hyperparameters | grid search on validation data | maximize the evidence: $\hat\alpha = 0.418$, $\hat\beta = 46$ for the cubic |
| Dependence on the prior | through the posterior only | strong: the width of every prior enters |
| Approximation when no formula | — | Laplace, BIC $= -2 \log p(X \mid \hat\theta) + d \log n$ |

## Limitations

- **Sensitivity to the prior.** The evidence depends on the prior width even when the posterior does not, and is undefined for improper priors. Model comparison needs priors that mean something.
- **Probable is not predictive.** The evidence ranks models by how probable they are given the data. When no model is right, as with a polynomial for a sine, the most probable model need not predict best.
- **Computation.** The evidence is an integral over all parameters, harder than the posterior itself. Outside linear-Gaussian and conjugate models it needs the Laplace approximation, a variational bound or careful sampling, and naive Monte Carlo estimates of it have enormous variance.
- **Empirical Bayes uses the data twice.** It ignores the uncertainty in the hyperparameters and can overfit when there are many of them.
- **BIC is asymptotic.** It assumes many points per parameter and a regular model with a unique, interior maximum; mixtures and neural networks break these conditions.

## What you should remember

- The evidence $p(X \mid \mathcal M) = \int p(X \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta$ is the likelihood of a model; models are compared by $p(\mathcal M \mid X) \propto p(X \mid \mathcal M)\, p(\mathcal M)$.
- It is a distribution over datasets and the product of one-step predictions: flexible models spread their probability thin, which is the Bayesian Occam's razor.
- Evidence ≈ best-fit likelihood × Occam factor, the posterior width over the prior width for each parameter.
- The Bayes factor $K_{10}$ turns prior odds into posterior odds; it can support the simpler model, but slowly.
- Linear-Gaussian models: $y \sim \mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} \Phi\Phi^\top + \beta^{-1} I)$; the log evidence is a fit term plus $-\frac12 \log \det C$. On twenty noisy points of a sine it peaks at degree 3.
- Empirical Bayes maximizes the evidence over hyperparameters; $\gamma = \sum_j \beta\lambda_j/(\alpha + \beta\lambda_j)$ counts the well-determined parameters.
- BIC $= -2 \log p(X \mid \hat\theta) + d \log n$ keeps the terms of the Laplace approximation that grow with $n$ and drops the prior.
- Broader priors lower the evidence of every model with parameters; in the flat limit the simplest model always wins.

## Code

The coin's Bayes factors, the Occam factor with the Laplace and BIC approximations, the evidence as a product of predictions, the evidence of polynomial models in two equivalent forms with its peak, the effect of the prior width, empirical Bayes by fixed-point iteration checked against a numerical optimizer, and the unbounded joint maximum. Seeds are fixed; the script runs in about a second, and exact numbers can differ in the last digit across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import optimize, special, stats

# Occam's razor on a coin: M0 says the coin is fair, M1 gives its bias a uniform prior.
# The evidence of a sequence with k heads in n flips: 2^-n under M0, B(k + 1, n - k + 1) under M1.
def log_ev_fair(k, n): return -n * np.log(2)
def log_ev_uniform(k, n): return special.betaln(k + 1, n - k + 1)
for k, n in ((5, 10), (9, 10), (50, 100)):
    bf = np.exp(log_ev_uniform(k, n) - log_ev_fair(k, n))      # the Bayes factor of M1 against M0
    print(k, n, round(bf, 3), round(1 / bf, 2))
# 5 10 0.369 2.71 | 9 10 9.309 0.11 | 50 100 0.124 8.04

# best-fit likelihood x Occam factor, and the Laplace and BIC approximations, for M1 with 9 heads in 10
k, n = 9, 10
th = k / n
log_fit = k * np.log(th) + (n - k) * np.log(1 - th)            # log p(D | theta_hat)
sd = np.sqrt(th * (1 - th) / n)                                 # posterior width from the curvature
print(round(np.exp(log_fit), 4), round(np.sqrt(2 * np.pi) * sd, 3))           # 0.0387 0.238
print(round(log_ev_uniform(k, n), 3), round(log_fit + 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) + np.log(sd), 3),
      round(log_fit - 0.5 * np.log(n), 3))                      # -4.7 -4.687 -4.402: exact, Laplace, BIC

# the evidence is the product of one-step-ahead predictions
seq = np.array([1] * 9 + [0])
a = b = 1.0
lp = 0.0
for x in seq:
    lp += np.log(a / (a + b) if x else b / (a + b))
    a, b = a + x, b + 1 - x
print(np.isclose(lp, log_ev_uniform(9, 10)))                    # True

# polynomial regression: y = Phi w + noise, w ~ N(0, I / alpha), noise ~ N(0, 1 / beta)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 20
x = np.linspace(-1, 1, N)
y = np.round(np.sin(np.pi * x) + 0.2 * rng.normal(size=N), 3)
x_test = np.linspace(-1, 1, 1001)

def design(x, M): return np.vander(x, M + 1, increasing=True)  # columns 1, x, ..., x^M

def log_evidence(Phi, y, alpha, beta):
    """log N(y | 0, C) with C = Phi Phi^T / alpha + I / beta, by a Cholesky factor."""
    C = Phi @ Phi.T / alpha + np.eye(len(y)) / beta
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(C)
    z = np.linalg.solve(L, y)
    return -0.5 * z @ z - np.log(np.diag(L)).sum() - 0.5 * len(y) * np.log(2 * np.pi)

def log_evidence_weights(Phi, y, alpha, beta):
    """The same number from the weight space: A = alpha I + beta Phi^T Phi, m = beta A^-1 Phi^T y."""
    n, d = Phi.shape
    A = alpha * np.eye(d) + beta * Phi.T @ Phi
    m = beta * np.linalg.solve(A, Phi.T @ y)
    E = beta / 2 * ((y - Phi @ m) ** 2).sum() + alpha / 2 * m @ m
    return d / 2 * np.log(alpha) + n / 2 * np.log(beta) - E - 0.5 * np.linalg.slogdet(A)[1] - n / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi)

for M in range(10):

    w = np.linalg.lstsq(Phi, y, rcond=None)[0]                  # maximum likelihood fit
    rss = ((y - Phi @ w) ** 2).sum()
    log_lik = -N / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi * rss / N) - N / 2       # at the ML noise variance rss / N
    bic = -2 * log_lik + (M + 2) * np.log(N)                     # M + 1 weights and the noise variance
    test = np.sqrt(((design(x_test, M) @ w - np.sin(np.pi * x_test)) ** 2).mean())
    le = log_evidence(Phi, y, 1.0, 25.0)
    assert np.isclose(le, log_evidence_weights(Phi, y, 1.0, 25.0))
    print(M, round(np.sqrt(rss / N), 3), round(le, 2), round(bic, 2), round(test, 3))

# empirical Bayes: maximize the evidence over alpha and beta, by MacKay's fixed-point updates
def evidence_fit(Phi, y, alpha=1.0, beta=1.0, iters=500):
    lam0 = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Phi.T @ Phi)
    for _ in range(iters):
        A = alpha * np.eye(Phi.shape[1]) + beta * Phi.T @ Phi
        m = beta * np.linalg.solve(A, Phi.T @ y)
        gamma = (beta * lam0 / (alpha + beta * lam0)).sum()         # the effective number of parameters
        alpha = gamma / (m @ m)
        beta = (len(y) - gamma) / ((y - Phi @ m) ** 2).sum()
    return alpha, beta, gamma

for M in (3, 9):
    Phi = design(x, M)
    al, be, ga = evidence_fit(Phi, y)
    res = optimize.minimize(lambda p: -log_evidence(Phi, y, *np.exp(p)), [0.0, 0.0], method='Nelder-Mead',
                            options=dict(xatol=1e-9, fatol=1e-12, maxiter=4000))
    print(M, round(al, 3), round(be, 2), round(ga, 2), round(log_evidence(Phi, y, al, be), 2), np.exp(res.x).round(3))
```
