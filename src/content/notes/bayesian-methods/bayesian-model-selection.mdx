Take twenty points $x_i$ equally spaced on $[-1, 1]$, with targets $y_i = \sin(\pi x_i)$ plus Gaussian noise of standard deviation $0.2$. Fit polynomials of degree $M = 0, 1, \dots, 9$ by [least squares](/notes/ml/least-squares). Which degree should you keep?

The training error cannot say. Its root mean square never rises with the degree, from $0.618$ for a constant to $0.116$ for degree 9, because the larger model contains the smaller one and its best fit can only be at least as good. The usual answer is a [validation set or cross-validation](/notes/ml/validation-and-overfitting#cross-validation-every-example-validates-once). It works, but it spends data on the comparison, it is noisy with twenty points, and its cost grows with the number of hyperparameters: a grid over ten of them is out of reach.

The [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors) met a quantity that avoids all three problems: the evidence, the probability of the data with the parameters integrated out. It is computed from the training data alone, and it does not prefer bigger models.

## The evidence of a model

A model $\mathcal M$ is a likelihood $p(X \mid \theta, \mathcal M)$ together with a prior $p(\theta \mid \mathcal M)$. Its **evidence**, or **marginal likelihood**, is

$$
p(X \mid \mathcal M) = \int p(X \mid \theta, \mathcal M)\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal M)\, d\theta .
$$

The integral is the sum rule over $\theta$ followed by the product rule. It is the probability of generating the data from the model when its parameters are first drawn from the prior ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 3.4).

Apply Bayes' formula one level up. Models are hypotheses like any other, with prior probabilities $p(\mathcal M)$, and the data update them:

$$
p(\mathcal M \mid X) = \frac{p(X \mid \mathcal M)\, p(\mathcal M)}{\sum_{\mathcal M'} p(X \mid \mathcal M')\, p(\mathcal M')} .
$$

The likelihood of a model is its evidence, the same integral that normalized the posterior over its parameters. Inference then has two levels ([MacKay, 1992](#references)). The first is the posterior $p(\theta \mid X, \mathcal M)$ within a model; the second is the posterior over models, through their evidences. With equal prior probabilities, models are ranked by evidence alone.

A prediction need not choose. **Bayesian model averaging** weights every model's predictive by its posterior probability, by the sum rule over models and the product rule:

$$
p(x_* \mid X) = \sum_{\mathcal M} p(x_* \mid X, \mathcal M)\, p(\mathcal M \mid X) .
$$

The evidence averages the likelihood over the prior instead of taking it at the best parameter. By the product rule, as the previous note found for the coin, it is a product of predictions:

$$
p(X \mid \mathcal M) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}, \mathcal M) .
$$

Each factor is the probability with which the model predicted a point from the ones before it, starting from the prior. A model earns evidence by predicting the data before seeing them; fitting them afterwards earns nothing. The log of a ratio of evidences is therefore a difference of predictive scores ([Kass and Raftery, 1995](#references), Section 3.2): a sequential form of validation in which every point is used first for testing and then for fitting.

## Why the evidence prefers simple models

The evidence is a probability distribution over datasets. Sum it over all datasets and exchange the sum with the integral over $\theta$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_X p(X \mid \mathcal M)
&= \int \Big(\sum_X p(X \mid \theta, \mathcal M)\Big)\, p(\theta \mid \mathcal M)\, d\theta \\
&= \int p(\theta \mid \mathcal M)\, d\theta = 1 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

because the likelihood sums to 1 over datasets for every $\theta$; for continuous data the sum is an integral. A model that can explain many different datasets must spread this unit of probability over all of them, and so gives each one less. A simple model concentrates its probability on the few datasets it can explain. When the data are among them, the simple model wins; when they are not, the flexible one does. This is the Bayesian **Occam's razor** ([MacKay, 2003](#references), Section 28.1). No penalty is added; it follows from normalization.

Three dice show the effect without an integral. The first has the faces 1 to 6. The second has the faces 1, 2 and 3, each twice. The third has 1 and 2, each three times. One die is picked at random, rolled, and shows a 3. The third die cannot produce it and is ruled out. The other two can, but the second gives a 3 the probability $2/6$ and the first only $1/6$. With equal prior probabilities, Bayes' formula makes the posterior proportional to these likelihoods: $2/3$ for the second die and $1/3$ for the first. It can produce fewer outcomes, and it predicted this one better.

Take ten flips of a coin. Model $\mathcal M_0$ says the coin is fair. Model $\mathcal M_1$ says its probability of heads $f$ is unknown, with a uniform prior. The probabilities the two models give to the number of heads $k$ are

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(k \mid \mathcal M_0) &= \binom{10}{k} 2^{-10}, \\
P(k \mid \mathcal M_1) &= \int_0^1 \binom{10}{k} f^k (1 - f)^{10-k}\, df = \frac{1}{11} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The integral is the beta function $B(k + 1, n - k + 1) = k!\,(n-k)!/(n+1)!$ of the [conjugate priors note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#beta-bernoulli-and-the-evidence), so

$$
\binom{n}{k} B(k + 1, n - k + 1) = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} \cdot \frac{k!\,(n-k)!}{(n+1)!} = \frac{1}{n + 1} :
$$

under the uniform prior every count is equally likely.

| Heads | $P(k \mid \mathcal M_0)$ | $P(k \mid \mathcal M_1)$ | Ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $k = 5$ | $252/1024 = 0.246$ | $1/11 = 0.091$ | $2.71$ for the fair coin |
| $k = 9$ | $10/1024 = 0.0098$ | $1/11 = 0.091$ | $9.31$ for the biased coin |

Five heads favour the fair coin, although $\mathcal M_1$ contains $f = 0.5$ and fits these data exactly as well. The fair coin predicted this outcome; the flexible model only allowed it.

### What flexibility costs

How much does $\mathcal M_1$ pay for its flexibility? When the posterior is a narrow peak, the evidence integral is about the height of the integrand at its peak times its width. In one dimension, take a uniform prior of width $\Delta\theta$, so that $p(\hat\theta) = 1/\Delta\theta$, and a posterior of standard deviation $\sigma_{\theta \mid X}$. Approximate the peak by a Gaussian bump of height $p(X \mid \hat\theta)\, p(\hat\theta)$ and standard deviation $\sigma_{\theta \mid X}$; a bump of height 1 integrates to $\sqrt{2\pi}\, \sigma_{\theta \mid X}$. This is the [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation#the-laplace-approximation) in one dimension:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(X \mid \mathcal M)
&\approx p(X \mid \hat\theta)\, p(\hat\theta)\, \sqrt{2\pi}\, \sigma_{\theta \mid X} \\
&= p(X \mid \hat\theta) \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\, \sigma_{\theta \mid X}}{\Delta\theta} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first factor is the best-fit likelihood. The second is MacKay's **Occam factor**: the fraction of the prior range that survives the data, at most about 1. A model with many parameters, or with broad priors, pays a factor for each of them.

For 9 heads in 10 under $\mathcal M_1$, with $\hat f = 0.9$ and a prior range of 1, the width comes from the curvature of the log-likelihood $k \log f + (n - k) \log(1 - f)$. Its second derivative at $\hat f = k/n$ is $-k/\hat f^2 - (n-k)/(1 - \hat f)^2 = -n/\big(\hat f(1 - \hat f)\big)$, and the variance is minus its inverse:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| best-fit likelihood $0.9^9 \cdot 0.1$ | $0.0387$ |
| the fair coin's likelihood $2^{-10}$ | $0.00098$ |
| posterior width $\sqrt{\hat f(1 - \hat f)/n}$ | $0.095$ |
| Occam factor $\sqrt{2\pi} \cdot 0.095$ | $0.238$ |
| approximate evidence $0.0387 \cdot 0.238$ | $0.0092$ |
| exact evidence $B(10, 2) = 1/110$ | $0.0091$ |

$\mathcal M_1$ fits forty times better than the fair coin ($0.0387/0.00098 = 39.6$), which has no parameter and pays no Occam factor. A factor of $1/0.238 = 4.2$ of that advantage is paid back for flexibility, leaving a ratio of about $9.4$; the exact evidences give $9.3$. For linear-Gaussian models the decomposition into fit and Occam factor is exact; [Bayesian linear regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#evidence-maximization-for-the-hyperparameters) derives it.

## Bayes factors

The ratio of the evidences of two models is their **Bayes factor**:

$$
K_{10} = \frac{p(X \mid \mathcal M_1)}{p(X \mid \mathcal M_0)} .
$$

Dividing Bayes' formula for $\mathcal M_1$ by the one for $\mathcal M_0$ cancels the common denominator, so the posterior odds are the Bayes factor times the prior odds:

$$
\frac{p(\mathcal M_1 \mid X)}{p(\mathcal M_0 \mid X)} = K_{10} \cdot \frac{p(\mathcal M_1)}{p(\mathcal M_0)} .
$$

Unlike a p-value, a Bayes factor can support the simpler model as well as reject it. [Kass and Raftery (1995)](#references), adapting Jeffreys, read it on this scale:

| $K_{10}$ | $2 \log K_{10}$ | Evidence against $\mathcal M_0$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 3 | 0 to 2 | not worth more than a bare mention |
| 3 to 20 | 2 to 6 | positive |
| 20 to 150 | 6 to 10 | strong |
| above 150 | above 10 | very strong |

Nine heads in ten give $K_{10} = 9.3$, positive evidence for a biased coin. With equal prior odds, $p(\mathcal M_1 \mid X) = 9.3/10.3 = 0.903$. Under $\mathcal M_1$ the next flip is heads with probability $(k + 1)/(n + 2) = 10/12$, the [rule of succession](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-approach#a-coin-with-an-unknown-bias); under $\mathcal M_0$ with $1/2$. Model averaging predicts heads with probability

$$
0.903 \cdot \frac{10}{12} + 0.097 \cdot \frac12 = 0.801 .
$$

Five heads in ten give $K_{01} = 2.71$ for the fair coin, barely worth a mention. Evidence for the simpler hypothesis accumulates slowly: 50 heads in 100 flips give $K_{01} = 8.04$, still only positive. The flexible model is never refuted by data it can also explain. It only loses its Occam factor, which shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$, because the posterior width $\sqrt{\hat f(1 - \hat f)/n}$ does.

When one of the models generated the data, the comparison is right on average. Averaged over datasets from the true model $\mathcal M_1$, the log Bayes factor is, by the definition of the [KL divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#kullbackleibler-divergence),

$$
\mathbb E_{X \sim p(X \mid \mathcal M_1)} \log \frac{p(X \mid \mathcal M_1)}{p(X \mid \mathcal M_0)} = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(\cdot \mid \mathcal M_1) \,\|\, p(\cdot \mid \mathcal M_0)\big) ,
$$

which is never negative ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 3.4). On a single dataset the wrong model can still win.

## The evidence of a linear-Gaussian model

For regression the evidence has a closed form whenever the weights have a Gaussian prior and the noise is Gaussian. Let $\Phi$ be the $N \times (M+1)$ design matrix of a polynomial of degree $M$, with columns $1, x, \dots, x^M$. Write the model as

$$
\begin{aligned}
y &= \Phi w + \varepsilon, \\
w &\sim \mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I), \\
\varepsilon &\sim \mathcal N(0, \beta^{-1} I),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $w$ and $\varepsilon$ independent, $\alpha$ the prior precision of each weight and $\beta$ the noise precision. Then $y$ is an [affine function of Gaussian vectors](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps), hence Gaussian, with mean 0 and covariance $\Phi \operatorname{Cov}(w) \Phi^\top + \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(y \mid \alpha, \beta) &= \mathcal N\big(y \mid 0,\ C\big), \\
C &= \alpha^{-1} \Phi \Phi^\top + \beta^{-1} I .
\end{aligned}
$$

No posterior was needed: the weights were integrated out by the rule for affine maps. Taking the logarithm of the Gaussian density,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(y \mid \alpha, \beta) ={}& \underbrace{-\tfrac12\, y^\top C^{-1} y}_{\text{data fit}} \\
&\underbrace{-\tfrac12 \log \det C}_{\text{complexity}} - \tfrac N2 \log 2\pi .
\end{aligned}
$$

The two terms are the razor in matrix form. Adding a column $v$ to $\Phi$ adds the rank-one term $\alpha^{-1} v v^\top$ to $C$: the model can now produce data in one more direction. The fit term can only improve, because a larger $C$ in the positive semidefinite order has a smaller inverse, so $y^\top C^{-1} y$ cannot grow. And $\det C$ grows by the factor $1 + \alpha^{-1} v^\top C^{-1} v > 1$, by the [determinant lemma](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#6-woodbury-and-the-determinant-lemma): the same unit of probability is spread over a larger volume of datasets. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression) derives the posterior of the weights and an equivalent form of this evidence in weight space.

The evidence jumps by 31 nats between degrees 2 and 3, where the cubic first captures the shape of the sine. The even degree 2 adds almost nothing, because $\sin(\pi x)$ is odd and $x^2$ is even, so on the symmetric grid the new column is orthogonal to the signal, and it is penalized for it. After degree 3 every extra weight improves the fit a little and costs an Occam factor, and the evidence falls slowly. And the evidence ranks models by how probable they are, which is a different question from which one predicts best. The error against the true curve is slightly lower at degrees 5 to 7, because the sine is not a polynomial and more terms approximate it better. The evidence prefers the simplest model that explains the data to within the noise.

## Empirical Bayes: fitting hyperparameters by the evidence

The hyperparameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ were fixed above. A Bayesian could give them a prior too, a **hyperprior**, and integrate them out:

$$
p(y) = \iint p(y \mid \alpha, \beta)\, p(\alpha, \beta)\, d\alpha\, d\beta .
$$

This hierarchical model is rarely tractable. **Empirical Bayes**, or **type-II maximum likelihood**, replaces the integral by its peak: choose the hyperparameters that maximize the evidence,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\hat\alpha, \hat\beta)
&= \arg\max_{\alpha, \beta}\ p(y \mid \alpha, \beta) \\
&= \arg\max_{\alpha, \beta} \int p(y \mid w, \beta)\, p(w \mid \alpha)\, dw ,
\end{aligned}
$$

then use the posterior $p(w \mid y, \hat\alpha, \hat\beta)$. This is maximum likelihood one level up, with the weights integrated out. It is justified when the evidence is sharply peaked in the hyperparameters, which is usual: there are few of them, and all the data inform them.

Why integrate the weights out instead of maximizing over $w$ and $\alpha$ together? The joint density $p(y \mid w, \beta)\, p(w \mid \alpha)$ at $w = 0$ grows without bound as $\alpha \to \infty$, because the prior $\mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$ becomes an ever higher spike at zero: its log-density at the centre is $\frac d2 \log(\alpha / 2\pi)$ for $d$ weights. For the cubic, $d = 4$, it rises by $2 \log 100 = 9.2$ nats for every hundredfold increase of $\alpha$, and has no maximum. Integrating $w$ out is what makes the problem well posed ([Vetrov and Kropotov, 2015](#references), lecture 3).

For the polynomial models the evidence is the Gaussian density of the previous section, a function of two numbers, and a general-purpose optimizer maximizes it:

| Degree | $\hat\alpha$ | $\hat\beta$ | noise sd $1/\sqrt{\hat\beta}$ | log evidence, fixed → fitted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | $0.418$ | $46.0$ | $0.147$ | $-4.18 \to -2.13$ |
| 9 | $0.671$ | $44.4$ | $0.150$ | $-6.77 \to -5.86$ |

Here "fixed" means $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 25$. Fitting the hyperparameters raises both evidences and leaves the cubic ahead. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#evidence-maximization-for-the-hyperparameters) derives a fixed-point iteration that finds the same maximum faster, and reads off from it how many parameters the data actually determine.

Empirical Bayes uses the data twice, to set the prior and then to update it, and so understates uncertainty about the hyperparameters. With many observations per hyperparameter the error is small. With a hyperparameter per weight, as in the [relevance vector machine](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine), it is not, and the evidence can then overfit.

## The BIC approximation

When the evidence has no closed form, it can be approximated by the same peak-times-width argument in $d$ dimensions. The [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation#the-laplace-approximation), later in the course, replaces the integrand by a Gaussian around its maximum $\hat\theta$ and integrates that. With $H$ the Hessian of $-\log\big(p(X \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\big)$ at $\hat\theta$, it gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(X \mid \mathcal M) \approx{}& \log p(X \mid \hat\theta) + \log p(\hat\theta) \\
&+ \frac d2 \log 2\pi - \frac12 \log \det H .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $n$ i.i.d. points the Hessian is a sum of $n$ per-point terms plus the prior's curvature, which does not grow. By the law of large numbers the sum is about $n$ times its expectation, $H \approx n\, I_1(\hat\theta)$, with $I_1$ the [Fisher information](/notes/probability/maximum-likelihood#fisher-information) of one point, so $\log \det H = d \log n + \log \det I_1$. Keeping only the terms that grow with $n$,

$$
\log p(X \mid \mathcal M) = \log p(X \mid \hat\theta) - \frac d2 \log n + O(1) .
$$

Multiplied by $-2$, this is the **Bayesian information criterion**, or Schwarz criterion, smaller being better ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 4.4.1):

$$
\mathrm{BIC} = -2 \log p(X \mid \hat\theta) + d \log n .
$$

The [Machine Learning course](/notes/ml/em-algorithm#how-many-components-bic) uses it to choose the number of mixture components. The derivation shows what it drops: the prior and the curvature, terms of order 1 that do not grow with $n$.

For 9 heads in 10 under $\mathcal M_1$, the three values of the log evidence are:

| | Log evidence |
| --- | --- |
| exact, $\log(1/110)$ | $-4.700$ |
| Laplace | $-4.687$ |
| BIC, $-3.251 - \frac12 \log 10$ | $-4.402$ |

For the polynomials, with $d = M + 2$ (the weights and the noise variance), the BIC also picks degree 3. It needs only the maximum likelihood fit and can be used for any model. It is reliable when $n$ is large compared with $d$ and the model is regular, with a unique interior maximum and a full-rank Hessian; mixtures and neural networks break these conditions. It ignores the prior entirely, which is both its robustness and its limitation.

## Where it breaks: the evidence depends on the prior width

The posterior forgets the prior as data accumulate; the evidence does not. The Occam factor is the ratio of the posterior width to the prior width, so a prior twice as broad halves the evidence of every parameter it governs, however many data there are. The same twenty points, with broader priors on the weights:

| Prior precision $\alpha$ | $M = 1$ | $M = 3$ | $M = 5$ | $M = 9$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | −34.5 | −4.2 | −5.2 | −6.8 |
| $0.01$ | −38.8 | −8.8 | −13.6 | −18.3 |
| $0.0001$ | −43.4 | −18.0 | −27.3 | −38.6 |

The cubic fit hardly changes between these priors, since the data determine its four weights. Once the prior is broad compared with the posterior, each tenfold broadening of its standard deviation costs $\log 10 = 2.3$ nats per weight. From $\alpha = 0.01$ to $0.0001$ the cubic loses $9.2 = 4 \cdot 2.3$ nats and degree 9 loses $20.3$. The gap between degrees 3 and 9 widens from 2.6 nats at $\alpha = 1$ to 20.6.

In the limit of a flat prior, $\alpha \to 0$, the evidence of every model with parameters goes to zero, and the model without parameters always wins. An improper prior, such as a uniform density $c$ on the whole real line, leaves the evidence undefined: the constant $c$ is arbitrary and multiplies the evidence, while it cancels from the posterior, which is often still fine ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 3.4).

Priors used for model comparison must therefore be proper and set on the scale of the problem. Here, inputs scaled to $[-1, 1]$ and outputs of order 1 make $\alpha = 1$ reasonable, and hyperparameters that set the scale are better fitted by empirical Bayes than guessed. Conclusions should be reported with their sensitivity to the prior. When they change, held-out likelihood or cross-validation, which depend on the prior only through the posterior, are the safer criterion.

A related failure shows in the product of predictions. Its first factors are predictions made from the prior alone, so a model with a poor prior that adapts quickly to data can have a low evidence and still generalize well. Scoring only the later predictions, given the first part of the data, removes most of this dependence ([Murphy, 2023](#references), Section 3.8). Computing the evidence is also harder than computing the posterior. Outside conjugate and linear-Gaussian models it needs the Laplace approximation, a variational bound or careful sampling ([Murphy, 2023](#references), Section 3.8.3). One shortcut fails. Bayes' formula gives $\mathbb E_{p(\theta \mid X)}\big[1/p(X \mid \theta)\big] = 1/p(X)$, so averaging $1/p(X \mid \theta)$ over posterior samples seems to estimate the evidence. The average is dominated by rare parameters with a tiny likelihood, which posterior samples almost never reach, and this harmonic-mean estimator can have infinite variance.

## What you should remember

- The evidence $p(X \mid \mathcal M) = \int p(X \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta$ is the likelihood of a model, and $p(\mathcal M \mid X) \propto p(X \mid \mathcal M)\, p(\mathcal M)$.
- It is a distribution over datasets and a product of one-step predictions: flexible models spread their probability thin.
- Evidence ≈ best fit × Occam factor, the posterior width over the prior width per parameter.
- Bayes factors turn prior odds into posterior odds; they can support the simpler model, but slowly.
- Linear-Gaussian: $y \sim \mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} \Phi\Phi^\top + \beta^{-1} I)$; on twenty noisy points of a sine the evidence peaks at degree 3. Empirical Bayes maximizes it over $\alpha$, $\beta$.
- BIC $= -2 \log p(X \mid \hat\theta) + d \log n$ drops the prior. The evidence itself falls with every broadening of the prior.

## Code

The coin's Bayes factors and model average, the Occam factor with the Laplace and BIC approximations, the evidence as a product of predictions, the evidence of polynomial models with its peak, the effect of the prior width, empirical Bayes by a numerical optimizer, and the unbounded joint maximum. Seeds are fixed; the script runs in about a second, and exact numbers can differ in the last digit across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import optimize, special, stats

# Occam's razor on a coin: M0 says the coin is fair, M1 gives its bias a uniform prior.
# The evidence of a sequence with k heads in n flips: 2^-n under M0, B(k + 1, n - k + 1) under M1.
def log_ev_fair(k, n): return -n * np.log(2)
def log_ev_uniform(k, n): return special.betaln(k + 1, n - k + 1)
for k, n in ((5, 10), (9, 10), (50, 100)):
    bf = np.exp(log_ev_uniform(k, n) - log_ev_fair(k, n))      # the Bayes factor of M1 against M0
    print(k, n, round(bf, 3), round(1 / bf, 2))
# 5 10 0.369 2.71 | 9 10 9.309 0.11 | 50 100 0.124 8.04

# model averaging after 9 heads in 10, equal prior odds
p1 = 9.309 / 10.309
print(round(p1, 3), round(p1 * 10 / 12 + (1 - p1) * 0.5, 3))   # 0.903 0.801

# best-fit likelihood x Occam factor, and the Laplace and BIC approximations, for M1 with 9 heads in 10
k, n = 9, 10
th = k / n
log_fit = k * np.log(th) + (n - k) * np.log(1 - th)            # log p(D | theta_hat)
sd = np.sqrt(th * (1 - th) / n)                                 # posterior width from the curvature
print(round(np.exp(log_fit), 4), round(np.sqrt(2 * np.pi) * sd, 3))           # 0.0387 0.238
print(round(log_ev_uniform(k, n), 3), round(log_fit + 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi) + np.log(sd), 3),
      round(log_fit - 0.5 * np.log(n), 3))                      # -4.7 -4.687 -4.402: exact, Laplace, BIC

# the evidence is the product of one-step-ahead predictions
seq = np.array([1] * 9 + [0])
a = b = 1.0
lp = 0.0
for x in seq:
    lp += np.log(a / (a + b) if x else b / (a + b))
    a, b = a + x, b + 1 - x
print(np.isclose(lp, log_ev_uniform(9, 10)))                    # True

# polynomial regression: y = Phi w + noise, w ~ N(0, I / alpha), noise ~ N(0, 1 / beta)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 20
x = np.linspace(-1, 1, N)
y = np.round(np.sin(np.pi * x) + 0.2 * rng.normal(size=N), 3)
x_test = np.linspace(-1, 1, 1001)

def design(x, M): return np.vander(x, M + 1, increasing=True)  # columns 1, x, ..., x^M

def log_evidence(Phi, y, alpha, beta):
    """log N(y | 0, C) with C = Phi Phi^T / alpha + I / beta, by a Cholesky factor."""
    C = Phi @ Phi.T / alpha + np.eye(len(y)) / beta
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(C)
    z = np.linalg.solve(L, y)
    return -0.5 * z @ z - np.log(np.diag(L)).sum() - 0.5 * len(y) * np.log(2 * np.pi)

print('M  train-rms  log-ev  BIC  test-rms')
for M in range(10):
    Phi = design(x, M)
    w = np.linalg.lstsq(Phi, y, rcond=None)[0]                  # maximum likelihood fit
    rss = ((y - Phi @ w) ** 2).sum()
    log_lik = -N / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi * rss / N) - N / 2       # at the ML noise variance rss / N
    bic = -2 * log_lik + (M + 2) * np.log(N)                     # M + 1 weights and the noise variance
    test = np.sqrt(((design(x_test, M) @ w - np.sin(np.pi * x_test)) ** 2).mean())
    print(M, round(np.sqrt(rss / N), 3), round(log_evidence(Phi, y, 1.0, 25.0), 2), round(bic, 2), round(test, 3))

# 1 0.411 -34.45 30.19 0.459
# 2 0.406 -35.41 32.67 0.463
# 3 0.132 -4.18 -9.38 0.147     <- the evidence and BIC peak at degree 3
# 4 0.131 -5.04 -6.48 0.146
# 5 0.121 -5.19 -6.85 0.123
# 6 0.12 -5.74 -4.2 0.123
# 7 0.119 -6.17 -1.42 0.123
# 8 0.116 -6.58 0.42 0.129
# 9 0.116 -6.77 3.38 0.13

# the evidence depends on the prior width: the same models with broader priors on the weights
for alpha in (1.0, 0.01, 0.0001):
    print(alpha, [round(float(log_evidence(design(x, M), y, alpha, 25.0)), 1) for M in (1, 3, 5, 9)])
# 1.0 [-34.5, -4.2, -5.2, -6.8] | 0.01 [-38.8, -8.8, -13.6, -18.3] | 0.0001 [-43.4, -18.0, -27.3, -38.6]

# empirical Bayes: maximize the evidence over log alpha and log beta with a general-purpose optimizer
for M in (3, 9):
    Phi = design(x, M)
    res = optimize.minimize(lambda p: -log_evidence(Phi, y, *np.exp(p)), [0.0, 0.0], method='Nelder-Mead',
                            options=dict(xatol=1e-9, fatol=1e-12, maxiter=4000))
    al, be = np.exp(res.x)
    print(M, round(al, 3), round(be, 2), round(1 / np.sqrt(be), 3), round(-res.fun, 2))
# 3 0.418 46.03 0.147 -2.13
# 9 0.671 44.44 0.15 -5.86

# maximizing the joint p(y, w | alpha) over w and alpha instead has no maximum: at w = 0 it grows like 2 log alpha
Phi = design(x, 3)
for alpha in (1e2, 1e4, 1e6):
    w0 = np.zeros(4)
    print(alpha, round(stats.norm.logpdf(y, Phi @ w0, 0.2).sum() + stats.norm.logpdf(w0, 0, alpha ** -0.5).sum(), 1))
```

## Next

The evidence of a polynomial model was a Gaussian density of the targets, obtained without ever writing down the posterior of the weights. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression) derives that posterior: for a linear model with Gaussian prior and noise, the posterior over the weights is Gaussian, its mean is the ridge solution, and its covariance gives a predictive distribution whose width grows away from the data. It then writes the evidence in weight space, splits it exactly into fit and Occam factor, and turns its maximization into a fixed-point iteration.
