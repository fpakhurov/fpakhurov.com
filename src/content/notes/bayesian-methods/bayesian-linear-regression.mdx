The [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection) compared models by their evidence, the probability of the data with the parameters integrated out, and chose hyperparameters by maximizing it. That integral is rarely available in closed form, but for one model of central importance it is: a linear model with Gaussian noise and a Gaussian prior on its weights. Everything is Gaussian there, so the posterior over the weights, the predictive distribution of a new target and the evidence can all be written down exactly. This note derives the three, using a handful of facts about Gaussians and matrix derivatives that the rest of the course uses again. The posterior mean turns out to be the ridge estimate of the Machine Learning course; what the Bayesian treatment adds is an error bar on every prediction and a way to set the penalty without a validation set.

**Notation.** The data are inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with real targets $t = (t_1, \dots, t_n)^\top$. A fixed feature map $\varphi(x) \in \mathbb R^m$ (for example $1, x, x^2, x^3$) turns each input into a feature vector, and $\Phi \in \mathbb R^{n \times m}$ is the **design matrix** whose $i$-th row is $\varphi(x_i)^\top$. The weights are $w \in \mathbb R^m$. The noise has **precision** (inverse variance) $\beta$ and the prior on the weights precision $\alpha$. $\mathcal N(w \mid \mu, \Sigma)$ is the Gaussian density with mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$, the posterior over the weights is $\mathcal N(w \mid \mu, \Sigma)$, and $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ are the eigenvalues of $\beta\Phi^\top\Phi$. A star marks a new input: $\varphi_* = \varphi(x_*)$.

## Baseline: one weight vector and no error bars

The [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares) fitted $w$ by minimizing $\lVert t - \Phi w\rVert^2$, and the [regularization note](/notes/ml/regularization) added a penalty $\lambda \lVert w\rVert^2$ to make the fit stable. Both return one weight vector $\hat w$ and predict $\hat w^\top \varphi_*$ at a new input. Two things are missing.

- **How sure is the prediction?** A prediction inside a dense cloud of training points and one far outside it are reported with the same confidence. The model knows nothing about where its data were.
- **How strong should the penalty be?** $\lambda$ is chosen by cross-validation: refit on many splits over a grid of values, which costs a factor of the number of folds times the grid size, and wastes part of the data on validation.

The [Bayesian inference note](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#map-estimation-and-regularization) showed that ridge regression is a MAP estimate under a Gaussian prior. Keeping the whole posterior instead of its mode answers the first question, and the evidence of the [model selection note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection) answers the second.

## The model: a Gaussian prior over the weights

The likelihood says that each target is the linear prediction plus independent Gaussian noise; the prior says that the weights are independently Gaussian around zero:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(t \mid w, \beta) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \mathcal N\big(t_i \mid w^\top \varphi(x_i),\ \beta^{-1}\big) = \mathcal N\big(t \mid \Phi w,\ \beta^{-1} I\big), \\
p(w \mid \alpha) &= \mathcal N\big(w \mid 0,\ \alpha^{-1} I\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

The model is linear in $w$, not in $x$: the features can be any fixed functions, polynomials, splines or the Gaussian bumps of the [kernels note](/notes/ml/kernels), and everything below holds unchanged. The two precisions are **hyperparameters**: $\beta^{-1}$ is the noise variance, and $\alpha^{-1}$ is the prior variance of each weight, which says how large we expect the weights to be before seeing the data. They are fixed for now and estimated in the last section.

## Tools: matrix derivatives and Gaussian identities

Six facts carry every derivation of this section of the course. Each is either a standard identity or follows in one line.

**1. Gradients of linear and quadratic forms.** For a vector $b$ and a matrix $A$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_w\, b^\top w &= b, \\
\nabla_w\, w^\top A w &= (A + A^\top)\, w ,
\end{aligned}
$$

which is $2Aw$ for symmetric $A$; the [matrix calculus note](/notes/math/matrix-calculus#linear-and-quadratic-forms) of the Mathematics course derives both. Expanding the squared error, $\nabla_w \lVert t - \Phi w\rVert^2 = \nabla_w\big(t^\top t - 2 w^\top \Phi^\top t + w^\top \Phi^\top\Phi w\big) = 2\Phi^\top(\Phi w - t)$.

**2. Derivatives of a log-determinant.** For a symmetric positive definite matrix $A(\theta)$ that depends on a scalar $\theta$,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log \det A(\theta) = \operatorname{tr}\Big(A^{-1} \frac{\partial A}{\partial \theta}\Big) .
$$

The [log-determinant section](/notes/math/matrix-calculus#the-log-determinant) of the same note derives it for any invertible matrix. As a check in the eigenvalues $a_j$ of $A$, $\log\det A = \sum_j \log a_j$; for $A = \alpha I + M$ with $M$ fixed, the eigenvalues are $\alpha + \lambda_j$ and the derivative in $\alpha$ is $\sum_j 1/(\alpha + \lambda_j) = \operatorname{tr}(\alpha I + M)^{-1}$, the formula with $\partial A/\partial\alpha = I$.

**3. Completing the square.** If a density over $w$ has the logarithm

$$
\log p(w) = -\tfrac12\, w^\top P w + b^\top w + \text{const}
$$

with $P$ symmetric positive definite, then $p(w) = \mathcal N(w \mid P^{-1} b,\ P^{-1})$. The proof is one line: $-\frac12 (w - P^{-1}b)^\top P (w - P^{-1}b) = -\frac12 w^\top P w + b^\top w - \frac12 b^\top P^{-1} b$, and the last term is a constant. The rule to remember: the matrix of the quadratic term is the **precision**, and the mean is the precision's inverse times the linear term.

**4. The Gaussian integral.** For the same $P$ and $b$,

$$
\int_{\mathbb R^m} \exp\Big(-\tfrac12\, w^\top P w + b^\top w\Big)\, dw = (2\pi)^{m/2} \det(P)^{-1/2} \exp\Big(\tfrac12\, b^\top P^{-1} b\Big) ,
$$

because the integrand is $\exp(\frac12 b^\top P^{-1} b)$ times an unnormalized $\mathcal N(P^{-1}b, P^{-1})$, whose normalizing constant is $(2\pi)^{m/2}\det(P)^{-1/2}$ ([the density](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#the-density)).

**5. The linear-Gaussian marginal.** If $w \sim \mathcal N(m_0, S_0)$ and, given $w$, $t = \Phi w + \varepsilon$ with independent noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \beta^{-1} I)$, then

$$
t \sim \mathcal N\big(\Phi m_0,\ \beta^{-1} I + \Phi S_0 \Phi^\top\big) .
$$

$\Phi w$ is an [affine map](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps) of a Gaussian, Gaussian with mean $\Phi m_0$ and covariance $\Phi S_0 \Phi^\top$; adding independent Gaussian noise keeps it Gaussian and adds the covariances.

**6. Woodbury and the determinant lemma.** For an invertible $n \times n$ matrix $A$, an invertible $m \times m$ matrix $C$ and matrices $U \in \mathbb R^{n \times m}$, $V \in \mathbb R^{m \times n}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(A + UCV)^{-1} &= A^{-1} - A^{-1} U \big(C^{-1} + V A^{-1} U\big)^{-1} V A^{-1}, \\
\det(A + UCV) &= \det\big(C^{-1} + V A^{-1} U\big) \det(C) \det(A) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first is checked by multiplying the right side by $A + UCV$ and simplifying to $I$; the second follows from the two block factorizations of $\begin{pmatrix} A & -U \\ V & C^{-1} \end{pmatrix}$. Their use: when $A^{-1}$ is easy (a multiple of $I$, say) and $m \ll n$, they turn the inverse and the determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix into those of an $m \times m$ one, or the other way round.

The [Matrix Cookbook](#references) collects these and many more; facts 3–5 are the Gaussian computations of the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals) written as recipes.

## The posterior over the weights

By Bayes' formula the log-posterior is the log-likelihood plus the log-prior, up to a constant that does not depend on $w$. Expanding both quadratics and collecting the terms in $w$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(w \mid t) &= -\frac{\beta}{2}\lVert t - \Phi w\rVert^2 - \frac{\alpha}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2 + \text{const} \\
&= -\frac{\beta}{2}\big(w^\top \Phi^\top \Phi\, w - 2 w^\top \Phi^\top t\big) - \frac{\alpha}{2}\, w^\top w + \text{const} \\
&= -\frac12\, w^\top \big(\alpha I + \beta \Phi^\top \Phi\big)\, w + w^\top \beta \Phi^\top t + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line drops $\log p(t)$ and the normalizing constants; the second expands $\lVert t - \Phi w\rVert^2$ and drops $\beta t^\top t/2$, which does not contain $w$; the third groups the quadratic and the linear terms. This is the form of fact 3, with precision $P = \alpha I + \beta\Phi^\top\Phi$ and linear term $b = \beta\Phi^\top t$:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p(w \mid t) &= \mathcal N(w \mid \mu, \Sigma), \\
\Sigma &= \big(\alpha I + \beta \Phi^\top \Phi\big)^{-1}, \\
\mu &= \beta\, \Sigma\, \Phi^\top t
\end{aligned}
}
$$

$P$ is positive definite for any $\alpha \gt 0$, because $\Phi^\top\Phi$ is positive semidefinite, so $\Sigma$ exists even with more features than points.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> With a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise, the posterior over the weights is Gaussian, and its precision is the prior precision plus the data precision: $\Sigma^{-1} = \alpha I + \beta\Phi^\top\Phi$. Every data point adds $\beta\, \varphi(x_i)\varphi(x_i)^\top$ to the precision, so the uncertainty shrinks along the directions the data measure and stays at the prior level along the directions they do not.</aside>

The precision being a sum over points has a direct consequence. Processing the data one point at a time, with the posterior after $n$ points used as the prior for point $n + 1$, gives $\Sigma_{n+1}^{-1} = \Sigma_n^{-1} + \beta\, \varphi(x_{n+1}) \varphi(x_{n+1})^\top$ and ends at the same posterior as processing them all at once. Since the added matrix is positive semidefinite, $\Sigma_{n+1} \preceq \Sigma_n$: a new point can only reduce the uncertainty about the weights, in every direction.

**Worked example.** Fit a line, $\varphi(x) = (1, x)$, through three points $x = (-1, 0, 1)$, $t = (-1, 0.5, 2)$, with $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta = 4$ (noise standard deviation $0.5$). The inputs are centred, so

$$
\Phi^\top \Phi = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Phi^\top t = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 4 \cdot 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 + 4 \cdot 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 13 & 0 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The posterior covariance is $\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1/13, 1/9)$ and the mean $\mu = 4\,\Sigma\, (1.5, 3)^\top = (6/13,\ 12/9) = (0.462,\ 1.333)$. Least squares gives $(1.5/3,\ 3/2) = (0.5,\ 1.5)$: the posterior mean shrinks the intercept by the factor $12/13$ and the slope by $8/9$, more where the data carry less precision. The posterior standard deviations are $1/\sqrt{13} = 0.277$ for the intercept and $1/3$ for the slope.

1. The prior contributes the precision $\alpha I$, the same in every direction.
2. The data contribute $\beta\Phi^\top\Phi$; the two precisions add to $\Sigma^{-1}$.
3. The posterior mean is $\Sigma$ applied to the data term $\beta\Phi^\top t$.
4. At a new input the prediction has a mean and a variance, computed in the next sections.

## The posterior mean is the ridge estimate

The mode of a Gaussian is its mean, so the MAP estimate and the posterior mean coincide. Dividing the precision and the linear term by $\beta$,

$$
\mu = \big(\Phi^\top \Phi + \tfrac{\alpha}{\beta} I\big)^{-1} \Phi^\top t ,
$$

the [ridge estimate](/notes/ml/regularization#ridge-a-penalty-on-the-size-of-the-weights) with penalty $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$: the noise variance divided by the prior variance, as in the [MAP section](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#map-estimation-and-regularization) of the probability course. In the worked example, $\lambda = 1/4$ and ridge gives $(1.5/3.25,\ 3/2.25) = (0.462,\ 1.333)$, the same vector. A noisier problem (smaller $\beta$) or a tighter prior (larger $\alpha$) is a stronger penalty.

What the Bayesian reading adds is everything except the mean. The covariance $\Sigma$, which the [Bayesian reading of ridge](/notes/ml/regularization#the-bayesian-reading) mentions in passing, gives an error bar for every weight and, below, for every prediction. And the ridge shrinkage factors $\sigma_j^2/(\sigma_j^2 + \lambda)$ of the [regularization note](/notes/ml/regularization#which-directions-it-shrinks), whose sum is the effective degrees of freedom, reappear in the evidence as $\lambda_j/(\alpha + \lambda_j)$, now with a formula for the best $\alpha$.

## The predictive distribution

A new target is $t_* = w^\top \varphi_* + \varepsilon_*$, with $w$ distributed as the posterior and fresh noise $\varepsilon_* \sim \mathcal N(0, \beta^{-1})$ independent of it. Fact 5 with the single row $\varphi_*^\top$ in place of $\Phi$, mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$ gives the **predictive distribution**:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p(t_* \mid x_*, t) &= \int \mathcal N\big(t_* \mid w^\top\varphi_*,\ \beta^{-1}\big)\, \mathcal N(w \mid \mu, \Sigma)\, dw = \mathcal N\big(t_* \mid \mu^\top \varphi_*,\ \sigma_*^2\big), \\
\sigma_*^2 &= \frac{1}{\beta} + \varphi_*^\top \Sigma\, \varphi_*
\end{aligned}
}
$$

The variance has two parts. $1/\beta$ is the noise in the new target, which no amount of data removes. $\varphi_*^\top\Sigma\varphi_*$ is the uncertainty about the weights, seen through the feature vector of the new input.

**Why the uncertainty grows away from the data.** $\Sigma$ has the same eigenvectors $u_j$ as $\beta\Phi^\top\Phi$, with eigenvalues $1/(\alpha + \lambda_j)$, so

$$
\varphi_*^\top \Sigma\, \varphi_* = \sum_{j=1}^m \frac{(u_j^\top \varphi_*)^2}{\alpha + \lambda_j} .
$$

$\lambda_j = \beta \sum_i (u_j^\top \varphi(x_i))^2$ measures how much the training inputs vary along $u_j$. A new input whose feature vector lies along well-measured directions (large $\lambda_j$) costs little; components along poorly measured directions are divided by little more than $\alpha$. For the line with centred inputs the eigenvectors are the axes, and

$$
\varphi_*^\top \Sigma\, \varphi_* = \frac{1}{\alpha + \beta n} + \frac{x_*^2}{\alpha + \beta \sum_i x_i^2} ,
$$

a parabola in $x_*$ with its minimum at the centre of the data: the error in the slope is multiplied by the distance from the centre. In the worked example the predictive means at $x_* = 0, 1, 3$ are $0.462$, $1.795$, $4.462$, and the variances $0.25 + 1/13 + x_*^2/9$ are $0.327$, $0.438$ and $1.327$. At $x_* = 3$, three times as far from the centre as the farthest training point, the weight uncertainty is $1/13 + 1 = 1.077$, more than four times the noise variance.

For polynomial features the same happens faster, because the high powers of $x_*$ grow quickly outside the range of the data. The figure fits a cubic to the first 2, 8 and 32 of 32 noisy points:

<PredictiveBand />

With two points on the left the band is narrow near them and wide everywhere else; with 32 points it is narrow across the data and opens up beyond their ends. Two limits are worth stating. As the data grow, $\varphi_*^\top\Sigma\varphi_*$ falls towards zero wherever the data are dense, and $\sigma_*^2$ falls to the noise floor $1/\beta$, not to zero. And the growth away from the data is a property of the features: for **localized** features, Gaussian bumps centred on the training inputs, $\varphi_* \to 0$ far from every centre, so $\varphi_*^\top\Sigma\varphi_* \to 0$ and the model becomes most confident exactly where it knows least. The [relevance vector machine](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine) inherits this flaw, and [Gaussian processes](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes) remove it.

## Evidence maximization for the hyperparameters

The evidence, or marginal likelihood, is the probability of the targets with the weights integrated out:

$$
p(t \mid \alpha, \beta) = \int p(t \mid w, \beta)\, p(w \mid \alpha)\, dw .
$$

It is a function of the hyperparameters only, and choosing them by maximizing it is **type-II maximum likelihood**, or [empirical Bayes](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection#empirical-bayes-fitting-hyperparameters-by-the-evidence): maximum likelihood one level up, with the weights integrated out instead of fitted. The model selection note explains why this does not overfit the way maximizing over $w$ would, and states the fixed point that maximizes it. This section derives the weight-space form of the evidence that the fixed point comes from, and then the fixed point itself.

**The evidence in two forms.** Fact 5 with the prior mean $0$ and covariance $\alpha^{-1} I$ gives it directly as a Gaussian over $t$, the [form of the model selection note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection#the-evidence-of-a-linear-gaussian-model):

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(t \mid \alpha, \beta) &= \mathcal N\big(t \mid 0,\ C\big), \\
C &= \beta^{-1} I + \alpha^{-1} \Phi \Phi^\top .
\end{aligned}
$$

This form needs the inverse and the determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix. A second form needs only $m \times m$ ones. Write the integrand as $(\beta/2\pi)^{n/2} (\alpha/2\pi)^{m/2} e^{-E(w)}$ with

$$
E(w) = \frac{\beta}{2}\lVert t - \Phi w\rVert^2 + \frac{\alpha}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2 .
$$

$E$ is quadratic in $w$, its gradient vanishes at the posterior mean $\mu$, and its Hessian is $\alpha I + \beta\Phi^\top\Phi = \Sigma^{-1}$, so its Taylor expansion around $\mu$ is exact: $E(w) = E(\mu) + \frac12 (w - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (w - \mu)$. The remaining integral is fact 4, $\int e^{-\frac12 (w-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(w-\mu)}\, dw = (2\pi)^{m/2}\det(\Sigma)^{1/2}$. Taking the logarithm,

$$
\boxed{\; \log p(t \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac{m}{2}\log\alpha + \frac{n}{2}\log\beta - E(\mu) - \frac12 \log\det\Sigma^{-1} - \frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi \;}
$$

**Fit times an Occam factor.** Regrouping the boxed formula,

$$
\log p(t \mid \alpha, \beta) = \underbrace{\log p(t \mid \mu, \beta)}_{\text{fit}} + \underbrace{\log\Big[p(\mu \mid \alpha)\, (2\pi)^{m/2} \det(\Sigma)^{1/2}\Big]}_{\text{Occam factor}} ,
$$

which is checked by writing out the two Gaussian densities. The first term is the best fit the model achieves. The second multiplies the prior density at the posterior mean by the volume of the posterior: it is the fraction of the prior's probability that lands on the weights the data allow, and it is small when the prior is much broader than the posterior ([MacKay, 2003](#references), Chapter 28). A very broad prior (small $\alpha$) does not improve the fit any further but makes the Occam factor tiny, and the evidence prefers the prior that is just broad enough.

**The fixed point.** Differentiate the boxed formula with respect to $\alpha$. $\mu$ depends on $\alpha$, but $E$ is minimized at $\mu$, so its derivative through $\mu$ vanishes and only the explicit $\frac\alpha2\lVert\mu\rVert^2$ counts; fact 2 gives $\partial_\alpha \log\det\Sigma^{-1} = \operatorname{tr}\Sigma = \sum_j 1/(\alpha + \lambda_j)$. Setting the derivative to zero and multiplying by $2\alpha$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{m}{2\alpha} - \frac12\lVert\mu\rVert^2 - \frac12 \sum_{j} \frac{1}{\alpha + \lambda_j} &= 0 \\
\alpha\lVert\mu\rVert^2 &= m - \sum_j \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \lambda_j} = \sum_j \frac{\lambda_j}{\alpha + \lambda_j} = \gamma .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $\beta$, the explicit dependence is in $\frac n2\log\beta$, in $\frac\beta2\lVert t - \Phi\mu\rVert^2$, and in the eigenvalues $\lambda_j$, which are proportional to $\beta$, so $\partial_\beta \lambda_j = \lambda_j/\beta$ and $\partial_\beta \log\det\Sigma^{-1} = \sum_j \lambda_j / \big(\beta(\alpha + \lambda_j)\big) = \gamma/\beta$. Setting the derivative to zero gives $n/\beta - \lVert t - \Phi\mu\rVert^2 - \gamma/\beta = 0$. Together, the fixed point of [MacKay (1992a)](#references):

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\gamma &= \sum_{j=1}^m \frac{\lambda_j}{\alpha + \lambda_j}, \\
\alpha &= \frac{\gamma}{\lVert\mu\rVert^2}, \\
\frac{1}{\beta} &= \frac{\lVert t - \Phi\mu\rVert^2}{n - \gamma}
\end{aligned}
}
$$

These are not closed-form solutions, because $\mu$ and $\gamma$ depend on $\alpha$ and $\beta$. They are iterated: compute $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ for the current values, then $\gamma$, then new $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and repeat. The eigenvalues of $\Phi^\top\Phi$ are computed once and rescaled by $\beta$.

The quantity $\gamma$ is the **effective number of parameters**. Each term $\lambda_j/(\alpha + \lambda_j)$ lies between 0 and 1: close to 1 when the data determine the weight along $u_j$ ($\lambda_j \gg \alpha$), close to 0 when the prior does. The noise estimate divides the residual sum of squares by $n - \gamma$, just as the unbiased variance estimate of least squares divides by $n - m$: $\gamma$ degrees of freedom were spent on fitting. The prior variance $1/\alpha = \lVert\mu\rVert^2/\gamma$ is the mean squared weight over the well-determined directions.

**Worked example.** For the 32 points and cubic features of the figure, starting from $\alpha = \beta = 1$, the iteration converges to $\alpha = 0.555$, $\beta = 22.29$ and $\gamma = 3.945$: almost all four parameters are well determined. The estimated noise standard deviation is $1/\sqrt{22.29} = 0.212$, against the true $0.2$. The log evidence rises from $-6.92$ at the values $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 25$ to $-6.43$ at the maximum. scikit-learn's `BayesianRidge`, which runs the same iteration (with nearly flat gamma priors on the two precisions), returns the same values.

<EvidenceCurve />

The same maximum can be found with the [EM algorithm](/notes/ml/em-algorithm), treating $w$ as a latent variable: its E-step is the posterior above and its M-step gives $\alpha = m / (\lVert\mu\rVert^2 + \operatorname{tr}\Sigma)$. At a stationary point this is the same equation as $\alpha\lVert\mu\rVert^2 = \gamma$, since $\gamma = m - \alpha\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$. EM guarantees that the evidence never decreases; MacKay's form has no such guarantee but was observed to converge much faster ([Tipping, 2001](#references), Appendix A). The note on [EM and Bayesian PCA](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca) returns to this choice.

### Why the two forms of the evidence agree

*Optional depth: the matrix identities behind the two forms. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

The determinant lemma, applied to $C = \beta^{-1} I + \Phi\, (\alpha^{-1} I)\, \Phi^\top$ with $A = \beta^{-1} I$, $U = \Phi$, $V = \Phi^\top$ and $\alpha^{-1} I$ as its middle matrix, gives $\det C = \det(\alpha I + \beta\Phi^\top\Phi)\, \alpha^{-m} \beta^{-n}$, which matches the terms $\frac m2 \log\alpha + \frac n2\log\beta - \frac12\log\det\Sigma^{-1}$. Woodbury gives $C^{-1} = \beta I - \beta^2 \Phi\Sigma\Phi^\top$, and with $\beta\Phi^\top t = \Sigma^{-1}\mu$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
t^\top C^{-1} t &= \beta\, t^\top t - \mu^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mu , \\
2E(\mu) &= \beta\, t^\top t - 2\mu^\top \beta \Phi^\top t + \mu^\top (\beta \Phi^\top\Phi + \alpha I)\, \mu = \beta\, t^\top t - \mu^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu ,
\end{aligned}
$$

so the quadratic forms match as well. The Code section checks both forms numerically.

## What changed

| | Least squares and ridge | Bayesian linear regression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The weights | one vector $\hat w$ | a Gaussian $\mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$ |
| The point estimate | $(\Phi^\top\Phi + \lambda I)^{-1}\Phi^\top t$ | the same vector, with $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$ |
| A prediction | $\hat w^\top\varphi_*$ | $\mathcal N\big(\mu^\top\varphi_*,\ 1/\beta + \varphi_*^\top\Sigma\varphi_*\big)$ |
| Far from the data | as confident as near it | wider, for features that grow away from the data |
| The penalty $\lambda$ | cross-validation over a grid | evidence maximization: $\alpha = \gamma/\lVert\mu\rVert^2$ |
| The noise level | a separate estimate | $1/\beta = \lVert t - \Phi\mu\rVert^2/(n - \gamma)$, from the same evidence |
| Degrees of freedom | $\sum_j \sigma_j^2/(\sigma_j^2 + \lambda)$ | $\gamma = \sum_j \lambda_j/(\alpha + \lambda_j)$, the same sum |

## Limitations

- **Gaussian noise and a linear model.** The closed forms need both. Heavy-tailed noise, outliers or classification targets break conjugacy, and the [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) or the methods of the next section are needed.
- **Fixed features.** The features are chosen before seeing the data. With localized features the predictive variance falls back to the noise level far from the data, the opposite of what it should do.
- **Point estimates of the hyperparameters.** Type-II maximum likelihood plugs in the best $\alpha$ and $\beta$ instead of integrating over them. With two hyperparameters and many data points this costs little; with one hyperparameter per weight, as in the next note, it can overfit the evidence.
- **One precision for all weights.** The prior $\alpha^{-1} I$ shrinks every weight by the same rule and keeps all of them. It cannot say that some features are irrelevant.
- **Cost.** The posterior needs the inverse of an $m \times m$ matrix, $O(m^3)$, and the predictive variance costs $O(m^2)$ per new input; with $m$ in the tens of thousands this is expensive.

## What you should remember

- Prior $\mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$ and noise precision $\beta$ give the posterior $\mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma^{-1} = \alpha I + \beta\Phi^\top\Phi$ and $\mu = \beta\Sigma\Phi^\top t$: precisions add.
- Completing the square: the quadratic term of a log-density is minus half the precision, and the mean is the precision's inverse times the linear term.
- The posterior mean is the ridge estimate with $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$.
- The predictive distribution is $\mathcal N\big(\mu^\top\varphi_*,\ 1/\beta + \varphi_*^\top\Sigma\varphi_*\big)$: noise plus weight uncertainty. The second part is small along directions the data measure, grows away from the data for polynomial features, and never grows when data are added.
- The evidence is $\mathcal N(t \mid 0, \beta^{-1} I + \alpha^{-1}\Phi\Phi^\top)$; its logarithm is the fit at $\mu$ plus an Occam factor.
- Evidence maximization: $\gamma = \sum_j \lambda_j/(\alpha + \lambda_j)$, $\alpha = \gamma/\lVert\mu\rVert^2$, $1/\beta = \lVert t - \Phi\mu\rVert^2/(n - \gamma)$, iterated to a fixed point; $\gamma$ is the effective number of parameters.

## Code

The worked example, the agreement with ridge, the predictive standard deviation of the cubic model as the data grow, the two forms of the evidence, and MacKay's fixed point against scikit-learn's `BayesianRidge`. The script runs in about a second; exact numbers can differ in the last digit across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal
from sklearn.linear_model import BayesianRidge

def posterior(Phi, t, alpha, beta):
    """Posterior N(mu, S) of the weights under the prior N(0, I/alpha) and noise precision beta."""
    S = np.linalg.inv(alpha * np.eye(Phi.shape[1]) + beta * Phi.T @ Phi)
    return beta * S @ Phi.T @ t, S

def predictive(phi, mu, S, beta):
    """Predictive mean and variance at the feature vectors in the rows of phi."""
    return phi @ mu, 1 / beta + np.einsum('ij,jk,ik->i', phi, S, phi)

def log_evidence(Phi, t, alpha, beta):
    n, m = Phi.shape
    mu, S = posterior(Phi, t, alpha, beta)
    E = beta / 2 * np.sum((t - Phi @ mu) ** 2) + alpha / 2 * mu @ mu
    return m / 2 * np.log(alpha) + n / 2 * np.log(beta) - E + 0.5 * np.linalg.slogdet(S)[1] - n / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi)

# the worked example: a line through three points, alpha = 1, beta = 4
Phi = np.array([[1, -1], [1, 0], [1, 1.0]])
t = np.array([-1, 0.5, 2.0])
mu, S = posterior(Phi, t, alpha=1.0, beta=4.0)
print(mu.round(4), np.diag(S).round(4))                                      # [0.4615 1.3333] [0.0769 0.1111]
print(np.linalg.solve(Phi.T @ Phi + 0.25 * np.eye(2), Phi.T @ t).round(4))   # [0.4615 1.3333]: ridge with lambda = alpha/beta
m, v = predictive(np.array([[1, 0], [1, 1], [1, 3.0]]), mu, S, beta=4.0)
print(m.round(3), v.round(3))                                                # [0.462 1.795 4.462] [0.327 0.438 1.327]

# 32 noisy points of sin(2.5 x), cubic features, alpha = 1 and the true noise precision beta = 25
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, 32).round(3)
t = (np.sin(2.5 * x) + 0.2 * rng.normal(size=32)).round(3)
feat = lambda x: np.vander(np.atleast_1d(x), 4, increasing=True)
grid = np.array([-1.5, -1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 1.5])
for n in (2, 8, 32):                       # predictive standard deviation at x = -1.5, -1, 0, 1, 1.5
    mu, S = posterior(feat(x[:n]), t[:n], 1.0, 25.0)
    print(n, np.sqrt(predictive(feat(grid), mu, S, 25.0)[1]).round(3))
# 2 [1.786 0.435 0.488 1.831 4.163] | 8 [1.585 0.364 0.228 0.747 2.501] | 32 [0.594 0.234 0.206 0.255 0.668]

# the evidence: the m x m form against the Gaussian N(0, I/beta + Phi Phi^T/alpha) over t
Phi = feat(x)
C = np.eye(32) / 25.0 + Phi @ Phi.T / 1.0
print(round(log_evidence(Phi, t, 1.0, 25.0), 4), round(multivariate_normal(np.zeros(32), C).logpdf(t), 4))   # -6.9188 -6.9188

# MacKay's fixed point for alpha and beta
alpha, beta = 1.0, 1.0
eig = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Phi.T @ Phi)
for _ in range(200):
    mu, S = posterior(Phi, t, alpha, beta)
    lam = beta * eig
    gamma = np.sum(lam / (lam + alpha))
    alpha, beta = gamma / (mu @ mu), (32 - gamma) / np.sum((t - Phi @ mu) ** 2)
print(round(alpha, 4), round(beta, 3), round(gamma, 3), round(log_evidence(Phi, t, alpha, beta), 4))   # 0.5551 22.294 3.945 -6.4286
br = BayesianRidge(fit_intercept=False, tol=1e-10, max_iter=1000).fit(Phi, t)
print(round(br.lambda_, 4), round(br.alpha_, 3))   # 0.5551 22.294: scikit-learn's lambda_ is the weight precision, alpha_ the noise precision
```

## Next

One precision $\alpha$ for all weights shrinks them all alike and keeps every feature in the model. Giving each weight its own precision, and maximizing the evidence over all of them, has a surprising effect: most precisions go to infinity, their weights are pinned at zero, and the model keeps only a few features. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine) derives this **automatic relevance determination** and applies it to kernel features, where it gives the relevance vector machine, a sparse alternative to the support vector machine with predictive variances.
