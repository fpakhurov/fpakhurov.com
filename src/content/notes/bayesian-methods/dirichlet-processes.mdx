The [Gaussian process](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes) let a regression function become as complex as the data required, with one coefficient per point instead of a fixed basis. Clustering needs the same freedom. A mixture model fixes the number of components $K$ before seeing the data, and the usual remedy, fitting several values of $K$ and comparing them by BIC or by the evidence, treats a quantity we are uncertain about as a choice made from outside. This note puts a prior on mixtures with infinitely many components. Any finite dataset uses only finitely many of them, and the number it uses is inferred together with everything else, growing slowly as more data arrive. The prior is the **Dirichlet process**; its two constructions, the Chinese restaurant and stick-breaking, give a sampler and a variational method.

**Notation.** $n$ data points $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with cluster labels $z_1, \dots, z_n$. $n_k$ is the number of points in cluster $k$, $K_n$ the number of occupied clusters, and a subscript $-i$ means "without point $i$". $\mathrm{Dir}(a_1, \dots, a_K)$ is the Dirichlet distribution on the simplex of probability vectors $\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K)$, with density proportional to $\prod_k \pi_k^{a_k - 1}$. $\alpha \gt 0$ is the **concentration parameter**, $H$ the **base distribution** of the cluster parameters $\theta_k$, $F(x \mid \theta)$ the distribution of a point in a cluster with parameter $\theta$, and $\delta_\theta$ a point mass at $\theta$. $\Gamma$ is the gamma function and $\psi$ its logarithmic derivative, the digamma function.

## Baseline: a Bayesian mixture with $K$ chosen in advance \{#baseline-a-bayesian-mixture-with-k-chosen-in-advance\}

A finite Bayesian mixture draws the weights from a symmetric Dirichlet distribution and the cluster parameters from $H$, then generates each point from one cluster:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi &\sim \mathrm{Dir}\big(\tfrac{\alpha}{K}, \dots, \tfrac{\alpha}{K}\big), \qquad \theta_k \sim H, \\
z_i &\sim \mathrm{Categorical}(\pi), \\
x_i &\sim F(\theta_{z_i}) .
\end{aligned}
$$

It is the [Gaussian mixture of the EM note](/notes/ml/em-algorithm) with priors on its parameters, and [Gibbs sampling](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling) or variational inference fits it. Two properties of the Dirichlet distribution, both from the [conjugate priors note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#dirichlet-categorical), do the work. It is conjugate to the categorical distribution: after observing labels with counts $n_1, \dots, n_K$, the weights are $\mathrm{Dir}(a_1 + n_1, \dots, a_K + n_K)$. And it has a closed-form predictive: integrating $\pi$ out,

$$
p(z_{n+1} = k \mid z_1, \dots, z_n) = \frac{n_k + a_k}{n + \sum_j a_j} = \frac{n_k + \alpha/K}{n + \alpha} ,
$$

the posterior mean of $\pi_k$, the same pseudo-count rule as the beta–binomial update of the [Bayesian inference note](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#conjugate-priors-the-beta-binomial).

The model still needs $K$. Choosing it means fitting the model for several values and comparing them, by [BIC](/notes/ml/em-algorithm#how-many-components-bic) or by the evidence of [Bayesian model selection](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection); each comparison is a separate fit, the answer is a single $K$ with no uncertainty attached, and when more data arrive, perhaps containing a cluster never seen before, the whole procedure starts again.

## From the Dirichlet distribution to the Dirichlet process

Let $K$ grow while the total concentration $\alpha$ stays fixed. Suppose the first $n$ labels occupy $K_n$ clusters. Each occupied cluster keeps its predictive probability, and the $K - K_n$ empty ones share the rest:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(z_{n+1} = k \mid z_{1:n}) &= \frac{n_k + \alpha/K}{n + \alpha} \;\to\; \frac{n_k}{n + \alpha}, \\
p(z_{n+1} \text{ is a new cluster} \mid z_{1:n}) &= \frac{(K - K_n)\, \alpha/K}{n + \alpha} \;\to\; \frac{\alpha}{n + \alpha} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The limit is a well-defined prior on partitions of the data that never mentions $K$: an occupied cluster attracts the next point in proportion to its size, and a new cluster is opened with probability proportional to $\alpha$ ([Neal, 2000](#references); [Rasmussen, 2000](#references)). The labels themselves have no meaning in the limit, only which points share a cluster.

What object is the limit of the weights? Not a vector, since there are infinitely many components, but a random **distribution**. Attach to each component its parameter $\theta_k \sim H$, and consider the random measure $G = \sum_k \pi_k \delta_{\theta_k}$, which puts mass $\pi_k$ at $\theta_k$. **Ferguson (1973)** characterized its law directly: $G$ follows a **Dirichlet process**, $G \sim \mathrm{DP}(\alpha, H)$, if for every partition of the parameter space into measurable sets $A_1, \dots, A_r$,

$$
\big(G(A_1), \dots, G(A_r)\big) \sim \mathrm{Dir}\big(\alpha H(A_1), \dots, \alpha H(A_r)\big) .
$$

The definition is consistent because of the **aggregation property** of the Dirichlet distribution: merging two components of a Dirichlet vector gives a Dirichlet vector whose parameter is the sum of the two, so refining or coarsening the partition never contradicts itself. It gives the first two moments at once, from those of a Dirichlet:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[G(A)] &= H(A), \\
\operatorname{var}[G(A)] &= \frac{H(A)\,\big(1 - H(A)\big)}{\alpha + 1} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The base distribution $H$ is the mean of the random distribution, and $\alpha$ says how tightly $G$ concentrates around it. **Worked example.** For a set with $H(A) = 0.5$, the mass that $G$ gives it has standard deviation $\sqrt{0.25/2} = 0.354$ when $\alpha = 1$, and $\sqrt{0.25/100} = 0.05$ when $\alpha = 99$. Even with a large $\alpha$, $G$ is discrete with probability one: it is a countable sum of point masses, however smooth $H$ is ([Ferguson, 1973](#references)). Discreteness is what makes it a clustering prior: draws $\theta_1, \theta_2, \dots$ from $G$ repeat values, and points with the same value form a cluster.

### The posterior of $G$ \{#the-posterior-of-g\}

*Optional depth: the conjugacy of the Dirichlet process itself. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

The Dirichlet process is conjugate to sampling from it. Given $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n \sim G$ independently, the counts of the $\theta_i$ in the sets $A_1, \dots, A_r$ update the Dirichlet distribution of $(G(A_1), \dots, G(A_r))$ as above, for every partition at once, so

$$
G \mid \theta_1, \dots, \theta_n \;\sim\; \mathrm{DP}\Big(\alpha + n,\ \frac{\alpha H + \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{\theta_i}}{\alpha + n}\Big) .
$$

The posterior base distribution is a mixture of the prior $H$, with weight $\alpha/(\alpha + n)$, and the empirical distribution of the observations, with weight $n/(\alpha + n)$. Taking its mean gives the predictive distribution of the next draw, the **Pólya urn** of [Blackwell and MacQueen (1973)](#references): $\theta_{n+1}$ equals one of the previous values $\theta_i$ with probability $1/(\alpha + n)$ each, or is a fresh draw from $H$ with probability $\alpha/(\alpha + n)$. Grouping equal values, this is the limit rule above.

## The Chinese restaurant process

The limit rule has a picture ([Aldous, 1985](#references)). Customers enter a restaurant with infinitely many tables one at a time. The first sits at the first table. Customer $n + 1$ sits at an occupied table $k$ with probability $n_k / (n + \alpha)$, or at a new table with probability $\alpha / (n + \alpha)$:

<CrpTables />

Tables are clusters and customers are data points. A large table attracts more customers, a **rich-get-richer** effect that produces a few large clusters and a tail of small ones. Multiplying the seating probabilities of all $n$ customers gives the probability of a partition into $K$ tables of sizes $n_1, \dots, n_K$. The numerators contribute $\alpha$ for every table opened and $1, 2, \dots, n_k - 1$ for the later customers of table $k$; the denominators are $\alpha, \alpha + 1, \dots, \alpha + n - 1$ whatever happens. So

$$
p(z_1, \dots, z_n) = \frac{\alpha^K\, \Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha + n)} \prod_{k=1}^K (n_k - 1)! \,,
$$

which depends only on the table sizes, not on the order in which the customers arrived. The process is **exchangeable**: any customer can be treated as the last to arrive, the property the Gibbs sampler below relies on. **Worked example.** With $\alpha = 1$, customers 1, 2 and 4 at one table and customer 3 alone: the sequential probabilities are $1 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac13 \cdot \frac24 = \frac1{12}$, and the formula gives $\frac{1 \cdot \Gamma(1)}{\Gamma(5)} \cdot 2! \cdot 0! = \frac{2}{24} = \frac1{12}$.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Let the number of mixture components go to infinity while the Dirichlet prior keeps a fixed total concentration $\alpha$. The weights become a random discrete distribution, the Dirichlet process; the labels become a random partition, the Chinese restaurant process. Only finitely many clusters are ever occupied, their number grows like $\alpha \log n$, and inference never needs to choose $K$.</aside>

## How many clusters

Customer $i$ opens a new table with probability $\alpha / (\alpha + i - 1)$, whatever happened before. The number of tables $K_n$ is a sum of these independent indicators, so

$$
\mathbb E[K_n] = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + i - 1} = \alpha\big(\psi(\alpha + n) - \psi(\alpha)\big) \approx \alpha \int_0^n \frac{dt}{\alpha + t} = \alpha \log\Big(1 + \frac n\alpha\Big)
$$

([Antoniak, 1974](#references)). The middle form uses $\psi(x + 1) = \psi(x) + 1/x$; the approximation replaces the sum by an integral, and since each term exceeds the integral over the interval that follows it, the approximation is a little low, by less than 1 (by 0.58 for $\alpha = 1$ and 0.51 for $\alpha = 5$ at $n = 1000$). For $n \gg \alpha$, $\mathbb E[K_n] \approx \alpha \log n$: the number of clusters grows without bound, but only logarithmically, so doubling the data adds about $\alpha \log 2$ clusters.

**Worked example.** With $\alpha = 1$ and $n = 100$, $\mathbb E[K_n]$ is the harmonic number $H_{100} = 5.187$, against the approximation $\log 101 = 4.615$. With $\alpha = 5$ and $n = 1000$ it is $27.03$, against $26.52$. In 200 simulated restaurants, the Code section finds averages of $5.23$ and $26.83$ tables.

<ExpectedClusters />

The concentration therefore sets the prior number of clusters, and the data can revise it. In practice $\alpha$ is either chosen from the expected number of clusters, by inverting the formula above, or given a gamma prior and sampled along with everything else ([Escobar and West, 1995](#references)).

## Stick-breaking

The restaurant describes the partition with $G$ integrated out. **Sethuraman (1994)** constructed $G$ itself. Break a stick of unit length: take a fraction $v_1$ of it as the first weight, a fraction $v_2$ of what remains as the second, and so on,

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_k &\sim \mathrm{Beta}(1, \alpha), \\
\pi_k &= v_k \prod_{j \lt k} (1 - v_j), \qquad \theta_k \sim H, \\
G &= \sum_{k=1}^\infty \pi_k\, \delta_{\theta_k} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

all independent. Then $G \sim \mathrm{DP}(\alpha, H)$. Since $\mathbb E[v_k] = 1/(1 + \alpha)$ and the $v_k$ are independent,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[\pi_k] &= \frac{1}{1 + \alpha}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{1 + \alpha}\Big)^{k - 1}, \\
\mathbb E\Big[1 - \sum_{k=1}^{T} \pi_k\Big] &= \Big(\frac{\alpha}{1 + \alpha}\Big)^{T} :
\end{aligned}
$$

the weights decay geometrically on average. **Worked example.** With $\alpha = 1$ the expected first three weights are $0.5, 0.25, 0.125$; with $\alpha = 5$ they are $0.167, 0.139, 0.116$, and the mass left after $T = 20$ sticks is $(5/6)^{20} = 0.026$ on average. A small $\alpha$ breaks off large pieces first, a large one many small pieces:

<StickBreaking />

The expected leftover mass is what makes **truncation** safe: replacing the infinite sum by $T$ sticks, with the last one taking the rest, changes the model by an amount that shrinks geometrically in $T$ ([Ishwaran and James, 2001](#references)). Truncated stick-breaking is the basis of variational inference for Dirichlet process mixtures ([Blei and Jordan, 2006](#references)).

## Dirichlet process mixtures

A **Dirichlet process mixture** uses $G$ as the mixing distribution of a mixture model:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G &\sim \mathrm{DP}(\alpha, H), \qquad \theta_i \mid G \sim G, \\
x_i \mid \theta_i &\sim F(\theta_i) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $G$ is discrete, the $\theta_i$ repeat, and points with equal parameters form a cluster. Integrating $G$ out gives the equivalent form used for inference: the labels follow the Chinese restaurant process, each occupied cluster gets a parameter $\theta_k \sim H$, and $x_i \sim F(\theta_{z_i})$. It is the finite Bayesian mixture of the baseline in the limit $K \to \infty$, the **infinite mixture model** ([Rasmussen, 2000](#references)). For Gaussian clusters, $F(x \mid \theta)$ is a Gaussian with parameters $\theta$ and $H$ a prior on them.

## Collapsed Gibbs sampling with the restaurant

The posterior over partitions has no closed form, but its conditionals do. With a conjugate $H$, the cluster parameters can be integrated out, and the sampler works on the labels alone: the **collapsed** Gibbs sampler of the [Gibbs sampling note](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling), here Algorithm 3 of [Neal (2000)](#references). By exchangeability, point $i$ can be treated as the last customer. Remove it from its table, then reseat it with

$$
p(z_i = k \mid z_{-i}, x) \;\propto\;
\begin{cases}
n_{-i,k}\; p(x_i \mid x_{-i,k}) & \text{an occupied cluster } k, \\[2pt]
\alpha\; p(x_i) & \text{a new cluster,}
\end{cases}
$$

where $x_{-i,k}$ are the other points of cluster $k$, $p(x_i \mid x_{-i,k}) = \int F(x_i \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid x_{-i,k})\, d\theta$ is the posterior predictive of that cluster, and $p(x_i) = \int F(x_i \mid \theta)\, H(d\theta)$ the prior predictive of an empty one. The first factor is the restaurant's prior, the second how well the cluster explains the point. A cluster emptied by removing point $i$ disappears; choosing "new" creates one.

**Gaussian clusters with known variance.** Let $F(x \mid \mu) = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2 I)$ in $d$ dimensions and $H = \mathcal N(\mu_0, \tau^2 I)$. For a cluster holding $m$ points with sum $s$, precisions add, as in the [normal update](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#normal-data-and-shrinkage):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda &= \frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{m}{\sigma^2}, \\
\hat\mu &= \frac{1}{\lambda}\Big(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau^2} + \frac{s}{\sigma^2}\Big), \\
p(x \mid \text{cluster}) &= \mathcal N\Big(x \;\Big|\; \hat\mu,\ \big(\tfrac1\lambda + \sigma^2\big) I\Big) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

the uncertainty about the cluster mean plus the noise. With $m = 0$ this is the prior predictive $\mathcal N(\mu_0, (\tau^2 + \sigma^2) I)$ of a new cluster. A sweep keeps the counts and sums of each cluster, so reseating a point costs $O(K_n d)$.

**Worked example.** In one dimension, take $\sigma^2 = 1$, $\tau^2 = 10$, $\mu_0 = 0$ and $\alpha = 1$. The other points form cluster A, five points with mean 0, and cluster B, three points with mean 4. Reseat $x_i = 3$:

| | prior weight | predictive mean | predictive variance | density at 3 | product | probability |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | $n = 5$ | $0$ | $1.196$ | $0.0085$ | $0.042$ | $0.047$ |
| B | $n = 3$ | $3.871$ | $1.323$ | $0.2604$ | $0.781$ | $0.865$ |
| new | $\alpha = 1$ | $0$ | $11$ | $0.0799$ | $0.080$ | $0.088$ |

Cluster B wins although it is smaller, because the point is close to it. A new cluster is more likely than cluster A, despite a prior weight five times smaller: the new cluster's predictive is wide and gives some density everywhere, while A's is narrow and centred three units away. For non-conjugate $H$ the integrals have no closed form, and Algorithm 8 of [Neal (2000)](#references) replaces them with a few auxiliary parameters drawn from $H$.

## An experiment: four clusters, an unknown number

The Code section draws 200 points in the plane from four round clusters of 80, 60, 40 and 20 points with standard deviation 0.5, centred at the corners of a square of side 4. It runs the collapsed sampler with the true $\sigma^2 = 0.25$, a vague prior on the means ($\tau = 3$ around the data mean) and $\alpha = 1$, for 100 sweeps, starting with all points in one cluster.

- **One run finds the partition.** With seed 1 the final state has clusters of 80, 60, 40 and 20 points, an adjusted Rand index of $1.0$ against the truth, and the same log joint probability $\log p(x, z) = -601.1$ as the true partition.
- **The number of clusters is a posterior, not a number.** Over sweeps 21–100 of that run, the sampler has 4 clusters in 21% of the sweeps and 5.2 on average: points in the tails of the clusters keep opening and closing clusters of one or two points. With $\alpha = 0.1$ the average is $4.08$ and four clusters occur in 92% of the sweeps; with $\alpha = 10$ the average is $11.48$ and four never occur. The concentration is the price of a new cluster.
- **Single-site Gibbs can get stuck.** Of five runs (seeds 0–4), one more ends with the four clusters plus three stray points, and three end with the 80-point cluster still split, into $71 + 9$, $61 + 19$ and $70 + 6 + 4$ points, with log joint probabilities 22 to 42 nats below the true partition. Merging two halves of a cluster one point at a time passes through states much less probable than either end. Split–merge moves, which propose to merge or split whole clusters in one Metropolis–Hastings step, fix this ([Jain and Neal, 2004](#references)).
- **The variational alternative agrees.** scikit-learn's `BayesianGaussianMixture` with a Dirichlet process prior, truncated at 10 sticks, gives weights $0.397, 0.281, 0.195, 0.103$ to four components and at most $0.005$ to each of the other six; the data's proportions are $0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1$.

## What changed

| | Finite Bayesian mixture | Dirichlet process mixture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of components | $K$, fixed in advance | infinite; $K_n$ occupied, inferred |
| Prior on the weights | $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha/K, \dots, \alpha/K)$ | stick-breaking with $\mathrm{Beta}(1, \alpha)$ |
| Prior on the partition | Dirichlet–categorical | Chinese restaurant process |
| Choosing $K$ | refit for every $K$, compare by BIC or evidence | one fit; a posterior over $K_n$ |
| New data | may need a larger $K$ and a refit | new clusters open as needed, about $\alpha \log n$ in all |
| Collapsed Gibbs | reseat among $K$ clusters | reseat among $K_n$ clusters or a new one |
| Variational inference | $K$ components | $T$ truncated sticks |

## Limitations

- **The prior on $K$ is not neutral.** $\alpha$ sets both the expected number of clusters and the rich-get-richer pattern of their sizes, and the posterior inherits both; a prior with many clusters of similar size needs a different process.
- **The number of clusters is not consistently estimated.** Even when the data come from a finite mixture of the assumed form, the posterior of a Dirichlet process mixture keeps mass on extra small clusters and does not concentrate on the true number as $n$ grows ([Miller and Harrison, 2013](#references)). For density estimation this does no harm; for counting clusters it does.
- **Slow mixing.** One-point-at-a-time Gibbs moves rarely merge or split large clusters, and runs from different starts can disagree for a long time; split–merge moves or variational methods are the usual remedies.
- **The cluster model still matters.** A Gaussian cluster model will cover a curved or heavy-tailed cluster with several Gaussians, exactly as a finite mixture does: the process chooses how many components, not what they look like.

## What you should remember

- The Dirichlet process $\mathrm{DP}(\alpha, H)$ is a random discrete distribution whose finite-partition masses are Dirichlet: mean $H$, concentration $\alpha$.
- It is the limit $K \to \infty$ of a mixture with a $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha/K, \dots, \alpha/K)$ prior on the weights.
- Chinese restaurant: join table $k$ with probability $n_k/(n + \alpha)$, open a new one with $\alpha/(n + \alpha)$; the partition probability $\alpha^K \Gamma(\alpha)/\Gamma(\alpha + n) \cdot \prod_k (n_k - 1)!$ is exchangeable.
- $\mathbb E[K_n] = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha/(\alpha + i - 1) \approx \alpha \log(1 + n/\alpha)$: logarithmic growth.
- Stick-breaking: $v_k \sim \mathrm{Beta}(1, \alpha)$, $\pi_k = v_k \prod_{j \lt k}(1 - v_j)$; the leftover after $T$ sticks is $(\alpha/(1 + \alpha))^T$ on average, which justifies truncation.
- Collapsed Gibbs for a DP mixture: reseat each point with weight $n_{-i,k}$ times the cluster's posterior predictive, or $\alpha$ times the prior predictive for a new cluster.

## Code

The restaurant and the sticks, then a collapsed Gibbs sampler for a Dirichlet process mixture of Gaussians on 2-D data, its sensitivity to the start and to $\alpha$, and scikit-learn's variational version. It runs in about ten seconds; exact numbers can vary across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import digamma, gammaln, logsumexp
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score
from sklearn.mixture import BayesianGaussianMixture

# the Chinese restaurant process: simulated number of tables against the exact expectation
def crp(n, alpha, rng):
    counts = []
    for i in range(n):                                   # customer i + 1 sees i seated customers
        p = np.array(counts + [alpha]) / (i + alpha)
        k = rng.choice(len(p), p=p)
        if k == len(counts): counts.append(1)
        else: counts[k] += 1
    return counts

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for alpha, n in ((1.0, 100), (5.0, 1000)):
    sim = np.mean([len(crp(n, alpha, rng)) for _ in range(200)])
    exact = alpha * (digamma(alpha + n) - digamma(alpha))     # = sum of alpha / (alpha + i) over i = 0, ..., n - 1
    print(alpha, n, round(sim, 2), round(exact, 3), round(alpha * np.log(1 + n / alpha), 3))
# 1.0 100 5.23 5.187 4.615 | 5.0 1000 26.83 27.031 26.517

# stick-breaking: expected weights (1/(1+alpha)) (alpha/(1+alpha))^(k-1), and the expected mass left after 20 sticks
for alpha in (1.0, 5.0):
    print(alpha, [round(1 / (1 + alpha) * (alpha / (1 + alpha)) ** (k - 1), 3) for k in (1, 2, 3)], round((alpha / (1 + alpha)) ** 20, 4))
# 1.0 [0.5, 0.25, 0.125] 0.0 | 5.0 [0.167, 0.139, 0.116] 0.0261

# 2-D data: four spherical clusters of 80, 60, 40 and 20 points with standard deviation 0.5
centres = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [4.0, 0.0], [0.0, 4.0], [4.0, 4.0]])
sizes = (80, 60, 40, 20)
truth = np.repeat(np.arange(4), sizes)
X = np.vstack([c + 0.5 * rng.normal(size=(m, 2)) for c, m in zip(centres, sizes)])
n, d = X.shape

# DP mixture of Gaussians with known covariance s2 I and a conjugate prior N(mu0, t2 I) on each cluster mean
s2, t2, mu0 = 0.25, 9.0, X.mean(axis=0)

def log_pred(x, cnt, tot):
    """log predictive density of x for clusters with cnt points summing to tot (cnt = 0: a new cluster)"""
    prec = 1 / t2 + cnt / s2                             # posterior precision of the cluster mean
    mean = (mu0 / t2 + tot / s2) / prec[:, None]
    var = 1 / prec + s2                                  # posterior variance of the mean plus the noise
    return -0.5 * d * np.log(2 * np.pi * var) - 0.5 * ((x - mean) ** 2).sum(axis=1) / var

def collapsed_gibbs(X, alpha, sweeps, rng):
    z = np.zeros(len(X), dtype=int)                      # start with every point in one cluster
    cnt, tot = np.array([len(X)]), X.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True)
    trace = []
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        for i in rng.permutation(len(X)):
            k = z[i]; cnt[k] -= 1; tot[k] -= X[i]        # take point i out of its cluster
            if cnt[k] == 0:                              # drop an empty cluster: move the last one into its slot
                cnt[k], tot[k] = cnt[-1], tot[-1]; z[z == len(cnt) - 1] = k
                cnt, tot = cnt[:-1], tot[:-1]
            logp = np.log(np.append(cnt, alpha)) + log_pred(X[i], np.append(cnt, 0), np.vstack([tot, np.zeros(d)]))
            k = rng.choice(len(logp), p=np.exp(logp - logsumexp(logp)))   # n_k p(x | cluster k), or alpha p(x)
            if k == len(cnt):                            # open a new cluster
                cnt, tot = np.append(cnt, 0), np.vstack([tot, np.zeros(d)])
            z[i] = k; cnt[k] += 1; tot[k] += X[i]
        trace.append(len(cnt))
    return z, cnt, np.array(trace)

def log_joint(z, alpha):                                 # log p(x, z): CRP prior times the cluster marginal likelihoods
    ks, cs = np.unique(z, return_counts=True)
    lp = len(ks) * np.log(alpha) + gammaln(alpha) - gammaln(alpha + len(z)) + gammaln(cs).sum()
    for k in ks:                                         # chain rule: each point given the earlier points of its cluster
        Xk = X[z == k]
        cum = np.vstack([np.zeros(d), np.cumsum(Xk, axis=0)[:-1]])
        lp += sum(log_pred(x, np.array([j]), cum[j][None]) [0] for j, x in enumerate(Xk))
    return lp

print(round(log_joint(truth, 1.0), 1))                   # -601.1 for the true partition
for seed in range(5):                                    # final sizes, adjusted Rand index, log p(x, z), counts of K = 4..8 after burn-in
    z, cnt, trace = collapsed_gibbs(X, 1.0, 100, np.random.default_rng(seed))
```

## Next

A Dirichlet process mixture gives each data point one cluster. A document is not like that: an article on the economics of football mixes words from a sports topic and from an economics topic, and a collection of documents shares the same topics in different proportions. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation) gives every document its own mixture over a common set of topics, latent Dirichlet allocation, and fits it with the collapsed Gibbs sampler of this note and with variational inference. Its nonparametric version, the hierarchical Dirichlet process, shares the atoms of one Dirichlet process among many ([Teh et al., 2006](#references)).
