[Stochastic variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference) made every step cheap, but every step still used a formula: the natural gradient $E_\phi[\eta_g] - \lambda$ exists in closed form only when each complete conditional is in a known exponential family. Bayesian logistic regression, the first model of the course without conjugacy, already breaks this, and so does any model with a neural network inside. **Black-box variational inference** (BBVI) of [Ranganath, Gerrish and Blei (2014)](#references) gives up the formula. The gradient of the ELBO is itself an expectation under $q$, so it can be estimated from samples of $q$, for any model whose log joint density can be evaluated. This note derives the two estimators that do this, the score function and the reparameterization gradient, measures their variances, and shows that the variational autoencoder is black-box inference with one more idea: an encoder that predicts the variational parameters.

**Notation.** $p(x, z)$ is the joint density of the data $x$ and the latent variables $z$, and all we assume about it is that we can evaluate $\log p(x, z)$ for any $z$, and for the second estimator also its gradient in $z$. $q(z \mid \lambda)$ is the variational distribution with parameters $\lambda$; for a mean-field Gaussian, $\lambda = (\mu, \sigma)$ and $q = \prod_j \mathcal N(z_j \mid \mu_j, \sigma_j^2)$. The ELBO is $\mathcal L(\lambda) = E_q[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z \mid \lambda)]$, and we abbreviate the integrand as $f_\lambda(z) = \log p(x, z) - \log q(z \mid \lambda)$. The **score** of $q$ is $\nabla_\lambda \log q(z \mid \lambda)$. $S$ is the number of Monte Carlo samples per gradient estimate, and $\varepsilon$ is standard normal noise.

## Baseline: a model without a closed-form update

Bayesian logistic regression has weights $w \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and labels $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ with $p(y_i = 1 \mid w) = \sigma(w^\top x_i)$, the [logistic model](/notes/ml/logistic-regression). With a Gaussian $q(w)$, coordinate ascent would need $E_q[\log \sigma(w^\top x_i)]$ and its derivatives in closed form. There is none: the expectation of the log-sigmoid under a Gaussian is a one-dimensional integral without an elementary antiderivative. The usual ways out are model-specific. The [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) replaces the posterior by a Gaussian at its mode; a quadratic lower bound on the log-sigmoid ([Jaakkola and Jordan, 2000](#references)) restores conjugacy for this one likelihood. Each new model needs its own derivation, and that is what made variational inference a specialist's tool. BBVI asks for one thing only: the ability to evaluate $\log p(x, z)$ at a point.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> The gradient of the ELBO can be rewritten as an expectation under $q$ of a quantity that can be evaluated at any sample. Its Monte Carlo average is an unbiased gradient estimate, and stochastic gradient ascent with Robbins–Monro steps then maximizes the ELBO of any model. The whole difficulty moves into the variance of the estimate: variance reduction, or the reparameterization of the samples, decides whether the method works in practice.</aside>

## The score-function gradient

Differentiate the ELBO under the integral sign. Both the density $q$ and the integrand $f_\lambda$ depend on $\lambda$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\lambda \mathcal L &= \nabla_\lambda \int q(z \mid \lambda)\, f_\lambda(z)\, dz = \int \nabla_\lambda q(z \mid \lambda)\, f_\lambda(z)\, dz + \int q(z \mid \lambda)\, \nabla_\lambda f_\lambda(z)\, dz \\
&= \int q(z \mid \lambda)\, \nabla_\lambda \log q(z \mid \lambda)\, f_\lambda(z)\, dz - \int q(z \mid \lambda)\, \nabla_\lambda \log q(z \mid \lambda)\, dz \\
&= E_q\big[\nabla_\lambda \log q(z \mid \lambda)\, f_\lambda(z)\big] - \nabla_\lambda \int q(z \mid \lambda)\, dz = E_q\big[\nabla_\lambda \log q(z \mid \lambda)\, \big(\log p(x, z) - \log q(z \mid \lambda)\big)\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the product rule. The second uses the log-derivative identity $\nabla_\lambda q = q\, \nabla_\lambda \log q$ in the first integral and, in the second, $\nabla_\lambda f_\lambda = -\nabla_\lambda \log q$, since $\log p(x, z)$ does not depend on $\lambda$. The last integral is the gradient of the constant 1, so the expected score is zero: $E_q[\nabla_\lambda \log q] = 0$. Exchanging derivative and integral needs the usual regularity, for example a dominating integrable bound on the derivative. The Monte Carlo estimate with $S$ samples is

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\hat\nabla_\lambda \mathcal L &= \frac1S \sum_{s=1}^S \nabla_\lambda \log q(z_s \mid \lambda)\, \big(\log p(x, z_s) - \log q(z_s \mid \lambda)\big), \\
z_s &\sim q(z \mid \lambda)
\end{aligned}
}
$$

([Ranganath et al., 2014](#references), equations 2 and 3). It needs samples from $q$, its score, and values of $\log p$; the model is a black box. In reinforcement learning the same estimator is **REINFORCE** ([Williams, 1992](#references)), with the reward in place of $f_\lambda$; the [policy gradients note](/notes/agents/policy-gradients#reinforce) of the AI Agents course derives it for trajectories and reduces its variance with the same baselines as below. In statistics it is the likelihood-ratio method.

**Worked example.** Estimate the gradient of $E_q[z^2]$ in $\mu$ for $q = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ at $\mu = \sigma = 1$; the exact value is $2\mu = 2$. The score in $\mu$ is $(z - \mu)/\sigma^2$, so one sample gives $(z - \mu) z^2/\sigma^2$. With $z = 1 + \varepsilon$ this is $\varepsilon (1 + \varepsilon)^2 = \varepsilon + 2\varepsilon^2 + \varepsilon^3$, whose mean is $0 + 2 + 0 = 2$. Its second moment is $E[\varepsilon^2 + 4\varepsilon^4 + \varepsilon^6 + 2\varepsilon^4] = 1 + 12 + 15 + 6 = 34$ (odd moments vanish, $E\varepsilon^4 = 3$, $E\varepsilon^6 = 15$), so the variance of one sample is $34 - 4 = 30$, 7.5 times the square of what we are estimating. A million samples in the Code section give mean $2.005$ and variance $30.1$.

## Reducing the variance

The estimator is unbiased, but a variance of 30 for a quantity of size 2 means hundreds of samples per step for a useful direction, and in real models it is much worse. Two generic tools, both from the [Monte Carlo toolbox](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#variance-reduction), cut it without model-specific work.

### Rao–Blackwellization: drop the terms that cannot matter

Take a mean-field $q = \prod_j q(z_j \mid \lambda_j)$. The score with respect to $\lambda_j$, $h_j(z_j) = \nabla_{\lambda_j} \log q(z_j \mid \lambda_j)$, depends on $z_j$ alone. For any function $g$ of the other variables, independence under $q$ and the zero mean of the score give $E_q[h_j(z_j)\, g(z_{-j})] = E[h_j(z_j)]\, E[g(z_{-j})] = 0$. So every term of $\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)$ that does not involve $z_j$ can be deleted from the $j$-th component of the estimator without changing its mean. What remains is

$$
\nabla_{\lambda_j} \mathcal L = E_q\big[h_j(z_j)\, \big(\log p_j(x, z_{(j)}) - \log q(z_j \mid \lambda_j)\big)\big],
$$

where $\log p_j$ collects the factors of the joint that contain $z_j$, which depend only on $z_j$ and its Markov blanket $z_{(j)}$ ([Ranganath et al., 2014](#references), equation 5). The deleted terms had mean zero but contributed variance, so the new estimator has lower variance. This is a form of **Rao–Blackwellization**: the expectation over the irrelevant variables is computed exactly, and it is zero.

**Worked example.** $n$ independent local variables, $z_i \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $x_i \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(e^{z_i})$, a non-conjugate model, with $q(z_i) = \mathcal N(0, 0.5^2)$. For the gradient in $\mu_1$, the plain estimator multiplies the score of $z_1$ by all $n$ terms of $\log p - \log q$; the Rao–Blackwellized one keeps only the term of $z_1$. In the Code section the single-sample variance of the plain estimator is $8.2$ for $n = 1$, $1921$ for $n = 10$ and $374{,}011$ for $n = 100$, growing with the number of irrelevant terms; the Rao–Blackwellized variance is $8.2$ for every $n$. In a model where every variable touches every likelihood term, as in logistic regression, the Markov blanket is everything and the trick removes only the entropy terms of the other coordinates.

### Control variates: subtract a baseline

A **control variate** is a function $c(z)$ with known mean that is correlated with the estimator. With $\hat g(z) = g(z) - a\,(c(z) - E[c])$, the mean is unchanged and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(\hat g) &= \operatorname{Var}(g) + a^2 \operatorname{Var}(c) - 2a \operatorname{Cov}(g, c), \\
a^* &= \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(g, c)}{\operatorname{Var}(c)},
\end{aligned}
$$

by differentiating in $a$. BBVI uses the score itself, $c = h_j$, whose mean is zero, so the corrected component is $h_j(z)\big(f_\lambda(z) - a_j\big)$: the integrand minus a constant **baseline** $a_j$ ([Ranganath et al., 2014](#references), equations 7 to 10). In practice $a_j$ is estimated from the same $S$ samples as the gradient. The baseline matters because the plain estimator is not invariant to shifting $\log p$ by a constant $c$: the shift adds $c\, h_j(z)$, whose mean is zero but whose variance is $c^2 \operatorname{Var}(h_j)$. For a Gaussian $q$ the score in $\mu_j$ is $\varepsilon_j/\sigma_j$, with variance $1/\sigma_j^2$, so the penalty for a large $|\log p|$ grows as $q$ concentrates. For the one-weight logistic model of the figure below, at the optimum of the ELBO, the optimal baseline lowers the single-sample variance from $94.7$ to $0.20$. Ranganath et al. report that Rao–Blackwellization lowered the variance on their model of patient records by several orders of magnitude and the control variate lowered it further (their Figure 2).

## The reparameterization gradient

The score-function estimator uses only values of $\log p$. When $\log p(x, z)$ is differentiable in $z$ and $q$ can be sampled as a differentiable function of parameter-free noise, the gradient of $\log p$ can be used too. For a Gaussian, $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ has the distribution $q$, and the randomness no longer depends on $\lambda$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\lambda) &= E_\varepsilon\big[\log p(x, \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon) - \log q(\mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon \mid \mu, \sigma)\big] \\
&= E_\varepsilon\big[\log p(x, \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon)\big] + \sum_j \log \sigma_j + \text{const}, \\
\nabla_\mu \mathcal L &= E_\varepsilon\big[\nabla_z \log p(x, z)\big], \qquad \nabla_{\sigma} \mathcal L = E_\varepsilon\big[\nabla_z \log p(x, z) \odot \varepsilon\big] + 1/\sigma, \qquad z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line uses $\log q(\mu + \sigma\varepsilon \mid \mu, \sigma) = -\sum_j \big(\tfrac12 \varepsilon_j^2 + \log \sigma_j\big) - \tfrac D2 \log 2\pi$: the entropy of a Gaussian depends only on $\sigma$. The third line differentiates under the expectation, which is now over a fixed distribution, with the chain rule $\partial z_j/\partial \mu_j = 1$ and $\partial z_j / \partial \sigma_j = \varepsilon_j$; $1/\sigma$ is taken elementwise. The Monte Carlo version averages $\nabla_z \log p$ over $S$ draws of $\varepsilon$:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\hat\nabla_\mu \mathcal L &= \frac1S \sum_{s=1}^S \nabla_z \log p(x, z_s), \\
z_s &= \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon_s
\end{aligned}
}
$$

This is the **reparameterization gradient** ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), section 2.4; [Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra, 2014](#references); [Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla, 2014](#references)). The [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#the-reparameterization-trick) introduces the same trick for an encoder; here it is a general-purpose gradient estimator for any differentiable model.

<TwoEstimators />

1. One sample: $\varepsilon = 0.5$ gives $z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon = 1.5$ at $\mu = \sigma = 1$.
2. Score function: the value $f(z) = z^2 = 2.25$ times the score $(z - \mu)/\sigma^2 = 0.5$ gives $1.125$.
3. Reparameterization: the derivative $f'(z) = 2z = 3$ times $\partial z / \partial \mu = 1$ gives $3$.
4. Over many samples both estimates average to the true gradient 2. Their single-sample variances are 30 and $\operatorname{Var}[2(1 + \varepsilon)] = 4$, so the score function needs 7.5 times as many samples for the same precision.

The figure shows why reparameterization usually has the lower variance: it transports the slope of the integrand at the sample, while the score function must infer the slope from how the values vary across samples. For a linear integrand $c^\top z$ the reparameterization gradient is exactly $c$ for every sample, with zero variance; the score-function estimator still fluctuates.

The plain score function is 40 to 2000 times worse than the reparameterization gradient at the start and 7000 to 12,000 times worse at the fitted $q$: the score $\varepsilon_j/\sigma_j$ grows as $\sigma$ shrinks, and with it the penalty for the large value of $|\log p - \log q|$. With a baseline it wins at the fitted $q$ for $d = 2$ and $d = 10$, and loses by a factor of $7.5$ at $d = 50$; at the start it loses by factors of $4$, $15$ and $70$. The baseline is one number per coordinate, and it cannot follow how the integrand varies along the other $d - 1$ directions; the reparameterization gradient does not need to, because it differentiates.

### When each estimator applies

*Optional depth: the requirements of the two estimators. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

The score function needs only a $q$ that can be sampled and whose score can be computed, so it works for discrete latent variables, for non-differentiable likelihoods and for simulators whose density is known but not differentiable. The reparameterization gradient needs $\log p$ to be differentiable in $z$ and $q$ to be written as a differentiable transformation of fixed noise: location-scale families directly, others through their inverse CDF or through implicit reparameterization. Discrete variables have no such transformation; for them one uses the score function with strong control variates, or a continuous relaxation that trades bias for variance ([Mohamed et al., 2020](#references), sections 4, 5 and 7, review the options). Automatic differentiation variational inference ([Kucukelbir et al., 2017](#references)) packages the reparameterization route for a whole class of models: it transforms every constrained latent variable to the real line, places a Gaussian $q$ there, and differentiates the model's log density automatically.

## The algorithm

1. Initialize $\lambda$; choose a step-size schedule $\rho_t$ that satisfies the Robbins–Monro conditions, or an adaptive rule such as AdaGrad, which Ranganath et al. use.
2. Draw $S$ samples from $q(z \mid \lambda)$, or $S$ noise vectors $\varepsilon_s$ for the reparameterization gradient.
3. Compute the gradient estimate: the score function with Rao–Blackwellization and a baseline, or the reparameterization gradient.
4. Step: $\lambda \leftarrow \lambda + \rho_t\, \hat\nabla_\lambda \mathcal L$, and repeat from 2.

With global and local variables, step 3 can also subsample the data, scaling the local terms by $N/B$ as SVI does. The estimate is then **doubly stochastic** ([Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla, 2014](#references)): random in the mini-batch and random in the samples of $q$, and still unbiased. In the Code section, 3000 steps with the reparameterization gradient, 10 samples each and all 200 data points per step, fit the logistic regressions of the table.

## Amortized inference and the VAE

With one local latent variable per data point, BBVI keeps local variational parameters $\lambda_i = (\mu_i, \sigma_i)$ for every $x_i$ and has to optimize them for each point, also for every new point at test time. **Amortized inference** replaces this table by a function: an **encoder** network with weights $\psi$ outputs $\big(\mu_\psi(x_i), \sigma_\psi(x_i)\big)$, and the ELBO is maximized over $\psi$ instead of over the $\lambda_i$. The cost of inference for a new point drops to one forward pass, and what is learned about inference on some points transfers to the others.

The [variational autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/vae) is exactly this, with four choices that the previous sections explain:

- the ELBO of each point, $E_{q_\psi(z \mid x_i)}[\log p_\theta(x_i \mid z)] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\psi(z \mid x_i) \,\Vert\, p(z)\big)$, derived in the [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from);
- a mean-field Gaussian $q$ and the reparameterization gradient, with $S = 1$ sample per point, its Monte Carlo part;
- mini-batches of data points, so the estimate is doubly stochastic, as above;
- the model parameters $\theta$ of the decoder, learned by maximizing the same ELBO, which makes the procedure variational EM in which both steps are stochastic gradient steps ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), section 2.3).

In the language of this course, the VAE is BBVI with a reparameterization gradient, amortized by an encoder, and a decoder whose parameters are fitted by the evidence lower bound; the KL term between two Gaussians is computed in closed form instead of by sampling, which removes its share of the variance in the same spirit as Rao–Blackwellization.

Amortization has a price. The ELBO reached by an encoder can be no higher than the one reached by optimizing $\lambda_i$ separately for every point, because the encoder's outputs are one particular choice of the $\lambda_i$. [Cremer, Li and Duvenaud (2018)](#references) split the gap between $\log p(x)$ and the ELBO into an **approximation gap**, from the family of $q$, and an **amortization gap**, from the encoder, and found that the amortization gap is often the larger one. Optimizing the $\lambda_i$ of a test point for a few steps, starting from the encoder's output, recovers part of it.

## What changed

| | Stochastic variational inference | Black-box variational inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Models | conditionally conjugate | any model with a computable $\log p(x, z)$ |
| Gradient | natural gradient in closed form | Monte Carlo estimate under $q$ |
| Estimators | none needed | score function; reparameterization if $\log p$ is differentiable |
| Variance control | mini-batch size | Rao–Blackwellization, baselines, reparameterization, more samples |
| Toy gradient, one sample | — | variance 30 against 4 |
| Local parameters | one $\phi_i$ per point | per point, or amortized by an encoder (the VAE) |

## Limitations

- **Variance remains the bottleneck.** Even with variance reduction, the score function can need many samples per step, and step sizes must be small enough for the noisiest component. Convergence is slower and less predictable than the closed-form steps of SVI.
- **Reparameterization needs differentiability.** It applies to continuous latent variables with differentiable log densities; discrete variables need the score function or relaxations with their own bias.
- **The family of $q$ is still the limit.** A mean-field Gaussian cannot represent correlations or several modes; BBVI makes richer families usable, such as full-covariance Gaussians or normalizing flows, but does not choose them.
- **The ELBO is noisy too.** Monitoring convergence with a Monte Carlo ELBO estimate needs care, and a stalled objective can be a step-size problem rather than convergence.
- **Amortization gap.** An encoder trades accuracy of the per-point posterior for speed and generalization.

## What you should remember

- The gradient of the ELBO is $E_q\big[\nabla_\lambda \log q(z \mid \lambda)\,(\log p(x, z) - \log q(z \mid \lambda))\big]$, because the expected score is zero; its Monte Carlo average needs only samples of $q$ and values of $\log p$.
- With mean field, terms that do not involve $z_j$ can be dropped from the $j$-th component (Rao–Blackwellization), and a baseline $a_j = \operatorname{Cov}(g_j, h_j)/\operatorname{Var}(h_j)$ subtracted from the integrand (a control variate); both keep the estimator unbiased.
- The reparameterization gradient writes $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon$ and averages $\nabla_z \log p(x, z)$; for $E[z^2]$ at $\mu = \sigma = 1$ its variance is 4 against 30 for the score function, and its advantage grows with the dimension.
- With mini-batches the estimate is doubly stochastic and still unbiased.
- The VAE is BBVI with the reparameterization gradient, an encoder that amortizes the local variational parameters, and decoder parameters fitted by the same ELBO.

## Code

The toy gradient of the worked example, the three estimators on the one-weight logistic model of the figure, the comparison across dimensions, and Rao–Blackwellization on a model with independent local variables. NumPy and SciPy; seeds are fixed, and exact numbers vary slightly across platforms and library versions. The script runs in about 2 seconds.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.special import gammaln
LOG2PI = np.log(2 * np.pi)

# 1. the toy objective E_q[z^2] with q = N(mu, sigma^2) at mu = sigma = 1: true gradient in mu is 2 mu = 2
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
eps = rng.normal(size=1_000_000); mu, sigma = 1.0, 1.0
z = mu + sigma * eps
g_score = (z - mu) / sigma ** 2 * z ** 2          # score times the function value
g_reparam = 2 * z * 1.0                            # derivative of the function times dz/dmu = 1
print(g_score.mean().round(3), g_score.var().round(2), g_reparam.mean().round(3), g_reparam.var().round(2))
# 2.005 30.12 2.002 4.01   (exact: 2, 30 and 2, 4)

# 2. one weight, eight points: logistic likelihood, prior N(0, 1), q = N(mu, sigma^2); variances by quadrature
x1 = np.array([-2, -1.5, -1, -0.5, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2.0]); y1 = np.array([0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1.0])
e = np.linspace(-9, 9, 6001); we = np.exp(-e ** 2 / 2); we /= we.sum()     # weights of a standard normal grid
logjoint1 = lambda w: (y1 * np.outer(w, x1) - np.logaddexp(0, np.outer(w, x1))).sum(1) - w ** 2 / 2 - LOG2PI / 2
dlogjoint1 = lambda w: ((y1 - 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.outer(w, x1)))) * x1).sum(1) - w
def elbo1(p):
    w = p[0] + np.exp(p[1]) * e
    return (we * (logjoint1(w) + e ** 2 / 2 + p[1] + LOG2PI / 2)).sum()   # E[log p] - E[log q]
opt = minimize(lambda p: -elbo1(p), [0.0, 0.0]).x; mu1, sg1 = opt[0], np.exp(opt[1])
def variances1(mu, sg):
    w = mu + sg * e; f = logjoint1(w) + e ** 2 / 2 + np.log(sg) + LOG2PI / 2   # log p(x, w) - log q(w)
    h = e / sg                                       # score of q with respect to mu
    a = (we * h * f * h).sum() / (we * h * h).sum()  # optimal constant a* = Cov(h f, h) / Var(h)
    var = lambda g: (we * g * g).sum() - (we * g).sum() ** 2
    return var(h * f), var(h * (f - a)), var(dlogjoint1(w))
print(round(mu1, 3), round(sg1, 3), [round(float(v), 2) for v in variances1(mu1, sg1)], [round(float(v), 2) for v in variances1(-1.0, sg1)])
# 0.76 0.535 [94.68, 0.2, 3.6] [523.15, 86.1, 2.87]

# 3. Bayesian logistic regression with d weights, 200 points; mean-field q; single-sample variance of the mu-gradient
def make(d, n=200, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed); X = r.normal(size=(n, d)); w_true = 3 * r.normal(size=d) / np.sqrt(d)
    return X, (r.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ w_true))).astype(float)
def logjoint(W, X, y):
    s = W @ X.T
    return (y * s - np.logaddexp(0, s)).sum(1) - 0.5 * (W ** 2).sum(1) - 0.5 * W.shape[1] * LOG2PI
def grad_logjoint(W, X, y):
    return (y - 1 / (1 + np.exp(-W @ X.T))) @ X - W
def fit(X, y, steps=3000, seed=1):             # BBVI with the reparameterization gradient, 10 samples per step
    d = X.shape[1]; m, log_s = np.zeros(d), np.zeros(d); r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    for t in range(steps):
        E = r.normal(size=(10, d)); s = np.exp(log_s); G = grad_logjoint(m + s * E, X, y)
        lr = 0.01 / (1 + t / 500)
        m += lr * G.mean(0); log_s += lr * ((G * E * s).mean(0) + 1)   # +1: gradient of the entropy in log sigma
    return m, np.exp(log_s)
def variances(X, y, m, s, S=20_000, seed=3):
    E = np.random.default_rng(seed).normal(size=(S, len(m))); W = m + s * E
    f = logjoint(W, X, y) - (-0.5 * E ** 2 - np.log(s) - 0.5 * LOG2PI).sum(1)   # log p(x, w) - log q(w)
    H = E / s                                    # score with respect to each mu_j
    G_score = H * f[:, None]
    a = ((G_score - G_score.mean(0)) * H).mean(0) / (H * H).mean(0)           # one control-variate weight per mu_j
    G_cv = G_score - a * H
    G_rep = grad_logjoint(W, X, y)               # log q(m + s e) does not depend on m, so only log p contributes
    return [G.var(0).sum() for G in (G_score, G_cv, G_rep)]
for d in (2, 10, 50):
    X, y = make(d)
    v0 = variances(X, y, np.zeros(d), np.ones(d))
    v1 = variances(X, y, *fit(X, y))
    print(d, ['%.3g' % v for v in v0], ['%.3g' % v for v in v1])
# 2 ['9.49e+04', '9.48e+03', '2.24e+03'] ['8.23e+05', '16.7', '67.8']
# 10 ['1.03e+06', '8.39e+04', '5.44e+03'] ['1.85e+06', '188', '259']
# 50 ['1.79e+07', '6.24e+05', '8.96e+03'] ['7.02e+06', '6.64e+03', '886']

# 4. Rao-Blackwellization: n independent local variables, z_i ~ N(0, 1), x_i ~ Poisson(exp(z_i)), q(z_i) = N(0, 0.5^2)
r = np.random.default_rng(4); xp = r.poisson(np.exp(r.normal(size=100))); E = r.normal(size=(20_000, 100)); s = 0.5
Z = s * E                                        # samples of q; log p and log q are sums of one term per i
terms = xp * Z - np.exp(Z) - gammaln(xp + 1) - 0.5 * Z ** 2 - 0.5 * LOG2PI - (-0.5 * E ** 2 - np.log(s) - 0.5 * LOG2PI)
h1 = E[:, 0] / s                                 # score with respect to mu_1
for n in (1, 10, 100):
    print(n, round(float((h1 * terms[:, :n].sum(1)).var()), 1), round(float((h1 * terms[:, 0]).var()), 1))
# 1 8.2 8.2
```

## Next

BBVI turns posterior inference for any differentiable model into stochastic optimization, and the VAE applies it to latent variables with a neural network as the likelihood. The last step of the course turns the setting around: the latent variables become the weights of the network itself. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-deep-learning) asks what a posterior over millions of weights buys, and compares the reparameterization route (Bayes by Backprop) with the cheaper approximations used in practice: Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensembles and the Laplace approximation.
