Eight labelled points and one weight: a [logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression) with $p(y_i = 1 \mid w) = 1/(1 + e^{-w x_i})$ and a prior $w \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$. We want a Gaussian $q(w) = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ close to the posterior. The evidence lower bound contains $\mathbb E_q[\log(1 + e^{-w x_i})]$, the expectation of a log-sigmoid under a Gaussian, and that integral has no closed form. Coordinate ascent has no update to apply, and [stochastic variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference) has no natural gradient $\mathbb E_\phi[\eta_g] - \lambda$, because no complete conditional is in a known exponential family.

The usual ways out are model-specific. The [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) puts a Gaussian at the mode; a quadratic lower bound on the log-sigmoid ([Jaakkola and Jordan, 2000](#references)) restores conjugacy for this one likelihood. Every new model needs a new derivation. Can the gradient of the ELBO be obtained from nothing more than the ability to evaluate $\log p(x, z)$ at a point?

**Black-box variational inference** (BBVI) of [Ranganath, Gerrish and Blei (2014)](#references) answers yes. The gradient of the ELBO is itself an expectation under $q$, so it can be estimated from samples of $q$ and followed by stochastic gradient ascent.

## The score-function gradient

Let $q(z \mid \lambda)$ be the variational distribution with parameters $\lambda$, for example $\lambda = (\mu, \sigma)$ for a Gaussian. The ELBO is $\mathcal L(\lambda) = \mathbb E_q[f_\lambda(z)]$, where $f_\lambda(z) = \log p(x, z) - \log q(z \mid \lambda)$. Both the density and the integrand depend on $\lambda$. Write $q$ for $q(z \mid \lambda)$ and $f$ for $f_\lambda(z)$. Besides the product rule, the derivation needs one identity, the chain rule applied to the logarithm:

$$
\nabla_\lambda \log q = \frac{\nabla_\lambda q}{q}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\nabla_\lambda q = q\, \nabla_\lambda \log q .
$$

This **log-derivative identity** turns the gradient of a density back into an expectation under that density. Step by step:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\lambda \mathcal L
&= \nabla_\lambda \int q\, f \, dz \\
&= \int \nabla_\lambda (q\, f) \, dz \quad \text{(exchange)} \\
&= \int (\nabla_\lambda q)\, f \, dz + \int q\, \nabla_\lambda f \, dz \quad \text{(product)} \\
&= \int q\, (\nabla_\lambda \log q)\, f \, dz + \int q\, \nabla_\lambda f \, dz \quad \text{(log-derivative)} \\
&= \mathbb E_q\big[\nabla_\lambda \log q\; f\big] - \mathbb E_q\big[\nabla_\lambda \log q\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line uses $\nabla_\lambda f = -\nabla_\lambda \log q$, since $\log p(x, z)$ does not depend on $\lambda$. The second expectation vanishes, by the same identity read backwards:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_q\big[\nabla_\lambda \log q\big]
&= \int q\, \frac{\nabla_\lambda q}{q} \, dz \\
&= \nabla_\lambda \int q \, dz \\
&= \nabla_\lambda 1 = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The **score** $\nabla_\lambda \log q$ has mean zero under $q$, and what remains is

$$
\nabla_\lambda \mathcal L = \mathbb E_q\big[\nabla_\lambda \log q(z \mid \lambda)\, \big(\log p(x, z) - \log q(z \mid \lambda)\big)\big] .
$$

The step marked "exchange" moves a derivative inside an integral. This is allowed when, for all $\lambda$ in a neighbourhood, the integrand is differentiable in $\lambda$ and its gradient is bounded in norm by one integrable function of $z$ ([Folland, 1999](#references), Theorem 2.27, a consequence of dominated convergence). The intuition: a difference quotient of the integral is the integral of difference quotients, and the common bound lets the limit pass inside. A Gaussian $q$ with a $\log p$ of polynomial growth qualifies. A $q$ whose support moves with $\lambda$ does not: for $q = \mathrm{Uniform}(0, \lambda)$ the derivative of $\frac1\lambda \int_0^\lambda f\, dz$ contains the boundary term $f(\lambda)/\lambda$, which the score $-1/\lambda$ never sees.

The Monte Carlo estimate with $S$ samples is

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\hat\nabla_\lambda \mathcal L &= \frac1S \sum_{s=1}^S \nabla_\lambda \log q(z_s \mid \lambda)\, f_\lambda(z_s), \\
z_s &\sim q(z \mid \lambda)
\end{aligned}
}
$$

with $f_\lambda(z_s) = \log p(x, z_s) - \log q(z_s \mid \lambda)$ ([Ranganath et al., 2014](#references), equations 2 and 3). It needs samples from $q$, its score and values of $\log p$; the model is a black box, and $p$ may even be unnormalized. In reinforcement learning the same estimator is **REINFORCE** ([Williams, 1992](#references)), with the reward in place of $f$; the [policy gradients note](/notes/agents/policy-gradients#reinforce) derives it for trajectories and reduces its variance with the same baselines as below. In statistics it is the likelihood-ratio method.

**Worked example.** Take the gradient of $\mathbb E_q[z^2]$ in $\mu$ for $q = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ at $\mu = \sigma = 1$; the exact value is $2\mu = 2$. Differentiating $\log \mathcal N(z \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = -(z - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2) + \text{const}$ gives the score in $\mu$, $(z - \mu)/\sigma^2$. With $z = 1 + \varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$, one sample gives $g = \varepsilon (1 + \varepsilon)^2$.

We need moments of $\varepsilon$. The standard normal density $\varphi$ satisfies $\varphi'(\varepsilon) = -\varepsilon\, \varphi(\varepsilon)$, so integration by parts gives $\mathbb E[\varepsilon^{m+1}] = m\, \mathbb E[\varepsilon^{m-1}]$. Odd moments vanish by symmetry, $\mathbb E\varepsilon^4 = 3\, \mathbb E\varepsilon^2 = 3$ and $\mathbb E\varepsilon^6 = 5\, \mathbb E\varepsilon^4 = 15$. Expanding the square and keeping the even powers,

$$
\begin{aligned}
g &= \varepsilon + 2\varepsilon^2 + \varepsilon^3, \\
\mathbb E[g] &= 0 + 2 + 0 = 2, \\
g^2 &= \varepsilon^2 + 4\varepsilon^3 + 6\varepsilon^4 + 4\varepsilon^5 + \varepsilon^6, \\
\mathbb E[g^2] &= 1 + 0 + 18 + 0 + 15 = 34, \\
\operatorname{Var}(g) &= 34 - 2^2 = 30 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The variance of one sample is 7.5 times the square of what we are estimating. A million samples in the Code section give mean $2.005$ and variance $30.1$.

## Reducing the variance

The estimator is unbiased, but a variance of 30 for a quantity of size 2 means hundreds of samples per step for a reliable direction, and real models are much worse. Two generic tools from the [Monte Carlo toolbox](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#variance-reduction) cut it without model-specific work.

### Rao–Blackwellization: drop the terms that cannot matter

Take a mean-field $q = \prod_j q(z_j \mid \lambda_j)$. The score with respect to $\lambda_j$, $h_j(z_j) = \nabla_{\lambda_j} \log q(z_j \mid \lambda_j)$, depends on $z_j$ alone. For any function $g$ of the other variables, independence under $q$ and the zero mean of the score give

$$
\mathbb E_q\big[h_j(z_j)\, g(z_{-j})\big] = \mathbb E\big[h_j(z_j)\big]\, \mathbb E\big[g(z_{-j})\big] = 0 .
$$

So every term of $\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)$ that does not involve $z_j$ can be deleted from the $j$-th component of the estimator without changing its mean:

$$
\nabla_{\lambda_j} \mathcal L = \mathbb E_q\Big[h_j(z_j)\, \big(\log p_j(x, z_{(j)}) - \log q(z_j \mid \lambda_j)\big)\Big],
$$

where $\log p_j$ collects the factors of the joint that contain $z_j$; they depend only on $z_j$ and its Markov blanket $z_{(j)}$ ([Ranganath et al., 2014](#references), equation 5). The deleted terms had mean zero and contributed only variance. This is a form of **Rao–Blackwellization**: the expectation over the irrelevant variables is computed exactly, and it is zero.

**Worked example.** A non-conjugate model with $n$ independent local variables, $z_i \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $x_i \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(e^{z_i})$, and $q(z_i) = \mathcal N(0, 0.5^2)$. For the gradient in $\mu_1$, the plain estimator multiplies the score of $z_1$ by all $n$ terms of $\log p - \log q$; the Rao–Blackwellized one keeps only the term of $z_1$. Single-sample variances from the Code section:

| $n$ | Plain | Rao–Blackwellized |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $8.2$ | $8.2$ |
| 10 | $1921$ | $8.2$ |
| 100 | $374{,}011$ | $8.2$ |

The plain variance grows with the number of irrelevant terms, and the Rao–Blackwellized one stays put. In a model where every variable touches every likelihood term, as in logistic regression, the Markov blanket is everything, and the trick removes only the entropy and prior terms of the other coordinates.

### Control variates: subtract a baseline

A **control variate** is a function $c(z)$ with known mean that is correlated with the estimator. Take $\hat g(z) = g(z) - a\,(c(z) - \mathbb E[c])$. Its mean is $\mathbb E[g] - a(\mathbb E[c] - \mathbb E[c]) = \mathbb E[g]$, unchanged for every $a$. Its variance expands by the bilinearity of covariance, and setting the derivative in $a$ to zero gives the best coefficient:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(\hat g) &= \operatorname{Var}(g) + a^2 \operatorname{Var}(c) - 2a \operatorname{Cov}(g, c), \\
\frac{d}{da} \operatorname{Var}(\hat g) &= 2a \operatorname{Var}(c) - 2 \operatorname{Cov}(g, c) = 0, \\
a^* &= \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(g, c)}{\operatorname{Var}(c)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The variance is a convex parabola in $a$, so this stationary point is its minimum.

BBVI uses the score itself, $c = h_j$, whose mean is zero. The corrected component is then $h_j(z)\big(f(z) - a_j\big)$: the integrand minus a constant **baseline** $a_j$ ([Ranganath et al., 2014](#references), equations 7 to 9). In practice $a_j$ is estimated from a few of the samples drawn for the gradient.

Why does a constant matter? Shifting $\log p$ by a constant $c$ adds $c\, h_j(z)$ to the plain estimator. Its mean, $c\, \mathbb E[h_j]$, is zero, so the estimator stays unbiased, but the variance becomes $\operatorname{Var}(h_j f) + 2c \operatorname{Cov}(h_j f, h_j) + c^2 \operatorname{Var}(h_j)$, which for large $\lvert c \rvert$ grows as $c^2 \operatorname{Var}(h_j)$. For a Gaussian $q$ the score in $\mu_j$ is $(z_j - \mu_j)/\sigma_j^2 = \varepsilon_j/\sigma_j$, with variance $1/\sigma_j^2$, so the penalty for a large $\lvert \log p \rvert$ grows as $q$ concentrates. For the one-weight logistic model of the opening, at the optimum of the ELBO, the optimal baseline lowers the single-sample variance from $94.7$ to $0.20$.

Ranganath et al. report that on their model of patient records, Rao–Blackwellization lowered the variance by several orders of magnitude and the control variate lowered it further (their Figure 2). Even so, they drew 1000 samples per gradient and set per-coordinate step sizes with AdaGrad.

## The reparameterization gradient

The score-function estimator uses only values of $\log p$. When $\log p(x, z)$ is differentiable in $z$, its gradient can be used too, provided the randomness of $q$ can be moved out of $\lambda$. A distribution $q(z \mid \lambda)$ is **reparameterizable** if there are a differentiable map $t(\lambda, \varepsilon)$ and a distribution $r(\varepsilon)$ that does not depend on $\lambda$ such that $z = t(\lambda, \varepsilon)$, with $\varepsilon \sim r$, has the distribution $q(z \mid \lambda)$. For a Gaussian, $t = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon$ and $r = \mathcal N(0, I)$.

Since $z = t(\lambda, \varepsilon)$ has the distribution $q$, the expectation of any function $\ell$ of $z$ can be computed over $\varepsilon$ instead (the change of variables in an expectation). Now $\lambda$ sits only inside the integrand, so the derivative moves inside, under the same condition as before but with $r$ fixed, and the chain rule finishes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\lambda \mathbb E_{q}\big[\ell(z)\big]
&= \nabla_\lambda \mathbb E_{r}\big[\ell(t(\lambda, \varepsilon))\big] \\
&= \mathbb E_{r}\big[\nabla_\lambda \ell(t(\lambda, \varepsilon))\big] \quad \text{(exchange)} \\
&= \mathbb E_{r}\big[J_\lambda^\top\, \nabla_z \ell(z)\big] \quad \text{(chain rule)},
\end{aligned}
$$

where $z = t(\lambda, \varepsilon)$ and $J_\lambda = \partial t/\partial \lambda$ is the Jacobian of the map. The estimate is the gradient of $\ell$ at a sample, carried back to $\lambda$.

For the ELBO the integrand $f_\lambda = \log p - \log q$ also depends on $\lambda$ through $\log q$. For a Gaussian that part has a closed form. With $D$ the dimension of $z$, substituting $z_j = \mu_j + \sigma_j \varepsilon_j$, so that $(z_j - \mu_j)/\sigma_j = \varepsilon_j$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q(z \mid \mu, \sigma)
&= -\sum_{j=1}^D \Big(\frac{(z_j - \mu_j)^2}{2\sigma_j^2} + \log \sigma_j\Big) - \tfrac D2 \log 2\pi \\
&= -\sum_{j=1}^D \Big(\tfrac12 \varepsilon_j^2 + \log \sigma_j\Big) - \tfrac D2 \log 2\pi .
\end{aligned}
$$

Averaging with $\mathbb E\varepsilon_j^2 = 1$, $-\mathbb E_q[\log q] = \sum_j \log \sigma_j + \frac D2 (1 + \log 2\pi)$, the entropy of the Gaussian. So

$$
\mathcal L(\lambda) = \mathbb E_\varepsilon\big[\log p(x, \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon)\big] + \sum_j \log \sigma_j + \text{const} .
$$

The first term is the case $\ell = \log p(x, \cdot)$ above, with a diagonal Jacobian, $\partial z_j/\partial \mu_j = 1$ and $\partial z_j / \partial \sigma_j = \varepsilon_j$; the second differentiates to $1/\sigma_j$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\mu \mathcal L &= \mathbb E_\varepsilon\big[\nabla_z \log p(x, z)\big], \\
\nabla_\sigma \mathcal L &= \mathbb E_\varepsilon\big[\nabla_z \log p(x, z) \odot \varepsilon\big] + 1/\sigma ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon$ and $1/\sigma$ taken elementwise. The Monte Carlo version averages over $S$ draws of $\varepsilon$:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\hat\nabla_\mu \mathcal L &= \frac1S \sum_{s=1}^S \nabla_z \log p(x, z_s), \\
z_s &= \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon_s
\end{aligned}
}
$$

This is the **reparameterization gradient** ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), Section 2.4; [Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra, 2014](#references); [Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla, 2014](#references)). The [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#the-reparameterization-trick) introduces the same trick for an encoder; here it is a general-purpose gradient estimator for any differentiable model.

On the toy objective $\mathbb E_q[z^2]$, one sample gives $2z = 2(1 + \varepsilon)$, with variance 4 against the score function's 30. The reparameterization gradient transports the slope of the integrand at the sample, while the score function has to infer the slope from how the values vary across samples. For a linear integrand $c^\top z$ the reparameterization gradient in $\mu$ is $\nabla_z (c^\top z) = c$ for every sample, with zero variance; the score-function sample $(c^\top z)\, \varepsilon/\sigma$ still fluctuates.

At the optimum $\mu = 0.76$ the plain score function has variance $94.7$ and the reparameterization gradient $3.6$, 26 times less. The optimal baseline brings the score function down to $0.20$, below the reparameterization gradient. The reason is exact at the posterior itself. If $q(z) = p(z \mid x)$, then for every $z$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z) &= \log p(x, z) - \log p(z \mid x) \\
&= \log p(x) \quad \text{(product rule of probability)},
\end{aligned}
$$

a constant, and with $a = \log p(x)$ every sample $h(z)(f(z) - a)$ is zero. Near the optimum $f$ is almost constant over the bulk of $q$, and a constant is exactly what a baseline removes. The reparameterization gradient has no such zero: $\nabla_z \log p(x, z)$ still varies with $z$. Away from the optimum the baseline helps less; at $\mu = -1$ the three variances are $523$, $86$ and $2.9$.

Dimension changes the picture. Take Bayesian logistic regression with $d$ weights and 200 points, and sum the variances over the $d$ components of the $\mu$-gradient, measured with 20,000 samples. At the start, $q = \mathcal N(0, I)$:

| $d$ | Score | Score + baseline | Reparameterization |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | $9.5 \cdot 10^4$ | $9.5 \cdot 10^3$ | $2.2 \cdot 10^3$ |
| 10 | $1.0 \cdot 10^6$ | $8.4 \cdot 10^4$ | $5.4 \cdot 10^3$ |
| 50 | $1.8 \cdot 10^7$ | $6.2 \cdot 10^5$ | $9.0 \cdot 10^3$ |

At a $q$ fitted by BBVI:

| $d$ | Score | Score + baseline | Reparameterization |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | $8.2 \cdot 10^5$ | $16.7$ | $67.8$ |

The plain score function is 40 to 2000 times worse than the reparameterization gradient at the start, and 7000 to 12,000 times worse at the fitted $q$, where the score $\varepsilon_j/\sigma_j$ has grown with the shrinking $\sigma$. With a baseline it wins at the fitted $q$ for $d = 2$ and $d = 10$, and loses by a factor of $7.5$ at $d = 50$; at the start it loses by factors of 4, 15 and 70. The baseline is one number per coordinate and cannot follow how the integrand varies along the other $d - 1$ directions. The reparameterization gradient does not need to, because it differentiates.

## The algorithm

1. Initialize $\lambda$; choose a step-size schedule $\rho_t$ that satisfies the [Robbins–Monro conditions](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference#step-sizes-the-robbinsmonro-conditions), or an adaptive rule such as AdaGrad.
2. Draw $S$ samples from $q(z \mid \lambda)$, or $S$ noise vectors $\varepsilon_s$ for the reparameterization gradient.
3. Compute the gradient estimate: the score function with Rao–Blackwellization and a baseline, or the reparameterization gradient.
4. Step: $\lambda \leftarrow \lambda + \rho_t\, \hat\nabla_\lambda \mathcal L$, and repeat from step 2.

With global and local variables, step 3 can also subsample the data, scaling the local terms by $N/B$ as SVI does. The estimate is then **doubly stochastic** ([Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla, 2014](#references)): random in the mini-batch and in the samples of $q$. Each source of randomness has its own one-sample rule, in the spirit of the table of stochastic gradients in the [lecture notes](#references) of the course «Байесовские методы в анализе данных»:

| Objective | Exact gradient | One-sample estimate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\sum_{i=1}^N f_i(\lambda)$ | $\sum_i \nabla f_i(\lambda)$ | $N\, \nabla f_j(\lambda)$, $j \sim \mathrm U\{1, \dots, N\}$ |
| $\mathbb E_{r(\varepsilon)}\, f(\lambda, \varepsilon)$ | $\mathbb E_r\, \nabla_\lambda f(\lambda, \varepsilon)$ | $\nabla_\lambda f(\lambda, \hat\varepsilon)$, $\hat\varepsilon \sim r$ |
| $\mathbb E_{q(z \mid \lambda)}\, f(z)$ | $\mathbb E_q[\nabla_\lambda \log q\; f]$ | $\nabla_\lambda \log q(\hat z \mid \lambda)\, f(\hat z)$, $\hat z \sim q$ |

Each row is unbiased. In the first, $\mathbb E_j[N \nabla f_j] = \sum_i \frac1N N \nabla f_i$; the second exchanges $\nabla$ and $\mathbb E_r$; the third is the score-function identity. When the rows are combined, conditioning on the batch and the tower property $\mathbb E[X] = \mathbb E_j\big[\mathbb E_\varepsilon[X \mid j]\big]$ carry unbiasedness through: the inner expectation gives the exact gradient of the batch term, and the outer one the sum over the data. A batch of $B$ indices averages $B$ such draws. Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla's paper is also the reading for the lecture on non-conjugate variational inference, number 13, in [Vetrov and Kropotov's course](#references). In the Code section, 3000 steps of the reparameterization gradient, with 10 samples each and all 200 points per step, fit the logistic regressions of the tables. Automatic differentiation variational inference ([Kucukelbir et al., 2017](#references)) packages this route for a whole class of models: it maps every constrained latent variable to the real line, places a Gaussian $q$ there and differentiates the log density automatically.

## Amortized inference and the VAE

With one local latent variable per data point, BBVI keeps local variational parameters $\lambda_i = (\mu_i, \sigma_i)$ for every $x_i$ and optimizes them point by point, and again for every new point at test time. **Amortized inference** replaces this table by a function: an **encoder** network with weights $\psi$ outputs $\big(\mu_\psi(x_i), \sigma_\psi(x_i)\big)$, and the ELBO is maximized over $\psi$ instead of over the $\lambda_i$. Inference for a new point then costs one forward pass, and what is learned about inference on some points transfers to the others.

The [variational autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/vae) is exactly this. For each point it maximizes the ELBO

$$
\mathbb E_{q_\psi(z \mid x_i)}\big[\log p_\theta(x_i \mid z)\big] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\psi(z \mid x_i) \,\|\, p(z)\big),
$$

derived in the [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from), with three choices that the previous sections explain:

- a mean-field Gaussian $q$ and the reparameterization gradient, with one sample per point;
- mini-batches of data points, so the estimate is doubly stochastic;
- decoder parameters $\theta$ fitted by maximizing the same ELBO, in the same gradient steps as $\psi$.

[Kingma and Welling (2014)](#references) take mini-batches of 100 points and one sample each (their Algorithm 1). The KL term between two Gaussians is computed in closed form instead of by sampling, which, they note, typically lowers the variance: the same move as Rao–Blackwellization, an expectation computed exactly. [Murphy (2023](#references), Section 10.1.5) calls the scheme amortized stochastic variational EM.

Amortization has a price. The ELBO reached by an encoder can be no higher than the one reached by optimizing every $\lambda_i$ separately, because the encoder's outputs are one particular choice of the $\lambda_i$. [Cremer, Li and Duvenaud (2018)](#references) split the gap between $\log p(x)$ and the ELBO into an **approximation gap**, from the family of $q$, and an **amortization gap**, from the encoder, and found that the amortization gap is often the larger one. Optimizing the $\lambda_i$ of a test point for a few steps, starting from the encoder's output, recovers part of it.

## Where it breaks

The two estimators ask for different things. The score function needs a $q$ that can be sampled and whose score can be computed, so it works for discrete latent variables, non-differentiable likelihoods and simulators. The reparameterization gradient needs $\log p$ differentiable in $z$ and $q$ written as a differentiable transform of fixed noise. [Kingma and Welling (2014](#references), Section 2.4) list three ways: location-scale families, an inverse CDF, and compositions such as a log-normal from a normal.

A discrete variable has no such transform, and trying anyway gives a wrong answer without an error. Let $z \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(\theta)$ with $\theta = 0.3$, and $f(z) = z$, so $\mathbb E_q[f] = \theta$ and the true gradient is 1. The natural sampler is $z = [u < \theta]$ with $u$ uniform. For every $u$ this is a step function of $\theta$, flat except at one point, so its derivative is 0 almost surely. The score function is computed from $\log q(z) = z \log\theta + (1 - z) \log(1 - \theta)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_\theta \log q(z) &= \frac z\theta - \frac{1 - z}{1 - \theta}, \\
g = f(z)\, \partial_\theta \log q(z) &= \frac z\theta \quad \text{(since } z(1 - z) = 0\text{)}, \\
\mathbb E[g] &= \theta \cdot \tfrac1\theta = 1, \\
\mathbb E[g^2] &= \theta \cdot \tfrac1{\theta^2} = \tfrac1\theta .
\end{aligned}
$$

So its single-sample variance is $1/\theta - 1$:

| Estimator | Mean | Single-sample variance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pathwise, through $z = [u < \theta]$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| score function, $f(z)\, \partial_\theta \log q(z)$ | $1.0$ | $1/\theta - 1 = 2.33$ |

The pathwise estimate is confidently wrong: zero variance and the wrong value. Automatic differentiation through a sampled discrete variable does exactly this. Discrete variables need the score function with strong control variates, or a continuous relaxation that trades bias for variance ([Mohamed et al., 2020](#references), Sections 4, 5 and 7, review the options).

Variance remains the bottleneck for everything else. Step sizes must suit the noisiest coordinate, the Monte Carlo ELBO used for monitoring is noisy too, and a stalled objective may only mean that the steps are too small. The family of $q$ is still the limit: a mean-field Gaussian cannot represent correlations or several modes, and BBVI makes richer families usable, such as full-covariance Gaussians or normalizing flows, without choosing them.

## What you should remember

- Score function: $\nabla \mathcal L = \mathbb E_q[\nabla_\lambda \log q \,(\log p - \log q)]$; it needs only samples of $q$ and values of $\log p$.
- Rao–Blackwellization drops the terms outside the Markov blanket; a baseline $a_j = \operatorname{Cov}(g_j, h_j)/\operatorname{Var}(h_j)$ subtracts a constant. Both stay unbiased.
- Reparameterization: $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon$, average $\nabla_z \log p$. Variance 4 against 30 on $\mathbb E[z^2]$; the gap grows with dimension.
- Mini-batches make the estimate doubly stochastic and still unbiased.
- VAE = BBVI + reparameterization + an encoder for the local parameters + a decoder fitted by the same ELBO.
- Through a discrete sample the pathwise gradient is zero and wrong.

The toy gradient of the worked example, the three estimators on the one-weight logistic model of the figure, the comparison across dimensions, Rao–Blackwellization on a model with independent local variables, and the discrete failure. NumPy and SciPy; seeds are fixed, and exact numbers can vary slightly across platforms and library versions. The script runs in about 2 seconds.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.special import gammaln
LOG2PI = np.log(2 * np.pi)

# 1. the toy objective E_q[z^2] with q = N(mu, sigma^2) at mu = sigma = 1: true gradient in mu is 2 mu = 2
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
eps = rng.normal(size=1_000_000); mu, sigma = 1.0, 1.0
z = mu + sigma * eps
g_score = (z - mu) / sigma ** 2 * z ** 2          # score times the function value
g_reparam = 2 * z * 1.0                            # derivative of the function times dz/dmu = 1
print(g_score.mean().round(3), g_score.var().round(2), g_reparam.mean().round(3), g_reparam.var().round(2))
# 2.005 30.12 2.002 4.01   (exact: 2, 30 and 2, 4)

# 2. one weight, eight points: logistic likelihood, prior N(0, 1), q = N(mu, sigma^2); variances by quadrature
x1 = np.array([-2, -1.5, -1, -0.5, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2.0]); y1 = np.array([0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1.0])
e = np.linspace(-9, 9, 6001); we = np.exp(-e ** 2 / 2); we /= we.sum()     # weights of a standard normal grid
logjoint1 = lambda w: (y1 * np.outer(w, x1) - np.logaddexp(0, np.outer(w, x1))).sum(1) - w ** 2 / 2 - LOG2PI / 2
dlogjoint1 = lambda w: ((y1 - 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.outer(w, x1)))) * x1).sum(1) - w
def elbo1(p):
    w = p[0] + np.exp(p[1]) * e
    return (we * (logjoint1(w) + e ** 2 / 2 + p[1] + LOG2PI / 2)).sum()   # E[log p] - E[log q]
opt = minimize(lambda p: -elbo1(p), [0.0, 0.0]).x; mu1, sg1 = opt[0], np.exp(opt[1])
def variances1(mu, sg):
    w = mu + sg * e; f = logjoint1(w) + e ** 2 / 2 + np.log(sg) + LOG2PI / 2   # log p(x, w) - log q(w)
    h = e / sg                                       # score of q with respect to mu
    a = (we * h * f * h).sum() / (we * h * h).sum()  # optimal constant a* = Cov(h f, h) / Var(h)
    var = lambda g: (we * g * g).sum() - (we * g).sum() ** 2
    return var(h * f), var(h * (f - a)), var(dlogjoint1(w))
print(round(mu1, 3), round(sg1, 3), [round(float(v), 2) for v in variances1(mu1, sg1)], [round(float(v), 2) for v in variances1(-1.0, sg1)])
# 0.76 0.535 [94.68, 0.2, 3.6] [523.15, 86.1, 2.87]

# 3. Bayesian logistic regression with d weights, 200 points; mean-field q; single-sample variance of the mu-gradient
def make(d, n=200, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed); X = r.normal(size=(n, d)); w_true = 3 * r.normal(size=d) / np.sqrt(d)
    return X, (r.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ w_true))).astype(float)
def logjoint(W, X, y):
    s = W @ X.T
    return (y * s - np.logaddexp(0, s)).sum(1) - 0.5 * (W ** 2).sum(1) - 0.5 * W.shape[1] * LOG2PI
def grad_logjoint(W, X, y):
    return (y - 1 / (1 + np.exp(-W @ X.T))) @ X - W
def fit(X, y, steps=3000, seed=1):             # BBVI with the reparameterization gradient, 10 samples per step
    d = X.shape[1]; m, log_s = np.zeros(d), np.zeros(d); r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    for t in range(steps):
        E = r.normal(size=(10, d)); s = np.exp(log_s); G = grad_logjoint(m + s * E, X, y)
        lr = 0.01 / (1 + t / 500)
        m += lr * G.mean(0); log_s += lr * ((G * E * s).mean(0) + 1)   # +1: gradient of the entropy in log sigma
    return m, np.exp(log_s)
def variances(X, y, m, s, S=20_000, seed=3):
    E = np.random.default_rng(seed).normal(size=(S, len(m))); W = m + s * E
    f = logjoint(W, X, y) - (-0.5 * E ** 2 - np.log(s) - 0.5 * LOG2PI).sum(1)   # log p(x, w) - log q(w)
    H = E / s                                    # score with respect to each mu_j
    G_score = H * f[:, None]
    a = ((G_score - G_score.mean(0)) * H).mean(0) / (H * H).mean(0)           # one control-variate weight per mu_j
    G_cv = G_score - a * H
    G_rep = grad_logjoint(W, X, y)               # log q(m + s e) does not depend on m, so only log p contributes
    return [G.var(0).sum() for G in (G_score, G_cv, G_rep)]
for d in (2, 10, 50):
    X, y = make(d)
    v0 = variances(X, y, np.zeros(d), np.ones(d))
    v1 = variances(X, y, *fit(X, y))
    print(d, ['%.3g' % v for v in v0], ['%.3g' % v for v in v1])
# 2 ['9.49e+04', '9.48e+03', '2.24e+03'] ['8.23e+05', '16.7', '67.8']
# 10 ['1.03e+06', '8.39e+04', '5.44e+03'] ['1.85e+06', '188', '259']
# 50 ['1.79e+07', '6.24e+05', '8.96e+03'] ['7.02e+06', '6.64e+03', '886']

# 4. Rao-Blackwellization: n independent local variables, z_i ~ N(0, 1), x_i ~ Poisson(exp(z_i)), q(z_i) = N(0, 0.5^2)
r = np.random.default_rng(4); xp = r.poisson(np.exp(r.normal(size=100))); E = r.normal(size=(20_000, 100)); s = 0.5
Z = s * E                                        # samples of q; log p and log q are sums of one term per i
terms = xp * Z - np.exp(Z) - gammaln(xp + 1) - 0.5 * Z ** 2 - 0.5 * LOG2PI - (-0.5 * E ** 2 - np.log(s) - 0.5 * LOG2PI)
h1 = E[:, 0] / s                                 # score with respect to mu_1
for n in (1, 10, 100):
    print(n, round(float((h1 * terms[:, :n].sum(1)).var()), 1), round(float((h1 * terms[:, 0]).var()), 1))
# 1 8.2 8.2
# 10 1920.7 8.2
# 100 374010.7 8.2

# 5. where it breaks: a discrete z ~ Bernoulli(theta), f(z) = z, so E_q[f] = theta and the true gradient is 1
theta, u = 0.3, np.random.default_rng(6).random(1_000_000)
z = (u < theta).astype(float)                    # z = [u < theta]: a step in theta, derivative 0 almost everywhere
g_score = z * (z / theta - (1 - z) / (1 - theta))   # f(z) times the score d/dtheta log q(z)
print(round(g_score.mean(), 3), round(g_score.var(), 2), round(1 / theta - 1, 2))   # the pathwise estimate is 0
# 1.0 2.33 2.33
```
