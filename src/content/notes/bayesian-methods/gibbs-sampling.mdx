The [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference) approximated the posterior by the best factorized distribution. The answer came fast and without noise, and it was wrong in a systematic way: in the normal model the standard deviation of the mean came out as $0.410$ instead of $0.449$, and nothing in the method revealed the error. **Gibbs sampling** uses the same ingredients, the complete conditional of each block given the others, in a different way. Instead of averaging the log density over the other blocks, it draws each block from its conditional at the current values of the others. The draws form a [Markov chain](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo) whose distribution converges to the exact posterior. This note shows why the update is valid and when it is not enough, writes Gibbs samplers for the normal model and for a Bayesian mixture, improves them by blocking and collapsing, and compares the two kinds of error: the bias of variational inference and the variance of sampling.

**Notation.** $p(Z)$ is the target, the posterior $p(Z \mid X)$ with the data left implicit, and $Z = (Z_1, \dots, Z_m)$ its blocks; $Z_{-j}$ are all blocks except $Z_j$, and $p(z_j \mid z_{-j})$ is the **complete conditional** of block $j$. A **sweep** updates every block once. $K(z \to z')$ is a transition density of the chain. ESS is the effective sample size and $\hat R$ the potential scale reduction factor, both of the [MCMC note](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#mixing-and-diagnostics). The normal model, its data $x_1, \dots, x_8$ and its prior $\mu \mid \lambda \sim \mathcal N(0, 1/\lambda)$, $\lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(2, 2)$ are those of the previous note.

## Baseline: Metropolis–Hastings with a random walk

[Metropolis–Hastings](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#metropolis-hastings) samples any density known up to a constant: propose a move, accept it with a probability that corrects for the proposal. With a random-walk proposal it ignores the structure of the model, and that has a price. Its step size must suit every direction at once: in the normal model the posterior standard deviations are $0.449$ for $\mu$ and $0.270$ for $\lambda$, and $\lambda$ must stay positive. In a mixture with 100 labels and a few continuous parameters, a joint random move of everything is almost always rejected, and a single shared step size is wrong for discrete and continuous blocks alike.

The models of this course are built from conjugate pieces. Their complete conditionals, the same ones that gave the closed-form factors of mean field, are standard distributions that can be sampled exactly. Gibbs sampling ([Geman and Geman, 1984](#references); [Gelfand and Smith, 1990](#references)) uses them as proposals that are never rejected.

## The Gibbs update

One sweep of the Gibbs sampler with a **fixed order** of blocks, from the current state $z^{(t)}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_1^{(t+1)} &\sim p\big(z_1 \mid z_2^{(t)}, z_3^{(t)}, \dots, z_m^{(t)}\big), \\
z_2^{(t+1)} &\sim p\big(z_2 \mid z_1^{(t+1)}, z_3^{(t)}, \dots, z_m^{(t)}\big), \\
&\;\;\vdots \\
z_m^{(t+1)} &\sim p\big(z_m \mid z_1^{(t+1)}, \dots, z_{m-1}^{(t+1)}\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Each block is drawn given the **newest** values of the others. A **random-order** sampler instead picks one block at random at each step. The conditional of block $j$ is found exactly as a mean-field factor was, by reading $\log p(X, Z)$ as a function of $Z_j$ alone,

$$
\log p(z_j \mid z_{-j}, X) = \log p(X, z) + \text{const},
$$

but with the other blocks fixed at their current values instead of averaged over $q$. The two methods are close relatives: coordinate ascent variational inference sets a factor to the normalized $\exp \mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(X, Z)]$, while Gibbs draws a value from the normalized $\exp \log p(X, z) = p(X, z)$ at the current values of the others.

Using the newest values is not a detail. Updating every block from the values of the previous sweep, all at once, gives a different chain with a different stationary distribution. For a bivariate normal with correlation $0.9$, the Code section finds a sample correlation of $0.899$ with the sequential update and $-0.009$ with the simultaneous one: the simultaneous chain samples the product of the marginals. The reason is visible in its covariance: $\operatorname{Cov}(z_1', z_2') = \rho^2 \operatorname{Cov}(z_2, z_1)$, whose only fixed point is zero.

## Why it is valid

**Each update leaves the target invariant.** Suppose the current state $z$ is distributed according to $p$. Then $z_{-j}$ has the marginal $p(z_{-j})$, and the new $z_j'$ is drawn from $p(z_j \mid z_{-j})$, so the new state $(z_j', z_{-j})$ has density $p(z_j' \mid z_{-j})\, p(z_{-j}) = p(z_j', z_{-j})$, the target again. In terms of the transition density $K_j$ of the update of block $j$,

$$
\int K_j(z \to z')\, p(z)\, dz = \int p(z_j' \mid z_{-j}')\, p(z_j, z_{-j}')\, dz_j = p(z_j' \mid z_{-j}')\, p(z_{-j}') = p(z'),
$$

since the update does not change $z_{-j}$ ($z_{-j} = z_{-j}'$) and integrating the joint over $z_j$ leaves the marginal. A sweep is a composition of such updates, and a composition of transitions that each preserve $p$ preserves $p$.

**Each update is a Metropolis–Hastings step that is always accepted.** With the proposal $q(z' \mid z) = p(z_j' \mid z_{-j})$ and $z_{-j}' = z_{-j}$, the acceptance ratio is

$$
\frac{p(z')\, q(z \mid z')}{p(z)\, q(z' \mid z)} = \frac{p(z_j' \mid z_{-j})\, p(z_{-j}) \cdot p(z_j \mid z_{-j})}{p(z_j \mid z_{-j})\, p(z_{-j}) \cdot p(z_j' \mid z_{-j})} = 1 .
$$

Each single update therefore satisfies [detailed balance](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#detailed-balance). A systematic sweep, a fixed sequence of reversible steps, is in general not reversible itself; the random-order version is. Neither matters for correctness: invariance is what the chain needs.

**Invariance is not convergence.** The chain must also be able to reach every region of the target from every start, irreducibility, and must not cycle, aperiodicity. Gibbs moves along one block at a time, and that can fail. Let $p$ be uniform on the union of the squares $[0, 1]^2$ and $[-1, 0]^2$, which touch only at a corner. From a point in the first square, the conditional of $z_1$ given $z_2 > 0$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$, and the conditional of $z_2$ given $z_1 > 0$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$: the chain never enters the second square, and in the Code section it spends 0 of 10,000 sweeps there. The uniform distribution on either square is stationary on its own, and a chain started in one square never approaches $p$, the equal mixture of the two. A simple sufficient condition for irreducibility is that the joint density is positive wherever the product of its marginals is, the **positivity condition** ([Robert and Casella, 2004](#references), Chapter 10). The normal model, with its density positive on $\mathbb R \times (0, \infty)$, satisfies it.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Gibbs sampling replaces one hard draw from the joint posterior by a cycle of easy draws from the complete conditionals, each from the newest values of the other blocks. Every draw leaves the posterior invariant, so the chain, once it has forgotten its start, produces samples from the exact posterior. Its errors are random, not systematic: they shrink like one over the square root of the effective number of samples, while the error of mean field stays where it is.</aside>

## A Gibbs sampler for the normal model

The log joint of the normal model, written in the previous note, has the terms in $\mu$

$$
-\frac\lambda2 \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 + \kappa_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big),
$$

a quadratic in $\mu$ with leading coefficient $-\frac{(\kappa_0 + n)\lambda}{2}$, and the terms in $\lambda$

$$
\Big(a_0 - 1 + \frac{n + 1}{2}\Big) \log \lambda - \lambda \Big(b_0 + \frac12 \Big[\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 + \kappa_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big]\Big) .
$$

The complete conditionals are therefore

$$
\mu \mid \lambda, X \sim \mathcal N\Big(\mu_n,\; \frac{1}{(\kappa_0 + n)\lambda}\Big), \qquad \lambda \mid \mu, X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\Big(a_0 + \frac{n + 1}{2},\; b_0 + \frac12 \Big[\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 + \kappa_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big]\Big),
$$

with $\mu_n = (\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n\bar x)/(\kappa_0 + n) = 0.478$. These are the mean-field factors of the previous note with $\mathbb E[\lambda]$ replaced by the current $\lambda$ and $\mathbb E_\mu[\cdot]$ by the value at the current $\mu$.

**Worked example.** Start at $(\mu, \lambda) = (1.7, 1.7)$. The conditional of $\mu$ is a normal with mean $0.478$ and precision $9 \cdot 1.7 = 15.3$, standard deviation $0.256$; the draw is $\mu = 0.87$. At this $\mu$ the conditional of $\lambda$ is $\mathrm{Gamma}(6.5, 9.78)$, with mean $0.66$; the draw is $\lambda = 0.91$. One sweep has moved the chain from the edge of the posterior into its bulk.

<GibbsSteps />

Four chains of 10,000 sweeps from the dispersed starts $(-0.5, 0.3)$, $(1.5, 0.3)$, $(-0.5, 1.6)$ and $(1.5, 1.6)$, with the first 1,000 sweeps of each discarded, give a posterior mean of $\mu$ of $0.478$, a standard deviation of $0.452$ and $\mathbb E[\lambda] = 0.660$, against the exact $0.478$, $0.449$ and $0.661$. The Monte Carlo error of the standard deviation is about $0.449/\sqrt{2 \cdot 36{,}000} = 0.002$, so the difference of $0.003$ is noise of the expected size, and unlike the $0.039$ of mean field it shrinks as the chains get longer.

This sampler mixes unusually well. The conditional mean of $\mu$ is $\mu_n$ whatever $\lambda$ is, so successive draws of $\mu$ are uncorrelated, lag-1 autocorrelation $-0.002$, and each chain's 9,000 draws of $\mu$ are worth 9,000 independent ones; for $\lambda$ the lag-1 autocorrelation is $0.05$ and the effective sample sizes are between 7,737 and 9,000. When the posterior couples the blocks strongly, Gibbs is slow, as the zigzag of the [MCMC note](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#gibbs-sampling) shows for a correlated Gaussian: successive draws have correlation $\rho^2$, the same factor by which mean field's means converge.

## Blocked and collapsed Gibbs

Two changes of what counts as a block make a Gibbs sampler mix faster.

**Blocking** draws several coupled blocks jointly from their joint conditional. In the limit of one block the sampler draws from the posterior directly, and successive samples are independent. For the normal model the joint posterior is a normal–gamma, so the blocked sampler draws $\lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_n, b_n)$ and then $\mu \mid \lambda$: the Code section's 9,000 draws give a standard deviation of $0.448$ and an effective sample size of 9,000. For a correlated Gaussian, drawing both coordinates together removes the $\rho^2$ autocorrelation entirely. Blocking pays when the joint conditional of a group is still easy to sample and the group is strongly coupled inside.

**Collapsing** integrates some variables out analytically and runs Gibbs on the rest: from $p(z_1, z_2, z_3)$ it samples $p(z_1, z_2) = \int p(z_1, z_2, z_3)\, dz_3$ by its conditionals. [Liu (1994)](#references), extending [Liu, Wong and Kong (1994)](#references), orders the three schemes by the norms of their forward operators, a measure of how fast the chains forget their start: collapsing is no worse than grouping $z_2$ and $z_3$ into one block, which is no worse than updating all three one at a time. The variables integrated out are not lost: given the sampled ones they can be drawn from their conditional, and expectations of functions of them can be estimated by averaging their conditional expectations, which has smaller variance than averaging draws (Rao–Blackwellization).

### A collapsed sampler for a Bayesian mixture

*Optional depth: the derivation of the collapsed conditional. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

Take a mixture of $K$ Gaussians in one dimension with known variance $\sigma^2$, weights $\pi \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha, \dots, \alpha)$, means $\mu_k \sim \mathcal N(0, s_0^2)$, labels $z_i \mid \pi \sim \mathrm{Cat}(\pi)$ and $x_i \mid z_i, \mu \sim \mathcal N(\mu_{z_i}, \sigma^2)$. The **standard sampler** cycles three conditionals, each conjugate: with $n_k$ the number of points with label $k$,

$$
\pi \mid z \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha + n_1, \dots, \alpha + n_K), \qquad \mu_k \mid z, x \sim \mathcal N(m_k, v_k), \qquad p(z_i = k \mid \pi, \mu, x_i) \propto \pi_k\, \mathcal N(x_i \mid \mu_k, \sigma^2),
$$

with $v_k = (1/s_0^2 + n_k/\sigma^2)^{-1}$ and $m_k = v_k \sum_{i: z_i = k} x_i / \sigma^2$, the normal–normal update of the [conjugate priors note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors). Given $\pi$ and $\mu$ the labels are independent, so all of them are drawn at once.

The **collapsed sampler** integrates $\pi$ and $\mu$ out and samples the labels alone, one at a time. By Bayes' formula, $p(z_i = k \mid z_{-i}, x) \propto p(z_i = k \mid z_{-i})\, p(x_i \mid z_i = k, z_{-i}, x_{-i})$. The first factor is the predictive probability of the Dirichlet–categorical model, a count plus a pseudo-count; the second is the posterior predictive density of $x_i$ under component $k$ given the other points with that label, a normal whose variance adds the uncertainty about $\mu_k$ to the noise:

$$
p(z_i = k \mid z_{-i}, x) \;\propto\; \frac{n_k^{-i} + \alpha}{n - 1 + K\alpha}\; \mathcal N\big(x_i \mid m_k^{-i},\; \sigma^2 + v_k^{-i}\big),
$$

where $n_k^{-i}$, $m_k^{-i}$ and $v_k^{-i}$ are computed without point $i$. The labels are no longer conditionally independent, which is why they are updated in turn; the counts make the update cheap.

**Worked example.** In the Code section, 120 points come from three unit-variance clusters centred at $-2.5$, $0$ and $2.5$, with $K = 3$, $\alpha = 1$, $s_0^2 = 25$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$. Both samplers start with every point in one cluster and run 2,000 sweeps. Component labels can permute during the run, so the monitored quantity must not depend on them: the size of the cluster that contains the first point. After 200 sweeps of burn-in, its 1,800 values are worth 56 independent ones under the standard sampler and 213 under the collapsed one. The averages, 42.3 and 40.1 points, carry the large noise of so few effective samples. The comparison is per sweep, and a collapsed sweep visits the points one at a time. The same collapsed sampler, with the Dirichlet weights replaced by a Dirichlet process, fits the infinite mixtures of the [Dirichlet processes note](/notes/bayesian-methods/dirichlet-processes), and for topic models it is the standard way to fit [latent Dirichlet allocation](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation) ([Griffiths and Steyvers, 2004](#references)).

## Checking convergence

The [MCMC note](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#mixing-and-diagnostics) introduces the tools; four habits make them work for Gibbs samplers.

1. **Several chains from dispersed starts.** Discard a burn-in, then compare the chains. The **split $\hat R$**, a version of the potential scale reduction factor of [Gelman and Rubin (1992)](#references), cuts each of $M$ chains in half and compares the $2M$ halves of length $h$: with $W$ the average within-half variance and $B$ the variance of the half means times $h$, it is $\hat R = \sqrt{\big(\frac{h - 1}{h} W + \frac1h B\big)/W}$, which approaches 1 when the halves agree. [Vehtari et al. (2021)](#references) recommend a rank-normalized version and treating $\hat R \ge 1.01$ or a total effective sample size below 400 as a sign of trouble. The four chains of the normal model give $\hat R = 1.0000$ for both $\mu$ and $\lambda$.
2. **Monitor what the labels cannot change.** In mixtures and other models with symmetric labels, $\hat R$ of a component mean measures label switching, not convergence; use cluster sizes, co-assignment of two points or the log likelihood.
3. **Report effective sample sizes, not sweeps.** 1,800 sweeps of the standard mixture sampler above were worth 56 independent samples.
4. **Remember what no diagnostic sees.** A region the chains never reach leaves no trace in their output. The chain on two squares looks perfectly converged in the first square. Starting chains in different modes, or knowing the posterior has symmetries, is the only defence.

## Gibbs or variational inference: bias against variance

The mean squared error of an estimate splits into a squared bias and a variance. The two methods of this section sit at the two ends of that split.

<BiasVariance />

Mean field reports the posterior standard deviation of $\mu$ as $0.410$ at once and with no randomness at all; its error, $0.039$, is a bias, and it stays the same however long the algorithm runs. The Gibbs estimates have no bias in the limit; their error is random, between $0.28$ and $0.53$ after 10 sweeps and between $0.443$ and $0.449$ after 10,000, shrinking roughly like $1/\sqrt n$. For a short budget the deterministic answer can be closer; for any precision beyond its bias, sampling wins by running longer.

| | Mean-field variational inference | Gibbs sampling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uses | $\mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(X, Z)]$ for each block | draws from $p(z_j \mid z_{-j}, X)$ for each block |
| Result | a distribution $q$ in closed form | correlated samples from the exact posterior, in the limit |
| Error | bias: too narrow, one mode, correlations lost | variance: about $\sigma/\sqrt{\mathrm{ESS}}$ |
| More computation | no improvement after convergence | the error keeps shrinking |
| Evidence | a lower bound $\mathcal L(q)$ | needs a further method, for example [Chib (1995)](#references) |
| Convergence check | the bound stops increasing | several chains, $\hat R$, ESS |
| Scales to | large data, with the [stochastic version](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference) later in the course | moderate data; each sweep visits every latent variable |
| Normal model, sd of $\mu$ | $0.410$ | $0.452$ from $4 \times 9{,}000$ draws (exact $0.449$) |

In practice the two are used together: variational inference to fit large models quickly, and a long Gibbs or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo run on a smaller problem to measure how far its uncertainty is off.

## What changed

| | Random-walk Metropolis–Hastings | Gibbs sampling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proposal | a random move of everything | an exact draw of one block from its complete conditional |
| Acceptance | a probability, tuned through the step size | always 1 |
| Tuning | step size, per direction | the choice of blocks |
| Needs | the density up to a constant | conditionals that can be sampled |
| Fails when | the step suits no direction | blocks are strongly coupled, or the support is disconnected along the axes |
| Improvements | better proposals: MALA, HMC | blocking, collapsing |

## Limitations

- **Coupled blocks mix slowly.** Moves along one block at a time crawl through strongly correlated posteriors; blocking or reparametrizing helps, but only when the joint conditional of the group is still easy.
- **Conditionals must be available.** Without conjugacy the conditionals need their own samplers, Metropolis steps inside Gibbs or slice sampling, and the simplicity is gone.
- **Multimodality.** A chain can stay in one mode for its whole run, and no diagnostic computed from that chain shows it; mixtures and models with symmetric labels are the usual cases.
- **Sequential and slow on large data.** Every sweep visits every latent variable, and collapsed samplers update them one at a time; the cost grows with the data, where [stochastic variational methods](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference) scale better.
- **Monte Carlo error is real.** Results come with noise that must be estimated, through effective sample sizes, and reported.

## What you should remember

- Gibbs sampling draws each block from its complete conditional $p(z_j \mid z_{-j})$, always from the newest values of the others; the conditionals are read off $\log p(X, Z)$ as for the mean-field factors, without the expectation.
- Each update leaves the target invariant, $(z_j', z_{-j}) \sim p(z_j' \mid z_{-j})\, p(z_{-j}) = p$, and is a Metropolis–Hastings step with acceptance 1.
- Invariance needs irreducibility to become convergence; a support disconnected along the axes breaks it, positivity of the density guarantees it.
- Blocking samples coupled blocks jointly; collapsing integrates variables out, and mixes at least as fast as blocking, which mixes at least as fast as one-at-a-time updates.
- Check convergence with several dispersed chains, split $\hat R$ below 1.01 and effective sample sizes, on quantities that do not depend on labels.
- Mean field has a fixed bias and no noise; Gibbs has no bias in the limit and noise that shrinks like $1/\sqrt{\mathrm{ESS}}$.

## Code

Gibbs for the normal model with four chains, split $\hat R$ and effective sample sizes, the blocked sampler, a reducible target, the bug of simultaneous updates, and the standard and collapsed samplers for a Bayesian mixture. NumPy and SciPy only; seeds are fixed, and exact numbers can differ across platforms and library versions. The script runs in about 12 seconds, almost all of it in the collapsed sampler, whose inner loop visits the points one at a time.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp

def ess(a):                                  # effective sample size, autocorrelations summed until they drop below 0.05
    a = a - a.mean(); n = len(a)
    r = np.correlate(a, a, 'full')[n - 1:] / (a.var() * n)
    cut = np.argmax(r[1:] < 0.05) + 1
    return n / (1 + 2 * r[1:cut].sum())
def split_rhat(chains):                      # chains: m x n; each chain split in two halves
    h = chains.shape[1] // 2
    c = np.vstack([chains[:, :h], chains[:, h:2 * h]])
    B, W = h * c.mean(axis=1).var(ddof=1), c.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean()
    return np.sqrt(((h - 1) / h * W + B / h) / W)

# the normal model of the variational-inference note
x = np.array([3.0, -1.6, 1.4, 0.4, 0.5, 0.8, -1.0, 0.8])
N, mu0, l0, a0, b0 = len(x), 0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 2.0
mN = (l0 * mu0 + x.sum()) / (l0 + N)
aE, bE = a0 + N / 2, b0 + 0.5 * ((x - x.mean()) ** 2).sum() + l0 * N * (x.mean() - mu0) ** 2 / (2 * (l0 + N))

def gibbs(start, sweeps, rng):
    mu, tau = start
    out = np.empty((sweeps, 2))
    for s in range(sweeps):
        mu = rng.normal(mN, 1 / np.sqrt((l0 + N) * tau))                                   # mu | tau, x
        b = b0 + 0.5 * (((x - mu) ** 2).sum() + l0 * (mu - mu0) ** 2)
        tau = rng.gamma(a0 + (N + 1) / 2, 1 / b)                                           # tau | mu, x
        out[s] = mu, tau
    return out
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
starts = [(-0.5, 0.3), (1.5, 0.3), (-0.5, 1.6), (1.5, 1.6)]
ch = np.stack([gibbs(s, 10000, rng) for s in starts])                                       # 4 x 10000 x 2
keep = ch[:, 1000:]                                                                          # discard 1000 sweeps of burn-in
print(keep[..., 0].mean().round(3), keep[..., 0].std().round(3), keep[..., 1].mean().round(3))   # 0.478 0.452 0.66
print(round(mN, 3), round(np.sqrt(bE / ((aE - 1) * (l0 + N))), 3), round(aE / bE, 3))       # 0.478 0.449 0.661: exact
print([f"{split_rhat(keep[..., j]):.4f}" for j in (0, 1)], [int(ess(c[:, 0])) for c in keep], [int(ess(c[:, 1])) for c in keep])
# ['1.0000', '1.0000'] [9000, 9000, 9000, 9000] [9000, 7937, 7737, 7917]
print([round(float(np.corrcoef(keep[0, :-1, j], keep[0, 1:, j])[0, 1]), 3) for j in (0, 1)])   # [-0.002, 0.05]: lag-1 autocorrelations

# blocking mu and tau: the joint conditional is the whole normal-gamma posterior, so the draws are independent
tau = rng.gamma(aE, 1 / bE, size=9000); mu = rng.normal(mN, 1 / np.sqrt((l0 + N) * tau))
print(mu.std().round(3), int(ess(mu)))                                                       # 0.448 9000

# a reducible target: uniform on two squares that touch only at a corner; Gibbs never leaves the first
z = np.array([0.5, 0.5]); visits = 0
for s in range(10000):
    z[0] = rng.uniform(0, 1) if z[1] > 0 else rng.uniform(-1, 0)
    z[1] = rng.uniform(0, 1) if z[0] > 0 else rng.uniform(-1, 0)
    visits += z[0] < 0
print(visits)                                                                                # 0

# a common bug: updating both coordinates of a bivariate normal (rho = 0.9) from the old values, not the newest
rho, z1, z2, seq, par = 0.9, 0.0, 0.0, [], []
for s in range(20000):
    z1 = rng.normal(rho * z2, np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2)); z2 = rng.normal(rho * z1, np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2)); seq.append((z1, z2))
y1, y2 = 0.0, 0.0
for s in range(20000):
    y1, y2 = rng.normal(rho * y2, np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2)), rng.normal(rho * y1, np.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2)); par.append((y1, y2))
print([round(float(np.corrcoef(np.array(c).T)[0, 1]), 3) for c in (seq, par)])              # [0.899, -0.009]

# a mixture of three unit-variance Gaussians in 1-D: pi ~ Dir(1, 1, 1), mu_k ~ N(0, 25)
K, s2, t2, alpha = 3, 1.0, 25.0, 1.0
xm = np.concatenate([rng.normal(-2.5, 1, 40), rng.normal(0, 1, 40), rng.normal(2.5, 1, 40)]); n = len(xm)
stat = lambda z: (z == z[0]).sum()          # size of the cluster of the first point: does not depend on the labels
def standard(T, g):                          # pi, mu and z in turn
    z, mu, out = np.zeros(n, int), np.zeros(K), []
    for t in range(T):
        nk = np.bincount(z, minlength=K)
        pi = g.dirichlet(alpha + nk)
        for k in range(K):
            v = 1 / (1 / t2 + nk[k] / s2); mu[k] = g.normal(v * xm[z == k].sum() / s2, np.sqrt(v))
        lw = np.log(pi) - 0.5 * (xm[:, None] - mu) ** 2 / s2
        p = np.exp(lw - logsumexp(lw, axis=1, keepdims=True))
        z = (p.cumsum(axis=1) < g.random(n)[:, None]).sum(axis=1)
        out.append(stat(z))
    return np.array(out)
def collapsed(T, g):                         # pi and mu integrated out: z_i | z_-i, one point at a time
    z = np.zeros(n, int); nk = np.bincount(z, minlength=K).astype(float); sk = np.array([xm[z == k].sum() for k in range(K)]); out = []
    for t in range(T):
        for i in range(n):
            nk[z[i]] -= 1; sk[z[i]] -= xm[i]
            v = 1 / (1 / t2 + nk / s2); m = v * sk / s2                   # posterior of mu_k from the other points
            lw = np.log(nk + alpha) + (-0.5 * np.log(s2 + v) - 0.5 * (xm[i] - m) ** 2 / (s2 + v))
            z[i] = g.choice(K, p=np.exp(lw - logsumexp(lw)))
            nk[z[i]] += 1; sk[z[i]] += xm[i]
        out.append(stat(z))
    return np.array(out)
for f in (standard, collapsed):
    a = f(2000, np.random.default_rng(1))
    print(f.__name__, a[:6], a[200:].mean().round(1), int(ess(a[200:])))
# standard [20 33 40 51 51 55] 42.3 56
# collapsed [91 51 54 53 59 53] 40.1 213
```
