Suppose we want topics for 3.8 million Wikipedia articles. Coordinate-ascent variational inference, as the [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation) ran it, first updates the topic proportions of every article and only then the topics. One update of the topics, the quantity we came for, waits for 3.8 million inner loops, and each of those loops was fitted to topics that are still close to random.

The waste shows on a much smaller model. Take a Bayesian mixture of eight Gaussians in the plane and 200,000 points. After one full pass of coordinate ascent the evidence lower bound is still 0.95 nats per point below its optimum, and five passes are needed to get within 0.01. Each pass touches every point to move eight means a little.

A hundred random points already say roughly where the means are. Can the shared parameters move after every hundred points instead of after all of them? **Stochastic variational inference** (SVI) of [Hoffman et al. (2013)](#references) does this. It keeps the model, the variational family and the objective of [variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference), and changes only how the objective is climbed.

## Global and local variables

SVI applies to models with two kinds of unknowns. A **global** latent variable $\beta$ is shared by all data points: the means of a mixture, the topics of LDA. A **local** latent variable $z_i$ belongs to one data point $x_i$: its cluster, or a document's topic proportions and word assignments. The joint density is

$$
p(\beta) \prod_{i=1}^N p(x_i, z_i \mid \beta) .
$$

The mean-field family is $q(\beta \mid \lambda) \prod_i q(z_i \mid \phi_i)$, with a global variational parameter $\lambda$ and local ones $\phi_i$. Under it the ELBO separates into a global term and a sum of local terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\lambda, \phi) ={}& \mathbb E_q[\log p(\beta)] - \mathbb E_q[\log q(\beta \mid \lambda)] \\
&+ \sum_{i=1}^N \Big( \mathbb E_q[\log p(x_i, z_i \mid \beta)] - \mathbb E_q[\log q(z_i \mid \phi_i)] \Big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The $i$-th local term involves only $x_i$, $\phi_i$ and $\lambda$. So for a fixed $\lambda$ each $\phi_i$ can be optimized on its own, and its optimum $\phi_i(\lambda)$ depends on nothing else. What remains is a function of the global parameter alone, $\mathcal L(\lambda) = \max_\phi \mathcal L(\lambda, \phi)$, and this is the objective SVI climbs. Its gradient is the partial gradient of $\mathcal L(\lambda, \phi)$ at $\phi = \phi(\lambda)$. By the chain rule, assuming the optimum $\phi(\lambda)$ is differentiable in $\lambda$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\, \mathcal L\big(\lambda, \phi(\lambda)\big)
&= \nabla_\lambda \mathcal L + \Big(\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \lambda}\Big)^{\!\top} \nabla_\phi \mathcal L \\
&= \nabla_\lambda \mathcal L ,
\end{aligned}
$$

because $\nabla_\phi \mathcal L = 0$ at an optimum in $\phi$. This is the envelope theorem.

The second ingredient is conjugacy. In the models of this course the **complete conditional** of $\beta$, its distribution given everything else, is in an [exponential family](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#natural-parameters-and-sufficient-statistics) whose natural parameter is the prior's plus one term per data point:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\beta \mid x, z) &= h(\beta) \exp\big(\eta_g(x, z)^\top u(\beta) - A_g(\eta_g(x, z))\big), \\
\eta_g(x, z) &= \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^N s(x_i, z_i) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $u(\beta)$ is the sufficient statistic, $A_g$ the log-normalizer, $\alpha$ the natural parameter of the prior and $s(x_i, z_i)$ the statistics one point contributes. The global factor $q(\beta \mid \lambda)$ is taken from the same family, with natural parameter $\lambda$, and the mean-field update of the [variational inference note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference#mean-field-and-the-general-update) sets it to the expected natural parameter, $\lambda = \mathbb E_\phi[\eta_g(x, z)]$.

### The running example: a Bayesian mixture

The mixture of [Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe (2017)](#references), Section 3, has $K$ means $\mu_k \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_0^2 I)$ in $\mathbb R^D$, an assignment $c_i$ uniform over $\{1, \dots, K\}$, and $x_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu_{c_i}, I)$. The family is $q(\mu_k) = \mathcal N(m_k, P_k^{-1} I)$, with mean $m_k$ and precision $P_k$, and $q(c_i = k) = \phi_{ik}$. Coordinate ascent alternates two updates:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi_{ik} &\propto \exp\Big(x_i^\top m_k - \tfrac12 \big(\lVert m_k \rVert^2 + D/P_k\big)\Big), \\
P_k &= \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \sum_{i=1}^N \phi_{ik}, \\
P_k m_k &= \sum_{i=1}^N \phi_{ik}\, x_i .
\end{aligned}
$$

Both follow from the mean-field rule $\log q_j^* = \mathbb E_{-j}[\log p] + \text{const}$ of the [variational inference note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference#mean-field-and-the-general-update). The local update is the expected log likelihood of the point under component $k$, and the global update is the conjugate posterior of $\mu_k$ in which point $x_i$ counts with weight $\phi_{ik}$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the two updates</summary>

For the label, only the likelihood term of point $i$ depends on $c_i$, and the uniform prior adds a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q^*(c_i = k)
&= \mathbb E_q\big[\log \mathcal N(x_i \mid \mu_k, I)\big] + \text{const} \\
&= x_i^\top \mathbb E_q[\mu_k] - \tfrac12 \mathbb E_q \lVert \mu_k \rVert^2 + \text{const} \\
&= x_i^\top m_k - \tfrac12 \big(\lVert m_k \rVert^2 + D/P_k\big) + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line drops $-\frac12 \lVert x_i \rVert^2$ and the $2\pi$ term, which do not involve $k$. The third uses that a second moment is the squared mean plus the variance, here summed over $D$ coordinates of variance $1/P_k$. For a mean, each label $c_i$ contributes the term of component $k$ with probability $\phi_{ik}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q^*(\mu_k)
&= -\frac{\lVert \mu_k \rVert^2}{2\sigma_0^2} + \sum_{i} \phi_{ik} \Big(x_i^\top \mu_k - \tfrac12 \lVert \mu_k \rVert^2\Big) + \text{const} \\
&= -\tfrac12 P_k \lVert \mu_k \rVert^2 + \mu_k^\top \sum_{i} \phi_{ik}\, x_i + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

A quadratic in $\mu_k$ with coefficient $-\frac12 P_k$ is the log density of a Gaussian with precision $P_k$; completing the square puts its mean at $m_k$ with $P_k m_k = \sum_i \phi_{ik} x_i$.

</details> In the coordinates $(P_k m_k, P_k)$, a linear transform of the natural parameters, the prior contributes $\alpha = (0, 1/\sigma_0^2)$ and point $i$ contributes $(x_i, 1)$ to component $c_i$.

## The natural gradient of the ELBO

Fix the local parameters and look at $\mathcal L$ as a function of $\lambda$. Only the terms with $\beta$ matter, and $\log p(x, z, \beta) = \log p(\beta \mid x, z) + \log p(x, z)$, where the second term does not involve $\beta$. Write $\bar\eta = \mathbb E_\phi[\eta_g(x, z)]$ for the expected natural parameter of the complete conditional. Substituting both exponential-family densities, the base measures $h(\beta)$ cancel, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\lambda)
&= \mathbb E_q[\log p(\beta \mid x, z)] - \mathbb E_q[\log q(\beta \mid \lambda)] + \text{const} \\
&= \bar\eta^\top \mathbb E_q[u(\beta)] - \lambda^\top \mathbb E_q[u(\beta)] + A_g(\lambda) + \text{const} \\
&= \bar\eta^\top \nabla A_g(\lambda) - \lambda^\top \nabla A_g(\lambda) + A_g(\lambda) + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line substitutes the two densities. Mean field makes $q(\beta)$ and $q(z)$ independent, so $\mathbb E_q[\eta_g^\top u(\beta)] = \bar\eta^\top \mathbb E_q[u(\beta)]$, and the term $\mathbb E_q[A_g(\eta_g(x, z))]$ depends on $\phi$ but not on $\lambda$ and joins the constant. The third line uses $\mathbb E_q[u(\beta)] = \nabla A_g(\lambda)$, since $q$ has natural parameter $\lambda$; the [conjugate priors note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#derivatives-of-the-log-partition-function) derives it by differentiating the normalization of the density. Differentiating by the product rule,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\lambda \mathcal L
&= \nabla^2 A_g(\lambda)\, \bar\eta - \nabla A_g(\lambda) - \nabla^2 A_g(\lambda)\, \lambda + \nabla A_g(\lambda) \\
&= \nabla^2 A_g(\lambda)\, (\bar\eta - \lambda) .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is equation 14 of [Hoffman et al. (2013)](#references). The factor $\nabla^2 A_g(\lambda)$ is the covariance of $u(\beta)$ under $q$, and it is also the [Fisher information](/notes/probability/maximum-likelihood#fisher-information) of $q(\beta \mid \lambda)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G(\lambda)
&= \mathbb E_q\big[\nabla_\lambda \log q \; \nabla_\lambda \log q^\top\big] \\
&= \mathbb E_q\big[(u - \nabla A_g)(u - \nabla A_g)^\top\big] \\
&= \nabla^2 A_g(\lambda) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the definition. The second differentiates $\log q = \log h(\beta) + \lambda^\top u(\beta) - A_g(\lambda)$, which gives $\nabla_\lambda \log q = u(\beta) - \nabla A_g(\lambda)$. The third is the identity $\operatorname{Cov}_q[u] = \nabla^2 A_g(\lambda)$ of the same [section on the log-partition function](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#derivatives-of-the-log-partition-function).

The **natural gradient** ([Amari, 1998](#references)) premultiplies the gradient by $G(\lambda)^{-1}$, and the factor cancels:

$$
\boxed{\;\hat\nabla_\lambda \mathcal L = G(\lambda)^{-1}\, \nabla_\lambda \mathcal L = \mathbb E_\phi[\eta_g(x, z)] - \lambda\;}
$$

(Hoffman et al., equation 22). The inverse exists when no linear combination of the statistics $u(\beta)$ is constant under $q$, as in the minimal families used here: then the covariance $\nabla^2 A_g$ is positive definite. A natural-gradient step of length $\rho$ moves $\lambda$ to $(1 - \rho)\lambda + \rho\,\bar\eta$, and with $\rho = 1$ it lands on $\bar\eta$, the coordinate-ascent update. So coordinate ascent on the global parameter is natural-gradient ascent with step 1 ([Sato, 2001](#references)). The natural gradient is also the cheaper one: no matrix is formed or inverted.

**Worked example.** One mean $\mu$ with prior $\mathcal N(0, 100)$, and ten points with average 2 and noise variance 1. The factor $q(\mu) = \mathcal N(m, s^2)$ has statistics $u(\mu) = (\mu, \mu^2)$ and natural parameters $\lambda = (m/s^2, -1/(2s^2))$. The complete conditional is the exact posterior, with precision $0.01 + 10 = 10.01$ and natural parameter $\bar\eta = (20, -10.01/2)$. We start at $m = 0$, $s = 1$:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| start $\lambda$ | $(0, -0.5)$ |
| natural gradient $\bar\eta - \lambda$ | $(20, -4.505)$ |
| Fisher information: the covariance of $(\mu, \mu^2)$ under $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ | $\operatorname{diag}(1, 2)$ |
| ordinary gradient | $(20, -9.01)$ |
| after a step of 1 along the natural gradient | mean $1.998$, precision $10.01$ |
| after a step of 1 along the ordinary gradient | mean $1.052$, precision $19.02$ |

The Fisher information is the covariance of $u = (\mu, \mu^2)$ under $\mathcal N(0, 1)$: $\operatorname{Var}\mu = 1$, $\operatorname{Cov}(\mu, \mu^2) = \mathbb E\mu^3 = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}\mu^2 = \mathbb E\mu^4 - (\mathbb E\mu^2)^2 = 3 - 1 = 2$. The ordinary gradient is $G\,(\bar\eta - \lambda) = (20, 2 \cdot (-4.505))$. A step of 1 along it gives $\lambda = (20, -9.51)$, so precision $-2\lambda_2 = 19.02$ and mean $\lambda_1/19.02 = 1.052$.

The natural step lands on the posterior. The ordinary gradient points elsewhere, and since $(20, -9.01)$ is not a multiple of $(20, -4.505)$, no step size along it reaches the posterior.

### Why measure steps by the Fisher information

*Optional depth: the geometry behind the natural gradient. The rest of the note uses only its formula.*

The ordinary gradient is the steepest direction when a step is measured by its Euclidean length $\lVert d\lambda \rVert$. That length says little about how much the distribution changes. For two normals with the same variance $s^2$ and means $a$ and $b$, averaging the difference of the log densities over $x \sim \mathcal N(a, s^2)$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
&= \frac{\mathbb E\big[(x - b)^2 - (x - a)^2\big]}{2s^2} \\
&= \frac{s^2 + (a - b)^2 - s^2}{2s^2} = \frac{(a - b)^2}{2s^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Moving the mean of $\mathcal N(0, 10^2)$ by 1 gives a distribution that is hard to tell from the first, with a KL divergence of $1/(2 \cdot 100) = 0.005$. Moving the mean of $\mathcal N(0, 0.1^2)$ by 1 gives one that barely overlaps it, with a divergence of 50. The two steps have the same Euclidean length.

A step can instead be measured by the change of the distribution. In an exponential family, $\log q_\lambda - \log q_{\lambda + d\lambda} = A_g(\lambda + d\lambda) - A_g(\lambda) - d\lambda^\top u(\beta)$. Averaging over $q_\lambda$, with $\mathbb E[u] = \nabla A_g(\lambda)$, leaves the remainder of a Taylor expansion of $A_g$, so the [KL divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#kullbackleibler-divergence) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\lambda \,\|\, q_{\lambda + d\lambda}\big)
&= A_g(\lambda + d\lambda) - A_g(\lambda) - d\lambda^\top \nabla A_g(\lambda) \\
&= \tfrac12\, d\lambda^\top \nabla^2 A_g(\lambda)\, d\lambda + O(\lVert d\lambda \rVert^3) \\
&= \tfrac12\, d\lambda^\top G(\lambda)\, d\lambda + O(\lVert d\lambda \rVert^3) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The linear term cancels because the score has mean zero, and the quadratic term is the Fisher information. Maximizing the linear change $\nabla \mathcal L^\top d\lambda$ subject to $\tfrac12\, d\lambda^\top G\, d\lambda = \epsilon$ with a [Lagrange multiplier](/notes/math/lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt) $\nu$ gives the stationarity condition $\nabla \mathcal L = \nu\, G\, d\lambda$, so $d\lambda = \nu^{-1} G^{-1} \nabla \mathcal L$.

So the natural gradient is the steepest ascent per unit of KL divergence. It also does not depend on how $\lambda$ is parameterized. Write $\lambda = \lambda(\theta)$ with an invertible Jacobian $J = \partial\lambda/\partial\theta$. By the chain rule $\nabla_\theta \mathcal L = J^\top \nabla_\lambda \mathcal L$, and the same rule inside the definition of $G$ gives $G_\theta = J^\top G_\lambda J$. The natural step in $\theta$, mapped back to $\lambda$, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
J\, G_\theta^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal L
&= J\, J^{-1} G_\lambda^{-1} J^{-\top} J^\top \nabla_\lambda \mathcal L \\
&= G_\lambda^{-1} \nabla_\lambda \mathcal L ,
\end{aligned}
$$

the same change of the distribution. [Hoffman et al. (2013](#references), Section 2.3) use the symmetrized divergence, which agrees with this one to second order: the reverse direction has the quadratic term $\frac12 d\lambda^\top G(\lambda + d\lambda)\, d\lambda$, and $G(\lambda + d\lambda) = G(\lambda) + O(\lVert d\lambda \rVert)$. The same idea, with the divergence between two policies, gives the trust-region update of [TRPO](/notes/agents/policy-gradients#trust-regions-how-far-one-step-may-go). In the worked example the Euclidean step treats both natural parameters on one scale, and it overshoots the precision, $19.02$ against $10.01$.

## Mini-batches: a noisy natural gradient

The natural gradient still needs $\bar\eta = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb E_{\phi_i}[s(x_i, z_i)]$, a sum over all points. Draw one index $I$ uniformly from $\{1, \dots, N\}$ and replace the sum by $N$ copies of its $I$-th term. Since

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_I\Big[N\, \mathbb E_{\phi_I}[s(x_I, z_I)]\Big]
&= \sum_{i=1}^N \frac1N \cdot N\, \mathbb E_{\phi_i}[s(x_i, z_i)] \\
&= \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb E_{\phi_i}[s(x_i, z_i)],
\end{aligned}
$$

the result is an unbiased estimate of $\bar\eta$. A batch drawn without replacement is unbiased too: each point enters it with probability $B/N$, which the factor $N/B$ below cancels. It is exactly the coordinate-ascent update for a data set made of $N$ copies of $x_I$ (Hoffman et al., Section 2.4). Averaging $B$ such draws gives the **intermediate global parameter** of a mini-batch $S$, and the SVI update blends it into the current value:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\hat\lambda_S &= \alpha + \frac{N}{B} \sum_{i \in S} \mathbb E_{\phi_i(\lambda)}\big[s(x_i, z_i)\big], \\
\lambda^{(t)} &= \big(1 - \rho_t\big)\,\lambda^{(t-1)} + \rho_t\, \hat\lambda_S
\end{aligned}
}
$$

Here $\phi_i(\lambda)$ is the local optimum for the current $\lambda$, computed only for the points of the batch, and $\hat\lambda_S - \lambda^{(t-1)}$ is an unbiased estimate of the natural gradient. One step costs $B$ local updates instead of $N$. [Murphy (2023](#references), Section 10.1.4) writes the same idea as stochastic gradient ascent on the ELBO of a mini-batch scaled by $N/B$.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Treat the mini-batch as if it were the whole data set, scaled by $N/B$, compute the coordinate-ascent update from it, and move only a fraction $\rho_t$ of the way there.</aside>

<SviLoop />

**Worked example.** One component of the mixture, first coordinate of its mean, with $N = 200{,}000$, $B = 100$ and prior precision $0.01$. The current $q(\mu_k)$ has precision $P = 24{,}000$ and mean $m = 3.00$, so $Pm = 72{,}000$. In the batch, the responsibilities of component $k$ sum to $12.5$ and $\sum_{i \in S} \phi_{ik} x_i = 40$. The step is $t = 10$, with $\rho_{10} = 11^{-0.7} = 0.187$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat P &= 0.01 + 2000 \cdot 12.5 = 25{,}000.01, \\
\widehat{Pm} &= 2000 \cdot 40 = 80{,}000, \\
P' &= 0.813 \cdot 24{,}000 + 0.187 \cdot 25{,}000 = 24{,}187, \\
(Pm)' &= 73{,}493, \\
m' &= 73{,}493 / 24{,}187 = 3.039 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The intermediate mean is $80{,}000 / 25{,}000 = 3.20$. The blend acts on natural parameters, so the mean does not move exactly a fraction $\rho$ of the way from $3.00$ to $3.20$, which would give $3.037$. The Code section also checks that $\hat\lambda_S$ is unbiased: averaged over 2000 batches at a fixed $q$, its precisions agree with the full-data ones to within $0.6\%$.

With $\rho_1 = 1$ and $\rho_t = 1/t$, multiplying the update $\lambda^{(t)} = (1 - 1/t)\lambda^{(t-1)} + \hat\lambda_t/t$ by $t$ gives $t\lambda^{(t)} = (t - 1)\lambda^{(t-1)} + \hat\lambda_t$. So $t\lambda^{(t)}$ is the running sum of the $\hat\lambda_s$, the start dropping out at $t = 1$, and

$$
\lambda^{(t)} = \frac1t \sum_{s=1}^t \hat\lambda_s ,
$$

the plain average of all intermediate estimates. Other schedules weight recent estimates more. That helps, because the early $\hat\lambda_s$ were computed from local steps under a poor $\lambda$.

## Step sizes: the Robbins–Monro conditions

With a constant step, SVI ends in a cloud around the optimum whose size is proportional to the step, as any stochastic gradient method does ([a constant step stalls in a noise ball](/notes/ml/stochastic-and-adaptive-optimizers#a-constant-step-stalls-in-a-noise-ball)). The classical conditions of [Robbins and Monro (1951)](#references) are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{t=1}^\infty \rho_t &= \infty, \\
\sum_{t=1}^\infty \rho_t^2 &< \infty .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first lets the steps travel any distance; the second keeps their accumulated noise finite. The convergence theorem needs more than the step sizes. Suppose $\mathcal L(\lambda)$ is bounded above with a Lipschitz-continuous gradient, the noisy step is unbiased for $G^{-1} \nabla \mathcal L$ with the eigenvalues of $G$ bounded above and away from zero, and the variance of the noise grows at most like $M + M_V \lVert \nabla \mathcal L \rVert^2$. Then steps meeting both conditions give $\liminf_t \mathbb E \lVert \nabla \mathcal L(\lambda^{(t)}) \rVert^2 = 0$ ([Bottou, Curtis and Nocedal, 2018](#references), Theorem 4.10): the iterates approach stationary points, in practice local maxima (Hoffman et al., Section 2.4). The schedule used in practice is their equation 26:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho_t &= (t + \tau)^{-\kappa}, \\
\kappa &\in (0.5, 1], \\
\tau &\ge 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first sum diverges because $\kappa \le 1$; the second converges because $2\kappa > 1$. Both are the $p$-series test: $\sum_t t^{-p}$ converges exactly when $p > 1$, and the shift $\tau$ does not change that. Hoffman et al. call $\kappa$ the **forgetting rate** and $\tau$ the **delay**, which damps the first steps.

With $\tau = 1$ and $\kappa = 0.7$, the schedule of the experiment below:

| Step $t$ | $\rho_t$ | about $1/\rho_t$ batches remembered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | $0.187$ | 5 |
| 1000 | $0.0079$ | 126 |
| 4000 | $0.0030$ | 330 |

Over a stretch where $\rho_t \approx \rho$ barely changes, unrolling the update, and ignoring the start, gives

$$
\lambda^{(t)} \approx \sum_{k \ge 0} \rho\, (1 - \rho)^k\, \hat\lambda_{t - k} .
$$

The newest $1/\rho$ batches carry the total weight $1 - (1 - \rho)^{1/\rho} \approx 1 - e^{-1} = 0.63$, since $(1 - \rho)^{1/\rho} \to e^{-1}$ as $\rho \to 0$. In this sense $\lambda$ remembers roughly the last $1/\rho_t$ batches. After two passes over 200,000 points that is 330 batches, or 33,000 points, and this memory sets the noise floor of the experiment. The batch size trades in the same terms: the variance of $\hat\lambda_S$, an average of $B$ independent draws, falls like $1/B$ while the cost of a step grows like $B$.

On their topic models, Hoffman et al. fixed $\tau = 1$, after finding the results insensitive to it, and tried $\kappa$ from $0.5$ to $1$ and batches of 10 to 1000 documents. Forgetting rates near 1 and larger batches did best; their large-scale comparisons use $\kappa = 0.9$ and 500 documents per batch.

## An experiment: SVI against batch coordinate ascent

Both methods fit the mixture above with $K = 8$ components in $D = 2$ dimensions, true means drawn from $\mathcal N(0, 5^2 I)$, $N = 200{,}000$ points and prior $\sigma_0 = 10$. Both start from the same $K$ randomly chosen data points as means, with precision 1. Batch coordinate ascent runs 30 passes. SVI uses $B = 100$, $\tau = 1$ and $\kappa = 0.7$ for two passes, 4000 steps.

The **gap** is the ELBO per point of the batch method after 30 passes, $-4.7142$, minus the current ELBO per point, computed on all the data with every $\phi_i$ at its optimum. A pass means $N$ points processed by the local step, the dominant cost of both methods.

| Gap below | Batch, passes | SVI, passes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0.1$ | 4 | 0.01 |
| $0.01$ | 5 | 0.02 |
| $0.001$ | 6 | 0.08 |
| smallest gap | $2.1 \cdot 10^{-6}$ after 12 passes | $5.4 \cdot 10^{-5}$ in 2 passes |

<ElboGap />

SVI needs 250 times fewer points than the batch method to get within $0.01$, and 75 times fewer to get within $0.001$. Both end at the same optimum. The last row is the other side of the trade: below about $10^{-4}$ SVI is held up by the noise of its steps, while the batch method is exact at every pass and passes below SVI's best value at pass 9. When a very precise optimum is needed, a common recipe is SVI first and a few batch passes at the end. These numbers come from one data set, one start and one seed; with more local optima the two methods can also end in different ones.

## SVI for LDA

For [LDA](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation) with $D$ documents, the global variables are the topics, with Dirichlet variational parameters $\lambda_{kw}$ and prior $\eta$; the local ones are each document's topic proportions and word assignments. The local step on a document is the inner loop of coordinate ascent over its $\gamma_d$ and $\phi_{dn}$, run until it converges. The intermediate topics of a batch are

$$
\hat\lambda_{kw} = \eta + \frac{D}{B} \sum_{d \in S} \sum_{n=1}^{N_d} \phi_{dnk}\, [w_{dn} = w],
$$

the expected number of times word $w$ is assigned to topic $k$ in the batch, scaled up to the corpus. This is the **online LDA** of [Hoffman, Blei and Bach (2010)](#references), the first special case of SVI. With it, Hoffman et al. (2013) fitted topic models to about 350,000 articles of Nature, 1.8 million articles of The New York Times and 3.8 million Wikipedia articles. On all three of their corpora, SVI on the full data reached a better held-out predictive likelihood, and sooner, than batch inference on a subset small enough for it (their Figure 7).

scikit-learn's `LatentDirichletAllocation(learning_method='online')` implements this update (checked in version 1.9.1): `learning_offset` is $\tau$ (default 10), `learning_decay` is $\kappa$ (default 0.7) and `batch_size` is $B$ (default 128). `fit` scales the batch by the true number of documents. `partial_fit` scales it by the parameter `total_samples`, whose default is $10^6$; when streaming a corpus of another size, set it to that size, or the intermediate topics are scaled wrongly.

## Where it breaks

The delay $\tau$ looks like a detail, and with one point per step it decides whether the fit survives its first step. Run the mixture with $B = 1$, the basic algorithm of Hoffman et al., for 0.2 passes, 40,000 steps, at $\kappa = 0.7$ and three delays, from two random seeds:

| $\tau$ | $\rho_1$ | Gap after 0.2 passes | Components left empty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | $1.00$ | $4.7$ and $2.2$ | 5 and 4 of 8 |
| 1 | $0.62$ | $0.19$ and $0.36$ | 1 and 0 |
| 10 | $0.19$ | $0.0015$ and $0.0034$ | none |

With $\tau = 0$ the first step has $\rho_1 = 1$ and replaces $\lambda$ entirely by the intermediate parameter of one point, counted $N$ times. In the first step of the run with seed 2, three components move onto that point and the other five, which receive almost no weight, are reset to the prior: mean near 0 and precision near $0.01$. For such a component $\mathbb E_q \lVert \mu_k \rVert^2 \approx D/P_k = 200$, so its responsibility for every point carries a factor of about $e^{-100}$, and it never receives data again. A larger delay keeps most of the starting means through the first, noisiest steps. Hoffman et al. found their topic models, fitted with batches of 10 to 1000 documents, insensitive to $\tau$; the scikit-learn default, $\tau = 10$, is on the safe side.

SVI still needs conditional conjugacy: the natural gradient has the closed form $\bar\eta - \lambda$ only when the complete conditional of $\beta$ is in the family of $q(\beta)$, and a logistic likelihood or a neural network breaks this. The local step must be cheap: in LDA it is an inner loop per document, and in models with richer local structure it can dominate the cost. And SVI optimizes the same mean-field ELBO, so it inherits [what mean field gets wrong](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference#what-mean-field-gets-wrong): posterior variances that are too small and local optima. It reaches them faster.

Monitoring needs care as well. The full ELBO costs a pass over the data, which defeats the purpose; Hoffman et al. instead tracked the predictive likelihood of 10,000 held-out documents.

## What you should remember

- With global and local variables, the ELBO is a global term plus one local term per point; each local optimum depends only on its point and $\lambda$.
- With conditional conjugacy, the natural gradient in $\lambda$ is $\mathbb E_\phi[\eta_g] - \lambda$; coordinate ascent is the natural-gradient step of length 1.
- SVI: $\hat\lambda_S = \alpha + (N/B) \sum_{i \in S} \mathbb E_{\phi_i}[s(x_i, z_i)]$ is unbiased, and $\lambda \leftarrow (1 - \rho_t)\lambda + \rho_t \hat\lambda_S$.
- $\rho_t = (t + \tau)^{-\kappa}$ with $\kappa \in (0.5, 1]$ meets Robbins–Monro; $\lambda$ remembers about $1/\rho_t$ batches.
- On 200,000 points, $10^{-3}$ nats per point in 0.08 passes against 6; then a noise floor near $10^{-4}$.
- A first step with $\rho_1 = 1$ and a tiny batch can empty components for good.

## Code

The natural against the ordinary gradient on one Gaussian mean, one SVI step with the numbers of the worked example, the unbiasedness of the intermediate parameter, the experiment, and the failure with $B = 1$. Seeds are fixed; exact numbers can vary across platforms and library versions. The script runs in about 11 seconds on a laptop CPU, most of it in the 240,000 single-point steps at the end.

```python
import numpy as np

# 1. natural against Euclidean gradient: q(mu) = N(m, s^2) with natural parameters (m / s^2, -1 / (2 s^2)),
#    statistics u(mu) = (mu, mu^2); ten points with mean 2, noise variance 1, prior N(0, 100); start at m = 0, s = 1
lam, eta_bar = np.array([0.0, -0.5]), np.array([10 * 2.0, -(0.01 + 10) / 2])   # eta_bar: the exact posterior
m, s2 = 0.0, 1.0
fisher = np.array([[s2, 2 * m * s2], [2 * m * s2, 2 * s2 ** 2 + 4 * m ** 2 * s2]])   # covariance of (mu, mu^2) under q
natural, euclid = eta_bar - lam, fisher @ (eta_bar - lam)
mean_prec = lambda l: (round(float(l[0] / (-2 * l[1])), 4), round(float(-2 * l[1]), 2))
print(natural, euclid, mean_prec(lam + natural), mean_prec(lam + euclid))
# [20.    -4.505] [20.   -9.01] (1.998, 10.01) (1.0515, 19.02)

# 2. one SVI step for one component, the worked example: N = 200,000, B = 100, prior precision 0.01
N, B, prior_prec = 200_000, 100, 0.01
P, h = 24_000.0, 72_000.0                       # current q(mu_k): precision P and h = P m, so m = 3.0
P_hat, h_hat = prior_prec + N / B * 12.5, N / B * 40.0   # batch: sum of phi_ik = 12.5, sum of phi_ik x_i = 40
rho = (10 + 1) ** -0.7                           # step t = 10, tau = 1, kappa = 0.7
P, h = (1 - rho) * P + rho * P_hat, (1 - rho) * h + rho * h_hat
print(round(rho, 4), round(h_hat / P_hat, 4), round(P, 1), round(h / P, 4))
# 0.1866 3.2 24186.7 3.0386

# 3. data from the Bayesian mixture: K = 8 means in 2-D drawn from N(0, 5^2 I), unit-variance clusters, equal weights
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
K, D, s0 = 8, 2, 10.0                            # s0: prior standard deviation of each mean, 1 / s0^2 = 0.01
X = rng.normal(0, 5, (K, D))[rng.integers(K, size=N)] + rng.normal(size=(N, D))

# q(mu_k) = N(m_k, I / P_k), stored by h_k = P_k m_k and P_k
def local_step(Xb, h, P):
    """phi_ik proportional to exp(E_q[log p(x_i | mu_k)]): the coordinate update of the local variables."""
    m = h / P[:, None]
    logit = Xb @ m.T - 0.5 * ((m ** 2).sum(1) + D / P)
    phi = np.exp(logit - logit.max(1, keepdims=True))
    return phi / phi.sum(1, keepdims=True)

def elbo(h, P):
    """ELBO per data point, with every phi_i at its optimum for the current global parameters."""
    m, s2 = h / P[:, None], 1 / P
    logit = X @ m.T - 0.5 * ((m ** 2).sum(1) + D * s2)
    mx = logit.max(1)
    data = mx + np.log(np.exp(logit - mx[:, None]).sum(1)) - np.log(K) - 0.5 * (X ** 2).sum(1) - np.log(2 * np.pi)
    glob = -D * np.log(s0) - ((m ** 2).sum(1) + D * s2) / (2 * s0 ** 2) + 0.5 * D * np.log(s2) + 0.5 * D
    return (data.sum() + glob.sum()) / N

def start():                                     # the same start for both methods: K data points as means, precision 1
    return X[np.random.default_rng(1).choice(N, K, replace=False)].copy(), np.ones(K)

# 4. the intermediate global parameter of a mini-batch is unbiased: average it over 2000 batches at a fixed q
h0, P0 = start()
full = 1 / s0 ** 2 + local_step(X, h0, P0).sum(0)
r = np.random.default_rng(5)
est = np.mean([1 / s0 ** 2 + N / B * local_step(X[r.integers(N, size=B)], h0, P0).sum(0) for _ in range(2000)], 0)
print(np.round(full[:4]), np.round(est[:4]))
# [39145. 25268. 51657. 11502.] [39145. 25180. 51951. 11468.]

# 5. batch coordinate ascent: each global update waits for a pass over all N points
h, P = start(); batch = [elbo(h, P)]
for it in range(30):
    phi = local_step(X, h, P)
    P, h = 1 / s0 ** 2 + phi.sum(0), phi.T @ X   # lambda = E_phi[eta_g(x, z)]: a natural-gradient step of length 1
    batch.append(elbo(h, P))
best = batch[-1]

# 6. SVI: mini-batches of B points, rho_t = (t + tau)^(-kappa); the ELBO is recorded every 0.01 pass
def svi(B=100, tau=1.0, kappa=0.7, passes=2.0, seed=2):
    h, P = start(); r = np.random.default_rng(seed); trace = []
    for t in range(1, int(passes * N / B) + 1):
        idx = r.integers(N, size=B)
        phi = local_step(X[idx], h, P)
        P_hat, h_hat = 1 / s0 ** 2 + N / B * phi.sum(0), N / B * phi.T @ X[idx]   # as if the batch were seen N / B times
        rho = (t + tau) ** -kappa
        P, h = (1 - rho) * P + rho * P_hat, (1 - rho) * h + rho * h_hat
        if t % (N // B // 100) == 0:
            trace.append((t * B / N, elbo(h, P)))
    return trace, P

trace, _ = svi()
print('optimum per point %.4f, batch after one pass %.3f, SVI after one pass %.4f, after two %.4f'
      % (best, batch[1], trace[99][1], trace[-1][1]))
for tol in (0.1, 0.01, 0.001):
    b = next(i for i, v in enumerate(batch) if v >= best - tol)
    s = next(p for p, v in trace if v >= best - tol)
    print('gap below %g: batch after %d passes, SVI after %.2f passes' % (tol, b, s))
floor = min(best - v for p, v in trace)
print('smallest SVI gap %.1e; batch goes below it at pass %d' % (floor, next(i for i, v in enumerate(batch) if best - v < floor)))
# optimum per point -4.7142, batch after one pass -5.661, SVI after one pass -4.7145, after two -4.7143
# gap below 0.1: batch after 4 passes, SVI after 0.01 passes
# gap below 0.01: batch after 5 passes, SVI after 0.02 passes
# gap below 0.001: batch after 6 passes, SVI after 0.08 passes
# smallest SVI gap 5.4e-05; batch goes below it at pass 9

# 7. where it breaks: one point per step (B = 1) for 0.2 passes; the delay tau sets the first step rho_1 = (1 + tau)^(-0.7)
for tau in (0, 1, 10):
    gaps, empty = [], []
    for seed in (2, 3):
        trace, P = svi(B=1, tau=tau, passes=0.2, seed=seed)
        gaps.append(best - trace[-1][1]); empty.append(int((P < 1).sum()))   # P_k < 1: almost no data left in component k
    print('tau %2d, rho_1 %.2f: gaps %.2g %.2g, empty components %d %d' % (tau, (1 + tau) ** -0.7, *gaps, *empty))
# tau  0, rho_1 1.00: gaps 4.7 2.2, empty components 5 4
# tau  1, rho_1 0.62: gaps 0.19 0.36, empty components 1 0
# tau 10, rho_1 0.19: gaps 0.0015 0.0034, empty components 0 0
```

## Next

SVI made each step of variational inference cheap, but it still needs a model in which the complete conditional of every variable is in a known exponential family: only then is the natural gradient $\bar\eta - \lambda$ available in closed form. Bayesian logistic regression already breaks this, and a model with a neural network inside breaks it everywhere. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference) estimates the gradient of the ELBO by Monte Carlo for any model whose log joint density can be evaluated, and arrives at the variational autoencoder. Vetrov and Kropotov's course teaches the two in one lecture, number 12 ([references](#references)).
