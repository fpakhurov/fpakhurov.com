Take the simplest model with two unknowns. Observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ come from $\mathcal N(\mu, \lambda^{-1})$, with an unknown mean $\mu$ and an unknown **precision** $\lambda$, the inverse variance. Give them the priors most people would write first, independent of each other: a normal prior on $\mu$ and a gamma prior on $\lambda$.

Each unknown is easy given the other. With $\lambda$ fixed, the posterior of $\mu$ is normal; with $\mu$ fixed, the posterior of $\lambda$ is gamma. Yet the joint posterior $p(\mu, \lambda \mid X)$ belongs to no standard family, because the evidence $p(X) = \iint p(X, \mu, \lambda)\, d\mu\, d\lambda$ has no closed form. The models used in practice are harder. The posterior of a Bayesian mixture of $K$ Gaussians has one term for each of the $K^n$ ways of assigning points to components: $3^{100} \approx 5 \cdot 10^{47}$ terms for three components and a hundred points.

The [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) fits a Gaussian at one point, the mode. The [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca) replaced the posterior of the latent variables by the best member of a tractable family. Can we do that for every unknown, and find the best distribution of a simple form without ever computing $p(X)$? That is **variational inference**: an integral we cannot compute is replaced by an optimization we can ([Jordan et al., 1999](#references)).

## The evidence lower bound and the reverse KL

Let $Z$ collect all unknowns and $q(Z)$ be any distribution over them. Start from the KL divergence from $q$ to the posterior and use $p(Z \mid X) = p(X, Z) / p(X)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X)\big)
&= \mathbb E_q\big[\log q(Z) - \log p(Z \mid X)\big] \\
&= \mathbb E_q\big[\log q(Z) - \log p(X, Z)\big] + \log p(X) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Rearranging gives the decomposition that the whole note rests on:

$$
\log p(X) = \underbrace{\mathbb E_q\big[\log p(X, Z)\big] - \mathbb E_q\big[\log q(Z)\big]}_{\mathcal L(q)} + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X)\big) .
$$

The first term is the **evidence lower bound** (ELBO), $\mathcal L(q)$. The same bound follows in one line from Jensen's inequality applied to $\log \mathbb E_q[p(X, Z)/q(Z)]$; the decomposition says more, because it names the gap.

Read it two ways. The left side does not depend on $q$, so making $\mathcal L(q)$ larger makes $q$ closer to the posterior: $\mathcal L$ is an objective for approximating the posterior. And since a KL divergence is never negative, $\mathcal L(q) \le \log p(X)$ for every $q$: $\mathcal L$ is also a lower bound on the evidence, with equality only at the exact posterior. The gap is finite only if $q$ puts no mass where the posterior has none.

Splitting the joint as $p(X \mid Z)\, p(Z)$ gives a third reading:

$$
\mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q\big[\log p(X \mid Z)\big] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(Z) \,\|\, p(Z)\big) .
$$

The first term rewards $q$ for putting mass on values that explain the data; the second keeps $q$ close to the prior. The [variational autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/vae#variational-approximation-and-the-elbo) trains with exactly this form.

Why is $\mathcal L$ computable when $p(X)$ is not? The evidence integrates a *product* of factors over all unknowns at once. The bound averages the *logarithm* of that product, which is a *sum* of log-factors, and under a $q$ that factorizes, each term of the sum is an expectation over one or two variables, often a known moment.

The direction of the divergence matters. Only the reverse direction can be minimized with the joint alone: the bound averages over $q$, and raising it lowers $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p)$ without that divergence ever being computed. The reverse divergence grows without limit where $q$ puts mass and the posterior has almost none, and it costs nothing where the posterior has mass that $q$ misses. So the optimal $q$ stays inside the region where the posterior is large: it is **zero-forcing**, and with several separated modes it typically settles on one, **mode-seeking**. The forward direction $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q)$ covers all the mass instead; expectation propagation works with it, through local moment matching, and the [Generative Models course](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#forward-kl-covers-both-modes-reverse-kl-picks-one) compares the two on a mixture of two Gaussians.

## Which family?

Variational inference makes one approximation: $q$ is restricted to a family $\mathcal Q$. Everything else is exact. Enlarging the family can only bring the bound closer to $\log p(X)$, so a richer $q$ cannot over-fit; the family is limited only to keep the expectations computable ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 10.1).

There are two ways to choose it. A **fixed-form** family fixes the shape, for example a Gaussian with a mean and a covariance to optimize; the [black-box methods](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference) at the end of the course optimize such families with stochastic gradients. A **free-form** family fixes only which variables are independent and lets the bound decide the shape of each factor. The rest of this note uses the free-form mean-field family.

## Mean field and the general update

The **mean-field** family splits the unknowns into blocks $Z_1, \dots, Z_m$ and makes them independent:

$$
q(Z) = \prod_{j=1}^m q_j(Z_j) .
$$

Fix every factor except $q_j$ and look at $\mathcal L$ as a function of $q_j$ alone. Write $\mathbb E_{-j}$ for the expectation over all factors except $q_j$. The expectation of the log joint over the product splits into an expectation over $q_j$ of $\mathbb E_{-j}$, and the entropy of a product is the sum of the factors' entropies:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(q)
&= \int q_j \, \mathbb E_{-j}\big[\log p(X, Z)\big]\, dZ_j - \int q_j \log q_j \, dZ_j + \text{const} \\
&= \int q_j \log \frac{\tilde p_j(Z_j)}{q_j(Z_j)} \, dZ_j + \log C_j + \text{const} \\
&= -D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_j \,\|\, \tilde p_j\big) + \log C_j + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\tilde p_j(Z_j) = \exp\big(\mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(X, Z)]\big) / C_j$ is a normalized density, $C_j$ its normalizing constant, and "const" collects the terms without $q_j$. The only term that depends on $q_j$ is a negative KL divergence, which is largest, zero, when $q_j = \tilde p_j$. So the best factor given the others is

$$
\boxed{\;\log q_j^*(Z_j) = \mathbb E_{-j}\big[\log p(X, Z)\big] + \text{const}\;}
$$

with the constant fixed by normalization.

Three facts make the formula usable.

**Only the neighbourhood of $Z_j$ matters.** Terms of $\log p(X, Z)$ without $Z_j$ fall into the constant, so the update can be written with the **complete conditional**, $\log q_j^* = \mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(Z_j \mid Z_{-j}, X)] + \text{const}$. In a graphical model this conditional involves only the Markov blanket of $Z_j$: its parents, children and the children's other parents. The [Gibbs sampler](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling) of the next note draws from the same conditional, at the current values of the neighbours instead of averaging over them.

**Conditional conjugacy gives closed forms.** Suppose the complete conditional is in an [exponential family](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#natural-parameters-and-sufficient-statistics),

$$
p(Z_j \mid Z_{-j}, X) = h(Z_j) \exp\big(\eta_j^\top u(Z_j) - A(\eta_j)\big),
$$

with natural parameter $\eta_j$ depending on $Z_{-j}$ and $X$. Its logarithm is linear in the sufficient statistics $u(Z_j)$, so the update is a member of the same family, with natural parameter $\mathbb E_{-j}[\eta_j]$ ([Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe, 2017](#references), Section 4). The model need not be conjugate as a whole; each block must be conjugate given the others. That is the case of the opening example.

**Coordinate ascent climbs the bound.** The updates depend on each other through the expectations, so we cycle through them: **coordinate ascent variational inference** (CAVI). $\mathcal L$ is concave in each factor separately, so each update finds the global maximum along its own coordinate, and $\mathcal L$ never decreases. Being bounded by $\log p(X)$, it converges, to a stationary point that is usually a local maximum and can depend on the start. This gives a free test: compute $\mathcal L$ after every update. A decrease means a bug in the derivation or the code.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Under mean field, the best factor given the others is the exponentiated average of the log joint over the others, $\log q_j^* = \mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(X, Z)] + \text{const}$; cycling these updates climbs the bound.</aside>

## Worked example: a Gaussian with unknown mean and precision

To check the approximation we need a model whose exact posterior we know. Take the normal model with the **normal–gamma prior** of the [conjugate priors note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#normal-gamma-unknown-mean-and-precision), in which the prior variance of $\mu$ is tied to $\lambda$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu \mid \lambda &\sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_0, (\kappa_0 \lambda)^{-1}\big), \\
\lambda &\sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_0, b_0) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The gamma density with shape $a$ and rate $b$ is $\mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda \mid a, b) = b^a \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda} / \Gamma(a)$, with mean $a/b$. The exact posterior is normal–gamma, $\mu \mid \lambda, X \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_n, (\kappa_n \lambda)^{-1}\big)$ and $\lambda \mid X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_n, b_n)$, with

$$
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_n &= \kappa_0 + n, \\
\mu_n &= \frac{\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n \bar x}{\kappa_0 + n}, \\
a_n &= a_0 + \frac n2, \\
b_n &= b_0 + \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n (\bar x - \mu_0)^2}{2(\kappa_0 + n)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

This posterior does not factorize: the conditional variance of $\mu$ is $1/(\kappa_n \lambda)$, so $\mu$ and $\lambda$ are dependent, and a factorized $q(\mu)\, q(\lambda)$ has something to lose ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 10.1.3).

We use eight observations, $3.0, -1.6, 1.4, 0.4, 0.5, 0.8, -1.0, 0.8$, and the prior $\mu_0 = 0$, $\kappa_0 = 1$, $a_0 = b_0 = 2$:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| $\bar x$ and $\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2$ | $0.5375$ and $13.899$ |
| $\kappa_n$, $\mu_n$, $a_n$, $b_n$ | $9$, $0.478$, $6$, $9.078$ |
| posterior mean of $\lambda$, $a_n/b_n$ | $0.661$ |
| posterior standard deviation of $\mu$ (a Student $t$) | $0.449$ |
| log evidence $\log p(X)$ | $-15.5113$ |

The log joint, up to a constant, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(X, \mu, \lambda) ={}& \frac n2 \log \lambda - \frac\lambda2 \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 \\
&+ \frac12 \log(\kappa_0 \lambda) - \frac{\kappa_0 \lambda}{2} (\mu - \mu_0)^2 \\
&+ (a_0 - 1) \log \lambda - b_0 \lambda + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

**The factor $q(\mu)$.** We keep the terms with $\mu$ and average over $q(\lambda)$, which only replaces $\lambda$ by $\mathbb E[\lambda]$. Completing the square, the bracket is $(\kappa_0 + n)\mu^2 - 2\mu(\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n\bar x) + \text{const}$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q^*(\mu)
&= -\frac{\mathbb E[\lambda]}{2} \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 + \kappa_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big) + \text{const} \\
&= -\frac{(\kappa_0 + n)\, \mathbb E[\lambda]}{2}\, (\mu - \mu_n)^2 + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The factor is $q^*(\mu) = \mathcal N(\mu \mid \mu_n, \omega^{-1})$, centred on the exact posterior mean $\mu_n$, with precision $\omega = (\kappa_0 + n)\, \mathbb E[\lambda]$.

**The factor $q(\lambda)$.** We keep the terms with $\lambda$ and average over $q(\mu)$:

$$
\log q^*(\lambda) = \Big(a_0 - 1 + \frac{n + 1}{2}\Big) \log \lambda - \lambda \Big(b_0 + \frac12\, \mathbb E_\mu\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 + \kappa_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big]\Big) + \text{const} .
$$

This is the logarithm of a gamma density, $q^*(\lambda) = \mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda \mid \tilde a, \tilde b)$, with

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde a &= a_0 + \frac{n + 1}{2}, \\
\tilde b &= b_0 + \frac12 \Big(\sum_i (x_i - \mu_n)^2 + \frac n\omega + \kappa_0 (\mu_n - \mu_0)^2 + \frac{\kappa_0}\omega\Big),
\end{aligned}
$$

where we used $\mathbb E_\mu[(x_i - \mu)^2] = (x_i - \mu_n)^2 + 1/\omega$. The shape has $(n + 1)/2$ rather than the exact posterior's $n/2$ because the prior of $\mu$ depends on $\lambda$ and contributes its half. Bishop's printed equation (10.29) has $N/2$ here; the derivation above agrees with [Murphy (2023](#references), chapter 10).

**Iterating.** Start from $q(\mu) = \mathcal N(1.4, 0.2^2)$ and a $q(\lambda)$ with mean $1.2$. The first update of $q(\mu)$ moves its mean to $0.478$ and sets its precision to $9 \cdot 1.2 = 10.8$. The first update of $q(\lambda)$ then gives $\tilde a = 6.5$ and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde b &= 2 + \tfrac12 \big(13.927 + 8/10.8 + 0.478^2 + 1/10.8\big) = 9.494, \\
\mathbb E[\lambda] &= 6.5 / 9.494 = 0.685 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The bound climbs with every update, as CAVI promises:

| After | $\mathcal L(q)$ |
| --- | --- |
| the start | $-21.596$ |
| update 1, $q(\mu)$ | $-16.867$ |
| update 2, $q(\lambda)$ | $-15.622$ |
| update 3, $q(\mu)$ | $-15.556$ |
| update 4, $q(\lambda)$ | $-15.553$ |
| convergence | $-15.5524$ |

The log evidence is $-15.5113$, so the remaining gap, $0.0411$, is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p)$; the Code section obtains the same number by integrating the divergence directly.

**The fixed point in closed form.** At convergence the mean of $\lambda$ is exact and the variance of $\mu$ is not. Two identities, $\bar x - \mu_n = \kappa_0(\bar x - \mu_0)/(\kappa_0 + n)$ and $\mu_n - \mu_0 = n(\bar x - \mu_0)/(\kappa_0 + n)$, give $\sum_i (x_i - \mu_n)^2 + \kappa_0 (\mu_n - \mu_0)^2 = 2(b_n - b_0)$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde b &= b_n + \frac{n + \kappa_0}{2\omega} = b_n + \frac{1}{2\, \mathbb E[\lambda]} && \text{since } \omega = (\kappa_0 + n)\,\mathbb E[\lambda], \\
\tilde a &= a_n + \frac12, \\
\mathbb E[\lambda]\, \tilde b &= \tilde a && \text{the mean of } q(\lambda), \\
\mathbb E[\lambda]\, b_n + \frac12 &= a_n + \frac12 , \\
\mathbb E[\lambda] &= \frac{a_n}{b_n} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The mean of the precision is exactly the posterior mean, $0.661$. The variance of $\mu$ under $q$ is $1/\omega = b_n/(a_n\kappa_n)$, against the exact $b_n/((a_n - 1)\kappa_n)$:

$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}_q(\mu)}{\operatorname{Var}_p(\mu)} = \frac{a_n - 1}{a_n} = \frac56 .
$$

So $q$ reports a standard deviation of $0.410$ for $\mu$ where the truth is $0.449$. The exact marginal of $\mu$ is a Student $t$: a mixture of Gaussians with variances $1/(\kappa_n \lambda)$ over the uncertain $\lambda$, in which small values of $\lambda$ widen the tails. The factorized $q$ uses one Gaussian with the average precision and loses that spread. As $n$ grows, $a_n = a_0 + n/2$ grows, the ratio tends to 1, and both $q(\mu)$ and the exact posterior collapse onto $\bar x$. The standard deviation of $\lambda$ is also slightly too small, $0.259$ against $0.270$.

## What mean field gets wrong

### Correlated unknowns

Let the posterior be a Gaussian $\mathcal N(m, \Lambda^{-1})$ in two blocks, with precision matrix $\Lambda$. The general update averages the quadratic form over $z_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q_1^*(z_1)
&= \mathbb E_{z_2}\Big[-\tfrac12 (z - m)^\top \Lambda (z - m)\Big] + \text{const} \\
&= -\tfrac12 \Lambda_{11} z_1^2 + z_1 \big(\Lambda_{11} m_1 - \Lambda_{12} (\mathbb E[z_2] - m_2)\big) + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

So $q_1^*$ is Gaussian with mean $m_1 - \Lambda_{11}^{-1} \Lambda_{12} (\mathbb E[z_2] - m_2)$ and variance $\Lambda_{11}^{-1}$, and symmetrically for $q_2$. The means converge to the exact means $m$. The variances do not: $1/\Lambda_{11}$ is the variance of $z_1$ *given* $z_2$, not its marginal variance $\Sigma_{11}$, and it is never larger. The width of $q$ is set by the narrowest direction of the posterior ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 10.1.2).

For unit variances and correlation $\rho = 0.9$, $\Lambda_{11} = 1/(1 - \rho^2)$, so each factor has variance $0.19$ instead of 1: standard deviation $0.436$ instead of 1. This is zero-forcing at work. The reverse KL forbids $q$ to put mass outside the narrow ridge of the posterior, and a product of independent factors can only stay inside the ridge by being narrow in every direction.

The divergence at the optimum is $-\frac12 \log(1 - \rho^2) = 0.830$. The best factorized $q$ in the forward direction is the product of the exact marginals, variance 1, with the same divergence. The two directions give the same value for every two-dimensional Gaussian; from three dimensions on they generally differ.

The coordinate updates of the means are $m_1 \leftarrow \rho\, m_2$ and $m_2 \leftarrow \rho\, m_1$ (for $m = 0$), the conditional means of the target. Each sweep multiplies the distance to the optimum by $\rho^2 = 0.81$, the same factor that slows the Gibbs sampler on this target in the [MCMC note](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#gibbs-sampling). From $(-2.4, 2.4)$, forty sweeps leave the means at $0.00058$ and $0.00052$.

The remedy is to put strongly coupled variables into one block. A factor over $(z_1, z_2)$ jointly keeps their correlation; this **structured mean field** costs a harder update for that block.

### Separated modes

A posterior with separated modes is approximated by a $q$ on one of them. Mixture models always have this structure: relabelling the $K$ components gives $K!$ equivalent modes. For predictions the choice does not matter. For the bound it does: $\mathcal L$ misses about $\log K!$ of the log evidence, which [Bishop (2006](#references), Section 10.2.4) adds back before comparing mixtures with different $K$.

More generally, comparing two models by their bounds compares $\log p(X)$ minus two different gaps. It is reliable only when the gaps are small next to the difference in evidence, and nothing in the method measures the gaps ([Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe, 2017](#references)).

### Overconfidence

In both examples $q$ is too narrow. This is systematic: the reverse KL penalizes mass where the posterior is small, so the variances of $q$ tend to be too small, and credible intervals from mean field are narrower than the exact ones. More iterations do not fix it. A richer family or more data does, and only a comparison with a more exact method, such as the sampler of the next note, shows how large the error is.

## The variational mixture of Gaussians

The standard showcase of variational inference is the Bayesian mixture of $K$ Gaussians: a Dirichlet prior on the weights $\pi$, Gauss–Wishart priors on the means and precisions $(\mu_k, \Lambda_k)$, and a latent component $z_i$ for each point. The only assumption is that latent assignments and parameters are independent, $q(Z)\, q(\pi, \mu, \Lambda)$. The update formula then factorizes further on its own: $q(Z)$ splits over points, and $q(\pi, \mu, \Lambda)$ into $q(\pi) \prod_k q(\mu_k, \Lambda_k)$. These **induced factorizations** follow from the structure of the model ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 10.2.5).

The updates look like EM. Each point gets a responsibility $r_{ik}$ for each component, proportional to $\tilde r_{ik}$ with

$$
\log \tilde r_{ik} = \mathbb E\big[\log \pi_k\big] + \tfrac12\, \mathbb E\big[\log \det \Lambda_k\big] - \tfrac D2 \log 2\pi - \tfrac12\, \mathbb E\big[(x_i - \mu_k)^\top \Lambda_k (x_i - \mu_k)\big] ,
$$

where $D$ is the dimension of the data. Expectations under $q$ take the place of EM's point estimates. The Dirichlet factor gets the parameters $\alpha_k = \alpha_0 + N_k$, the prior pseudo-count plus $N_k = \sum_i r_{ik}$, and the Gauss–Wishart factors are updated with the responsibility-weighted statistics.

Two things change compared with EM for the same mixture. Maximum likelihood can collapse a component onto a single point, with variance shrinking to zero and the likelihood growing without bound; the prior and the averaging over parameters rule this out. And with a small $\alpha_0$, below 1, components that explain few points see their weights driven to zero, so a mixture started with too many components switches the extra ones off, as Bayesian PCA switched off directions in the previous note. [Latent Dirichlet allocation](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation), later in the course, is fitted the same way.

## Where variational inference sits

The methods of this part of the course differ in which parts of the model are tractable and what they give up; the table follows one in [Vetrov and Kropotov's lecture 7](#references), with the last row added:

| What the model allows | Method | What is approximated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the whole posterior is conjugate | exact Bayesian inference | nothing |
| each block is conjugate given the others | mean-field variational inference | the posterior, by a factorized $q$ |
| the posterior of the latent variables is tractable given the parameters | EM | the parameters, by a point estimate |
| it is not | variational EM | the latent posterior by a factorized $q$, the parameters by a point estimate |
| no conjugate structure at all | sampling, or black-box variational inference | the posterior, by samples or by a parametric $q$ |

## What you should remember

- $\log p(X) = \mathcal L(q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X))$: maximizing the bound minimizes the reverse KL, using only the joint density.
- $\mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q[\log p(X \mid Z)] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p(Z))$: fit the data, stay near the prior.
- Mean field: $\log q_j^* = \mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(X, Z)] + \text{const}$; with conditional conjugacy each factor stays in its family.
- CAVI never decreases $\mathcal L$; a decrease is a bug.
- Mean field gets the means right and the variances too small: $(a_n - 1)/a_n$ for the normal model, $1 - \rho^2$ for a correlated Gaussian.
- The reverse KL is zero-forcing and mode-seeking; the bound misses about $\log K!$ for a mixture.

## Code

The exact posterior and evidence of the normal model, CAVI with the bound after every update, the gap computed as a KL divergence by numerical integration, the closed-form variance ratio, and mean field for a correlated Gaussian. It uses NumPy and SciPy and runs in a few seconds, most of them in the numerical integration; exact numbers can differ slightly across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import gammaln, digamma
from scipy.stats import norm, gamma
from scipy.integrate import dblquad

# the normal model: x_i ~ N(mu, 1/tau), prior mu | tau ~ N(mu0, 1/(l0 tau)), tau ~ Gam(a0, b0) (rate b0)
# tau is the precision lambda of the text, l0 is kappa_0, and lam below is the precision omega of q(mu)
x = np.array([3.0, -1.6, 1.4, 0.4, 0.5, 0.8, -1.0, 0.8])
N, xbar = len(x), x.mean()
mu0, l0, a0, b0 = 0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 2.0

# exact posterior: normal-gamma, and the exact log evidence
lN, mN, aE = l0 + N, (l0 * mu0 + N * xbar) / (l0 + N), a0 + N / 2
bE = b0 + 0.5 * ((x - xbar) ** 2).sum() + l0 * N * (xbar - mu0) ** 2 / (2 * lN)
logZ = gammaln(aE) - gammaln(a0) + a0 * np.log(b0) - aE * np.log(bE) + 0.5 * np.log(l0 / lN) - N / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi)
print(round(mN, 3), round(aE / bE, 3), round(np.sqrt(bE / ((aE - 1) * lN)), 3), round(logZ, 4))   # 0.478 0.661 0.449 -15.5113

def elbo(m, lam, a, b):                  # L(q) for q(mu) = N(m, 1/lam), q(tau) = Gam(a, b)
    Elog, Et = digamma(a) - np.log(b), a / b
    Esq, Ep = ((x - m) ** 2).sum() + N / lam, (m - mu0) ** 2 + 1 / lam
    lik = N / 2 * Elog - N / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - Et * Esq / 2
    pmu = 0.5 * (np.log(l0) + Elog - np.log(2 * np.pi)) - l0 * Et * Ep / 2
    ptau = a0 * np.log(b0) - gammaln(a0) + (a0 - 1) * Elog - b0 * Et
    Hmu, Htau = 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * np.e / lam), a - np.log(b) + gammaln(a) + (1 - a) * digamma(a)
    return lik + pmu + ptau + Hmu + Htau

# coordinate ascent from q(mu) = N(1.4, 0.2^2), q(tau) = Gam(6.5, 6.5 / 1.2)
m, lam, a, b = 1.4, 25.0, 6.5, 6.5 / 1.2
trace = [elbo(m, lam, a, b)]
for sweep in range(30):
    m, lam = mN, lN * a / b                                           # update q(mu)
    trace.append(elbo(m, lam, a, b))
    a = a0 + (N + 1) / 2                                              # update q(tau)
    b = b0 + 0.5 * (((x - m) ** 2).sum() + N / lam + l0 * ((m - mu0) ** 2 + 1 / lam))
    trace.append(elbo(m, lam, a, b))
    if sweep == 0: print(round(lam, 2), round(a / b, 3))              # 10.8 0.685
print(np.round(trace[:5], 4), bool(np.all(np.diff(trace) >= -1e-12)))
# [-21.5957 -16.8674 -15.622  -15.5562 -15.5527] True
print(round(a / b, 3), round(1 / np.sqrt(lam), 3), round(np.sqrt(a) / b, 3), round(np.sqrt(aE) / bE, 3))
# 0.661 0.41 0.259 0.27: E[tau] is exact, the standard deviations are too small
print(round(trace[-1], 4), round(logZ - trace[-1], 4))              # -15.5524 0.0411

# the gap is KL(q || p(mu, tau | x)), here by numerical integration
logq = lambda mu, t: norm.logpdf(mu, m, 1 / np.sqrt(lam)) + gamma.logpdf(t, a, scale=1 / b)
logp = lambda mu, t: norm.logpdf(mu, mN, 1 / np.sqrt(lN * t)) + gamma.logpdf(t, aE, scale=1 / bE)
kl = dblquad(lambda mu, t: np.exp(logq(mu, t)) * (logq(mu, t) - logp(mu, t)), 1e-6, 5, -4, 5)[0]
print(round(kl, 4))                                                  # 0.0411
print(round((aE - 1) / aE, 4), round((1 / lam) / (bE / ((aE - 1) * lN)), 4))   # 0.8333 0.8333: Var_q / Var_p = (a_n - 1) / a_n

# a correlated Gaussian, rho = 0.9: mean-field updates m1 <- rho m2, m2 <- rho m1, variances 1 - rho^2
rho = 0.9
m1, m2 = -2.4, 2.4
for sweep in range(40): m1 = rho * m2; m2 = rho * m1
print(round(m1, 5), round(m2, 5), round(1 - rho ** 2, 2), round(-0.5 * np.log(1 - rho ** 2), 3))   # 0.00058 0.00052 0.19 0.83
```

## Next

Mean field is fast and deterministic, and its errors are systematic: the variances it reports are too small, by an amount it cannot measure. Each new model also needs its own derivation, and every update of a global factor reads all the data; [stochastic](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference) and [black-box](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference) variational inference, later in the course, remove these two limits. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling) uses the same complete conditionals differently: instead of averaging over the other blocks, it draws each block from its conditional at the current values of the others. The result is a Markov chain whose samples come, in the limit, from the exact posterior: unbiased, but noisy and slower.
