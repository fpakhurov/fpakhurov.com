The [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca) ended with variational EM: the posterior over the latent variables was replaced by the best member of a tractable family, while the parameters kept a point estimate. **Variational inference** goes one step further and treats every unknown, latent variables and parameters alike, as a random variable whose posterior is to be approximated. The question changes from "which parameters fit best" to "which simple distribution is closest to the posterior", and an integral without a closed form is replaced by an optimization problem that can be solved ([Jordan et al., 1999](#references)). This note derives the objective, the general update of the mean-field approximation, a complete example in which the exact answer is known for comparison, and the systematic errors of the approximation.

**Notation.** $X$ are the observations and $Z$ all unknowns, split into blocks $Z_1, \dots, Z_m$. $p(X, Z)$ is the joint density of the model, $p(Z \mid X)$ the posterior and $p(X)$ the evidence. $q(Z)$ is the approximation, $\mathcal Q$ the family it is chosen from, and $\mathcal L(q)$ the evidence lower bound (ELBO). $\mathbb E_{-j}[\cdot]$ is the expectation over all factors of $q$ except $q_j$. In the example, $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are observations of $\mathcal N(\mu, \lambda^{-1})$ with unknown mean $\mu$ and **precision** $\lambda$ (inverse variance), $\bar x$ is their mean, and $\mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda \mid a, b) = b^a \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda} / \Gamma(a)$ is the gamma density with shape $a$ and rate $b$, mean $a/b$. As in the [conjugate priors note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#normal-gamma-unknown-mean-and-precision), $\mu_0, \kappa_0, a_0, b_0$ are the parameters of the prior and $\mu_n, \kappa_n, a_n, b_n$ those of the exact posterior; $\omega$, $\tilde a$ and $\tilde b$ are the parameters of the variational factors.

## Baseline: the exact posterior, when there is one

Bayes' formula gives the posterior as $p(Z \mid X) = p(X, Z) / p(X)$, and everything hard is in the evidence $p(X) = \int p(X, Z)\, dZ$. For a [conjugate](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors) model the integral is known. The normal model with the **normal–gamma prior**, whose update is derived in the [conjugate priors note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#normal-gamma-unknown-mean-and-precision),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu \mid \lambda &\sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_0, (\kappa_0 \lambda)^{-1}\big), \\
\lambda &\sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)
\end{aligned}
$$

has a normal–gamma posterior, $\mu \mid \lambda, X \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_n, (\kappa_n \lambda)^{-1}\big)$ and $\lambda \mid X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a_n, b_n)$, with

$$
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_n &= \kappa_0 + n, \\
\mu_n &= \frac{\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n \bar x}{\kappa_0 + n}, \qquad a_n = a_0 + \frac n2, \\
b_n &= b_0 + \frac12 \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar x)^2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n (\bar x - \mu_0)^2}{2(\kappa_0 + n)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example.** The eight observations $3.0, -1.6, 1.4, 0.4, 0.5, 0.8, -1.0, 0.8$ have $\bar x = 0.5375$ and $\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 = 13.899$. With $\mu_0 = 0$, $\kappa_0 = 1$, $a_0 = b_0 = 2$, the posterior has $\kappa_n = 9$, $\mu_n = 0.478$, $a_n = 6$ and $b_n = 9.078$. The posterior mean of the precision is $a_n/b_n = 0.661$, and the marginal of $\mu$ is a Student $t$ with standard deviation $\sqrt{b_n / ((a_n - 1)\kappa_n)} = 0.449$. The log evidence is $\log p(X) = -15.5113$.

This note uses the normal model because its exact posterior is known: every approximation can be checked against it. The models of practice are rarely so kind. A Bayesian mixture of $K$ Gaussians has, for each of the $K^n$ ways of labelling the points, one conjugate term in its posterior; for $K = 3$ and $n = 100$ that is $3^{100} \approx 5 \cdot 10^{47}$ terms. A normal model whose prior makes $\mu$ and $\lambda$ independent, instead of linking the variance of $\mu$ to $\lambda$, already has a posterior outside any standard family. The [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) replaces such a posterior by a Gaussian fitted at one point, the mode. Variational inference fits an approximation to the whole distribution.

## The evidence lower bound and the reverse KL

For any $q$ that is positive wherever the posterior is, the decomposition of the [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca#em-as-coordinate-ascent-on-the-evidence-lower-bound) holds with all unknowns in $Z$:

$$
\log p(X) = \underbrace{\mathbb E_q\big[\log p(X, Z)\big] - \mathbb E_q\big[\log q(Z)\big]}_{\mathcal L(q)} + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(Z) \,\|\, p(Z \mid X)\big) .
$$

The left side does not depend on $q$, so maximizing $\mathcal L$ over a family $\mathcal Q$ and minimizing the KL divergence from $q$ to the posterior are the same problem. The bound needs only the joint density $p(X, Z)$, which the model writes down, never the evidence or the posterior. Since the KL divergence is non-negative, $\mathcal L(q) \le \log p(X)$ for every $q$, with equality only for the exact posterior.

The direction of the divergence is forced. The other one, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(Z \mid X) \,\|\, q)$, averages over the posterior, which is exactly what cannot be computed. The direction has consequences, worked out for a single Gaussian fitted to two modes in the [Generative Models course](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#forward-kl-covers-both-modes-reverse-kl-picks-one). The reverse divergence

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p) = \int q(Z) \log \frac{q(Z)}{p(Z \mid X)}\, dZ
$$

is an average over $q$. It becomes very large where $q$ puts mass and $p$ has almost none, and it costs nothing where $p$ has mass and $q$ has none. The optimal $q$ therefore stays inside the region where the posterior is large, **zero-forcing**, and when the posterior has several separated modes it settles on one, **mode-seeking**. The forward divergence of [maximum likelihood](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#kullbackleibler-divergence) does the opposite and covers all the mass. For the target $\tfrac12 \mathcal N(-3, 1) + \tfrac12 \mathcal N(3, 1)$, the Code section finds that the Gaussian minimizing the reverse KL is $\mathcal N(\pm 2.984, 1.023^2)$, one mode, with $D_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0.689$, close to $\log 2 = 0.693$, the price of ignoring half of the mass. The Gaussian minimizing the forward KL matches the moments, $\mathcal N(0, 10)$, and puts its peak where the target has almost nothing.

## Mean field and the general update

The **mean-field** family factorizes $q$ over the blocks and assumes nothing else about the factors:

$$
q(Z) = \prod_{j=1}^m q_j(Z_j) .
$$

The optimal form of each factor is derived, not chosen. Fix every factor except $q_j$. In $\mathcal L(q)$, the expectation of $\log p(X, Z)$ over the product is an expectation over $q_j$ of the expectation over the others, and the entropy of a product is the sum of the entropies of its factors:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(q) &= \int q_j(Z_j)\, \mathbb E_{-j}\big[\log p(X, Z)\big]\, dZ_j - \int q_j(Z_j) \log q_j(Z_j)\, dZ_j - \sum_{i \ne j} \int q_i \log q_i\, dZ_i \\
&= \int q_j(Z_j) \log \frac{\tilde p_j(Z_j)}{q_j(Z_j)}\, dZ_j + \log C_j + \text{const} \;=\; -D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_j \,\|\, \tilde p_j\big) + \log C_j + \text{const},
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\tilde p_j(Z_j) = \exp\big(\mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(X, Z)]\big) / C_j$ is a normalized density, $C_j$ its normalizing constant, and "const" collects the terms that do not involve $q_j$. The only term that depends on $q_j$ is a negative KL divergence, largest, at zero, when $q_j = \tilde p_j$. The optimal factor given the others is therefore

$$
\boxed{\;\log q_j^*(Z_j) = \mathbb E_{-j}\big[\log p(X, Z)\big] + \text{const}\;}
$$

with the constant fixed by normalization. Three remarks make the formula usable.

1. **Only the terms with $Z_j$ matter.** Since $\log p(X, Z) = \log p(Z_j \mid Z_{-j}, X) + \log p(Z_{-j}, X)$ and the second term does not involve $Z_j$, the update can equally be written with the **complete conditional**: $\log q_j^* = \mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(Z_j \mid Z_{-j}, X)] + \text{const}$. The [Gibbs sampler](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling) of the next note draws from the same complete conditional, at the current values of the other blocks instead of averaging over them.
2. **Conditional conjugacy gives closed forms.** Suppose the complete conditional of $Z_j$ is in an [exponential family](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#natural-parameters-and-sufficient-statistics), $p(Z_j \mid Z_{-j}, X) = h(Z_j) \exp\big(\eta_j^\top u(Z_j) - A(\eta_j)\big)$ with natural parameter $\eta_j = \eta_j(Z_{-j}, X)$. Its logarithm is linear in the sufficient statistics $u(Z_j)$, and $A(\eta_j)$ does not involve $Z_j$, so the update is $\log q_j^*(Z_j) = \log h(Z_j) + \mathbb E_{-j}[\eta_j]^\top u(Z_j) + \text{const}$: a member of the same family with natural parameter $\mathbb E_{-j}[\eta_j(Z_{-j}, X)]$ ([Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe, 2017](#references), Section 4). The model need not be conjugate as a whole; each block must be conjugate given the others.
3. **Coordinate ascent.** The updates depend on each other through the expectations, so they are cycled: **coordinate ascent variational inference** (CAVI). Each update maximizes $\mathcal L$ over one factor, so $\mathcal L$ never decreases, and it is bounded by $\log p(X)$, so the sequence of its values converges. $\mathcal L$ is not jointly concave in the factors in general, and the limit is a local optimum that can depend on the start.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Variational inference replaces an integral by an optimization: among tractable distributions, find the one with the largest evidence lower bound, which is the one closest to the posterior in reverse KL, a quantity computable from the joint density alone. Under the mean-field factorization the optimum of each factor given the others has a closed form, $\log q_j^* = \mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(X, Z)] + \text{const}$, and cycling through the factors climbs the bound.</aside>

## Worked example: a Gaussian with unknown mean and precision

Take the normal model of the baseline with the factorization $q(\mu, \lambda) = q(\mu)\, q(\lambda)$ ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 10.1.3). The exact posterior does not factorize, since the conditional variance of $\mu$ is $1/(\kappa_n \lambda)$, so the approximation has something to lose. The log joint is

$$
\log p(X, \mu, \lambda) = \frac n2 \log \lambda - \frac\lambda2 \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 + \frac12 \log(\kappa_0 \lambda) - \frac{\kappa_0 \lambda}{2} (\mu - \mu_0)^2 + (a_0 - 1) \log \lambda - b_0 \lambda + \text{const} .
$$

**The factor $q(\mu)$.** Keep the terms with $\mu$ and average over $q(\lambda)$, which only replaces $\lambda$ by $\mathbb E[\lambda]$:

$$
\log q^*(\mu) = -\frac{\mathbb E[\lambda]}{2} \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 + \kappa_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big) + \text{const} = -\frac{(\kappa_0 + n)\, \mathbb E[\lambda]}{2}\, (\mu - \mu_n)^2 + \text{const},
$$

by completing the square: the bracket is $(\kappa_0 + n)\mu^2 - 2\mu(\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n\bar x) + \text{const}$. So $q^*(\mu) = \mathcal N(\mu \mid \mu_n, \omega^{-1})$ with the exact posterior mean $\mu_n$ and precision $\omega = (\kappa_0 + n)\, \mathbb E[\lambda]$.

**The factor $q(\lambda)$.** Keep the terms with $\lambda$ and average over $q(\mu)$:

$$
\log q^*(\lambda) = \Big(a_0 - 1 + \frac{n + 1}{2}\Big) \log \lambda - \lambda \Big(b_0 + \frac12\, \mathbb E_\mu\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 + \kappa_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2\Big]\Big) + \text{const},
$$

the logarithm of a gamma density, $q^*(\lambda) = \mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda \mid \tilde a, \tilde b)$ with $\tilde a = a_0 + \frac{n + 1}{2}$ and $\tilde b = b_0 + \frac12\, \mathbb E_\mu[\cdots]$. The expectations under $q(\mu)$ are $\mathbb E_\mu\big[\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2\big] = \sum_i (x_i - \mu_n)^2 + n/\omega$ and $\mathbb E_\mu\big[(\mu - \mu_0)^2\big] = (\mu_n - \mu_0)^2 + 1/\omega$. The shape has $(n + 1)/2$ rather than the $n/2$ of the exact posterior because the prior of $\mu$, which also depends on $\lambda$, contributes its half.

**Iterating.** Start from $q(\mu) = \mathcal N(1.4, 0.2^2)$ and a $q(\lambda)$ with mean $1.2$. The first update of $q(\mu)$ sets its mean to $0.478$ and its precision to $9 \cdot 1.2 = 10.8$. The first update of $q(\lambda)$ gives $\tilde a = 6.5$ and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde b &= 2 + \tfrac12 \big(13.927 + 8/10.8 + 0.478^2 + 1/10.8\big) = 2 + \tfrac12 \cdot 14.988 = 9.494, \\
\mathbb E[\lambda] &= 6.5/9.494 = 0.685 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The bound climbs from $-21.596$ at the start through $-16.867$, $-15.622$, $-15.556$, $-15.553$ after the first four updates, and settles at $-15.5524$. The log evidence is $-15.5113$; the gap $0.0411$ is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p)$, and the Code section computes the same value by integrating the divergence numerically.

**The fixed point in closed form.** At convergence, $\mathbb E[\lambda]$ is exact and the variance of $\mu$ is not. Write $\sum_i (x_i - \mu_n)^2 + \kappa_0 (\mu_n - \mu_0)^2 = 2(b_n - b_0)$, an identity that follows from $\bar x - \mu_n = \kappa_0(\bar x - \mu_0)/(\kappa_0 + n)$ and $\mu_n - \mu_0 = n(\bar x - \mu_0)/(\kappa_0 + n)$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde b &= b_n + \frac{n + \kappa_0}{2\omega} = b_n + \frac{1}{2\, \mathbb E[\lambda]}, \qquad \tilde a = a_n + \frac12, \\
\mathbb E[\lambda] &= \frac{\tilde a}{\tilde b} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbb E[\lambda]\, b_n + \frac12 = a_n + \frac12 \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathbb E[\lambda] = \frac{a_n}{b_n} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The mean of the precision is exactly the posterior mean, $0.661$. The variance of $\mu$ under $q$ is $1/\omega = b_n/(a_n\kappa_n)$, against the exact $b_n/((a_n - 1)\kappa_n)$:

$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}_q(\mu)}{\operatorname{Var}_p(\mu)} = \frac{a_n - 1}{a_n} = \frac56 = 0.833, \qquad \text{standard deviation } 0.410 \text{ instead of } 0.449 .
$$

The exact marginal of $\mu$ is a Student $t$, a mixture of Gaussians of variances $1/(\kappa_n \lambda)$ over the uncertain $\lambda$; small values of $\lambda$ widen it. The factorized $q$ uses one Gaussian with the average precision and loses the extra spread. The error shrinks as $a_n = a_0 + n/2$ grows: with more data the precision is better determined, and the coupling matters less. The standard deviation of $\lambda$ is also slightly too small, $0.259$ against $0.270$.

## What mean field gets wrong

**It ignores correlations and underestimates variances.** Let the posterior be a Gaussian $\mathcal N(m, \Lambda^{-1})$ in two blocks, with precision matrix $\Lambda$. The general update gives

$$
\log q_1^*(z_1) = \mathbb E_{z_2}\Big[-\tfrac12 (z - m)^\top \Lambda (z - m)\Big] + \text{const} = -\tfrac12 \Lambda_{11} z_1^2 + z_1 \big(\Lambda_{11} m_1 - \Lambda_{12} (\mathbb E[z_2] - m_2)\big) + \text{const},
$$

so $q_1^* = \mathcal N\big(m_1 - \Lambda_{11}^{-1} \Lambda_{12} (\mathbb E[z_2] - m_2),\; \Lambda_{11}^{-1}\big)$, and symmetrically for $q_2$. The means converge to the exact means $m$. The variance of each factor is $1/\Lambda_{11}$, the variance of $z_1$ **given** $z_2$, not its marginal variance $\Sigma_{11}$, and it is never larger. For unit variances and correlation $\rho$, $\Lambda_{11} = 1/(1 - \rho^2)$, and with $\rho = 0.9$ each factor has variance $0.19$ instead of 1, standard deviation $0.436$ instead of 1. The divergence at the optimum is $-\frac12 \log(1 - \rho^2) = 0.830$. The best factorized $q$ in the forward direction is the product of the exact marginals, with variance 1 and the same divergence, $0.830$; the two directions coincide here only because of the symmetry of the example.

The coordinate updates of the means are $m_1 \leftarrow \rho\, m_2$ and $m_2 \leftarrow \rho\, m_1$ (for $m = 0$), the conditional means of the target. Each sweep multiplies the distance to the optimum by $\rho^2 = 0.81$, the same factor that slows the Gibbs sampler on this target in the [MCMC note](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#gibbs-sampling): from $(-2.4, 2.4)$, forty sweeps leave the means at $0.00058$ and $0.00052$.

**It seeks one mode.** A posterior with separated modes is approximated by a $q$ that covers one of them, as in the two-mode example above. The posterior of a mixture model always has this structure: relabelling the components gives $K!$ equivalent modes. For predictions the choice of mode does not matter, but the bound then misses about $\log K!$ of the log evidence, and [Bishop (2006](#references), Section 10.2.4) adds $\log K!$ back before comparing mixtures with different $K$.

**Its bound is a biased model score.** $\mathcal L(q) = \log p(X) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p)$, and the divergence differs from model to model. Comparing two models by their bounds compares their evidences minus two different gaps; the comparison is reliable only when the gaps are small compared with the difference in evidence.

**Its uncertainty is overconfident in a systematic direction.** Because the reverse KL penalizes mass where the posterior is small, the variances of $q$ tend to be too small, as in both examples of this note. Credible intervals from mean field are then typically narrower than the exact ones, and the error does not shrink with more iterations, only with a richer family $\mathcal Q$ or with more data.

## Beyond the normal model

The same recipe applies to any model whose blocks are conditionally conjugate. For a Bayesian mixture of Gaussians with a Dirichlet prior on the weights and normal–Wishart priors on the components, the factorization $q(Z)\, q(\pi, \mu, \Lambda)$ gives updates that look like EM: a responsibility for each point and component, computed with $\mathbb E[\log \pi_k]$ and $\mathbb E[\log \det \Lambda_k]$ in place of the point estimates, and Dirichlet and normal–Wishart factors whose parameters are the prior pseudo-counts plus responsibility-weighted statistics ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 10.2). Components that explain few points see their weights driven towards zero, so that, as in Bayesian PCA, a model started with too many components switches the extra ones off. [Latent Dirichlet allocation](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation), later in the course, is fitted the same way, and the [variational autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/vae#variational-approximation-and-the-elbo) keeps the bound of this note and replaces the closed-form factors by a neural network.

## What changed

| | Exact posterior | Laplace approximation | Mean-field variational inference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What is computed | $p(Z \mid X)$ and $p(X)$ | a Gaussian at the mode | the best factorized $q$ |
| Needs | the evidence integral | the mode and the Hessian | expectations of $\log p(X, Z)$ under the factors |
| Objective | none | none | $\mathcal L(q) = \log p(X) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p)$ |
| Shape of the answer | anything | Gaussian | each factor's form follows from the model |
| Normal model, sd of $\mu$ | $0.449$ | — | $0.410$ |
| Normal model, $\mathbb E[\lambda]$ | $0.661$ | — | $0.661$ |
| Evidence | $-15.5113$ | an approximation | a lower bound, $-15.5524$ |

## Limitations

- **Underestimated uncertainty.** The reverse KL and the factorization both make $q$ too narrow, by a factor that the method itself does not report; only a comparison with a more exact method, such as the sampler of the next note, reveals it.
- **Correlations are lost by construction.** Dependencies between blocks are invisible to the factorized $q$; grouping strongly coupled variables into one block, or a structured family, is the remedy.
- **Local optima and dependence on the start.** CAVI climbs a non-concave bound; for mixtures and topic models different starts give different answers, and the bound can be used to choose among them.
- **Derivations per model.** Each new model needs its own expectations and updates, and models without conditional conjugacy need further approximations; the [black-box methods](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference) at the end of the course remove this requirement at the price of noisy gradients.
- **Full passes over the data.** Every CAVI update of a global factor uses all observations; [stochastic variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference), later in the course, replaces these passes by mini-batches.

## What you should remember

- $\log p(X) = \mathcal L(q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p(Z \mid X))$ with $\mathcal L(q) = \mathbb E_q[\log p(X, Z)] - \mathbb E_q[\log q]$; maximizing the bound minimizes the reverse KL and needs only the joint density.
- The reverse KL is zero-forcing and mode-seeking; the forward KL, which would need the posterior, is mass-covering.
- Mean field: $q(Z) = \prod_j q_j(Z_j)$, and the optimal factor given the others is $\log q_j^* = \mathbb E_{-j}[\log p(X, Z)] + \text{const}$; with conditional conjugacy, each factor is in the family of its complete conditional, with averaged natural parameters.
- CAVI cycles the updates; the bound never decreases and converges to a local optimum.
- Normal model with a normal–gamma prior: $q(\mu) = \mathcal N(\mu_n, ((\kappa_0 + n)\mathbb E[\lambda])^{-1})$, $q(\lambda) = \mathrm{Gamma}(a_0 + \frac{n+1}{2}, \tilde b)$; at the fixed point $\mathbb E[\lambda]$ is exact and the variance of $\mu$ is too small by the factor $(a_n - 1)/a_n$.
- For a correlated Gaussian, mean field gives the right means and the conditional variances $1/\Lambda_{jj}$ instead of the marginal ones.

## Code

The exact posterior and evidence of the normal model, CAVI with the bound at every update, the gap computed as a KL divergence by numerical integration, the closed-form variance ratio, mean field for a correlated Gaussian, and the two KL directions on two modes. NumPy and SciPy only; exact numbers can differ slightly across platforms and library versions. The script runs in a few seconds, most of it in the numerical integration.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import gammaln, digamma
from scipy.stats import norm, gamma
from scipy.integrate import dblquad
from scipy.optimize import minimize

# the normal model: x_i ~ N(mu, 1/tau), prior mu | tau ~ N(mu0, 1/(l0 tau)), tau ~ Gam(a0, b0) (rate b0)
# tau is the precision lambda of the text, l0 is kappa_0, and lam below is the precision omega of q(mu)
x = np.array([3.0, -1.6, 1.4, 0.4, 0.5, 0.8, -1.0, 0.8])
N, xbar = len(x), x.mean()
mu0, l0, a0, b0 = 0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 2.0

# exact posterior: normal-gamma, and the exact log evidence
lN, mN, aE = l0 + N, (l0 * mu0 + N * xbar) / (l0 + N), a0 + N / 2
bE = b0 + 0.5 * ((x - xbar) ** 2).sum() + l0 * N * (xbar - mu0) ** 2 / (2 * lN)
logZ = gammaln(aE) - gammaln(a0) + a0 * np.log(b0) - aE * np.log(bE) + 0.5 * np.log(l0 / lN) - N / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi)
print(round(mN, 3), round(aE / bE, 3), round(np.sqrt(bE / ((aE - 1) * lN)), 3), round(logZ, 4))   # 0.478 0.661 0.449 -15.5113

def elbo(m, lam, a, b):                  # L(q) for q(mu) = N(m, 1/lam), q(tau) = Gam(a, b)
    Elog, Et = digamma(a) - np.log(b), a / b
    Esq, Ep = ((x - m) ** 2).sum() + N / lam, (m - mu0) ** 2 + 1 / lam
    lik = N / 2 * Elog - N / 2 * np.log(2 * np.pi) - Et * Esq / 2
    pmu = 0.5 * (np.log(l0) + Elog - np.log(2 * np.pi)) - l0 * Et * Ep / 2
    ptau = a0 * np.log(b0) - gammaln(a0) + (a0 - 1) * Elog - b0 * Et
    Hmu, Htau = 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi * np.e / lam), a - np.log(b) + gammaln(a) + (1 - a) * digamma(a)
    return lik + pmu + ptau + Hmu + Htau

# coordinate ascent from q(mu) = N(1.4, 0.2^2), q(tau) = Gam(6.5, 6.5 / 1.2)
m, lam, a, b = 1.4, 25.0, 6.5, 6.5 / 1.2
trace = [elbo(m, lam, a, b)]
for sweep in range(30):
    m, lam = mN, lN * a / b                                           # update q(mu)
    trace.append(elbo(m, lam, a, b))
    a = a0 + (N + 1) / 2                                              # update q(tau)
    b = b0 + 0.5 * (((x - m) ** 2).sum() + N / lam + l0 * ((m - mu0) ** 2 + 1 / lam))
    trace.append(elbo(m, lam, a, b))
    if sweep == 0: print(round(lam, 2), round(a / b, 3))              # 10.8 0.685
print(np.round(trace[:5], 4), bool(np.all(np.diff(trace) >= -1e-12)))
# [-21.5957 -16.8674 -15.622  -15.5562 -15.5527] True
print(round(a / b, 3), round(1 / np.sqrt(lam), 3), round(np.sqrt(a) / b, 3), round(np.sqrt(aE) / bE, 3))
# 0.661 0.41 0.259 0.27: E[tau] is exact, the standard deviations are too small
print(round(trace[-1], 4), round(logZ - trace[-1], 4))              # -15.5524 0.0411

# the gap is KL(q || p(mu, tau | x)), here by numerical integration
logq = lambda mu, t: norm.logpdf(mu, m, 1 / np.sqrt(lam)) + gamma.logpdf(t, a, scale=1 / b)
logp = lambda mu, t: norm.logpdf(mu, mN, 1 / np.sqrt(lN * t)) + gamma.logpdf(t, aE, scale=1 / bE)
kl = dblquad(lambda mu, t: np.exp(logq(mu, t)) * (logq(mu, t) - logp(mu, t)), 1e-6, 5, -4, 5)[0]
print(round(kl, 4))                                                  # 0.0411
print(round((aE - 1) / aE, 4), round((1 / lam) / (bE / ((aE - 1) * lN)), 4))   # 0.8333 0.8333: Var_q / Var_p = (a_n - 1) / a_n

# a correlated Gaussian, rho = 0.9: mean-field updates m1 <- rho m2, m2 <- rho m1, variances 1 - rho^2
rho = 0.9
m1, m2 = -2.4, 2.4
for sweep in range(40): m1 = rho * m2; m2 = rho * m1
print(round(m1, 5), round(m2, 5), round(1 - rho ** 2, 2), round(-0.5 * np.log(1 - rho ** 2), 3))   # 0.00058 0.00052 0.19 0.83

# one Gaussian for two modes, p = 0.5 N(-3, 1) + 0.5 N(3, 1): reverse KL picks a mode, forward KL covers both
logp1 = lambda z: np.logaddexp(norm.logpdf(z, -3, 1), norm.logpdf(z, 3, 1)) + np.log(0.5)
zs = np.linspace(-12, 12, 4001); dz = zs[1] - zs[0]
def rev(v):                                  # KL(q || p) for q = N(v[0], exp(v[1])^2), by quadrature
    lq = norm.logpdf(zs, v[0], np.exp(v[1]))
    return np.sum(np.exp(lq) * (lq - logp1(zs))) * dz
for start in ((-1.0, 0.0), (0.5, 0.0)):
    r = minimize(rev, start, method='Nelder-Mead', options={'xatol': 1e-6, 'fatol': 1e-10})
    print(np.round([r.x[0], np.exp(r.x[1]), r.fun], 3))          # [-2.984  1.023  0.689], then [2.984 1.023 0.689]
print(0.0, round(np.sqrt(1 + 9), 3))         # 0.0 3.162: forward KL matches the moments, mean 0 and variance 1 + 3^2
```

## Next

Mean field is fast and deterministic, and its errors are systematic: the variances it reports are too small by an amount it cannot measure. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling) uses the same complete conditionals in a different way. Instead of averaging over the other blocks, it draws each block from its conditional at the current values of the others. The result is a Markov chain whose samples are, in the limit, from the exact posterior: unbiased, but noisy and slower.
