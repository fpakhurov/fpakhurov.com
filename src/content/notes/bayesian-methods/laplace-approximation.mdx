The [relevance vector machine](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine) needed two things in closed form: the posterior over the weights and the evidence. Both came from the Gaussian likelihood of regression. For classification the targets are 0 or 1, the likelihood is a product of sigmoids, and the integral over the weights that normalizes the posterior has no formula. This note introduces the simplest way around it, the first approximate posterior of the course: replace the posterior by a Gaussian centred at its mode, with the curvature of the log-posterior there as its precision. This **Laplace approximation** turns logistic regression into Bayesian logistic regression, gives predictions whose confidence falls away from the data, and lets the relevance vector machine classify. It also has blind spots, which the last section shows and the next part of the course addresses.

**Notation.** $\theta \in \mathbb R^d$ is a parameter with unnormalized posterior $\tilde p(\theta) = p(D \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)$ and normalizer $Z = \int \tilde p(\theta)\, d\theta = p(D)$, the evidence. $\hat\theta$ is the mode and $A = -\nabla\nabla \log \tilde p(\hat\theta)$ the negative Hessian there. For classification, the targets are $t_i \in \{0, 1\}$, $\sigma(a) = 1/(1 + e^{-a})$ is the logistic sigmoid, $y_i = \sigma(w^\top\varphi(x_i))$ the predicted probability of class 1, $\Phi$ the design matrix as before, and $B = \operatorname{diag}\big(y_i(1 - y_i)\big)$. $\Phi_{\mathcal N}(z)$ is the standard normal distribution function, written with a subscript to keep it apart from the design matrix. For a new input, $a = w^\top\varphi_*$ is its **activation**.

## Baseline: logistic regression with a point estimate

[Logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression) fits $w$ by maximizing the log-likelihood $\sum_i \big(t_i\log y_i + (1 - t_i)\log(1 - y_i)\big)$, or, with a Gaussian prior $\mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$, the log-posterior, which adds $-\frac\alpha2\lVert w\rVert^2$. The prediction is the plug-in $\sigma(\hat w^\top\varphi_*)$. Two problems are familiar from regression.

- **The probability ignores the uncertainty in $w$.** Far from the data, $\lvert\hat w^\top\varphi_*\rvert$ grows and the plug-in heads to 0 or 1, however few points determined $\hat w$. On [separable data](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#separable-data-the-weights-run-away) the maximum likelihood weights run off to infinity and every probability becomes 0 or 1.
- **No evidence.** Without the normalizer $Z$ the prior strength $\alpha$ cannot be set by the evidence, and the relevance vector machine cannot run at all.

The fully Bayesian prediction averages the sigmoid over the posterior, $p(t_* = 1 \mid x_*, t) = \int \sigma(w^\top\varphi_*)\, p(w \mid t)\, dw$, and the posterior is what we cannot compute.

## The Laplace approximation

Here is the idea: near its maximum, any smooth log-density looks like a parabola, and a density whose logarithm is a parabola is a Gaussian. Expand $\log\tilde p$ to second order around the mode $\hat\theta$, where the gradient vanishes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\tilde p(\theta) &\approx \log\tilde p(\hat\theta) + \nabla\log\tilde p(\hat\theta)^\top (\theta - \hat\theta) + \tfrac12 (\theta - \hat\theta)^\top \nabla\nabla\log\tilde p(\hat\theta)\, (\theta - \hat\theta) \\
&= \log\tilde p(\hat\theta) - \tfrac12 (\theta - \hat\theta)^\top A\, (\theta - \hat\theta) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the [second-order Taylor expansion](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians#the-hessian-and-the-second-order-taylor-expansion) of the Mathematics course; the second uses $\nabla\log\tilde p(\hat\theta) = 0$ and the definition of $A$. Exponentiating, $\tilde p(\theta) \approx \tilde p(\hat\theta) \exp\big(-\frac12(\theta - \hat\theta)^\top A (\theta - \hat\theta)\big)$, an unnormalized Gaussian. Its normalizer is the Gaussian integral of the [previous notes](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#tools-matrix-derivatives-and-gaussian-identities), so

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p(\theta \mid D) &\approx q(\theta) = \mathcal N\big(\theta \mid \hat\theta,\ A^{-1}\big), \\
Z &\approx \tilde p(\hat\theta)\, (2\pi)^{d/2} \det(A)^{-1/2}, \\
A &= -\nabla\nabla\log\tilde p(\hat\theta)
\end{aligned}
}
$$

The approximation needs $A$ to be positive definite, which holds at a strict local maximum, and a smooth $\log\tilde p$. Its two ingredients are an optimizer, which finds $\hat\theta$, and a second derivative at one point. For a linear-Gaussian model the log-posterior is exactly quadratic and the approximation is exact; that is how the evidence of [Bayesian linear regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#evidence-maximization-for-the-hyperparameters) was computed. The logarithm of the $Z$ formula is the Laplace approximation of the evidence, whose large-sample limit is the BIC; the [model selection note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection#the-bic-approximation) derives that limit. The method is old: Laplace used it for integrals of this kind, and [Tierney and Kadane (1986)](#references) made it a standard tool for posterior moments.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Replace the posterior by the Gaussian that matches it at the mode: the same location, and the same curvature of the logarithm. Everything that is easy for Gaussians, predictions, marginals and the evidence, then becomes available for any model whose log-posterior can be maximized and differentiated twice.</aside>

**Worked example.** A coin with a uniform prior shows 3 heads and 1 tail. The exact posterior is $\mathrm{Beta}(4, 2)$, with $\log\tilde p(p) = 3\log p + \log(1 - p)$. The derivative $3/p - 1/(1 - p)$ vanishes at the mode $\hat p = 3/4$, and the second derivative there is $-3/\hat p^2 - 1/(1 - \hat p)^2 = -16/3 - 16 = -21.33$. So $q = \mathcal N(0.75,\ 1/21.33)$, a standard deviation of $0.2165$. The evidence estimate is $\hat p^3 (1 - \hat p) \sqrt{2\pi/21.33} = 0.1055 \cdot 0.5427 = 0.0572$, against the exact $B(4, 2) = 1/20 = 0.05$: 14.5% too large. The posterior has only four observations and is visibly skewed, which the last section returns to.

## Bayesian logistic regression

With the prior $\mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$, the log-posterior of logistic regression is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(w \mid t) &= \sum_{i=1}^n \big(t_i\log y_i + (1 - t_i)\log(1 - y_i)\big) - \frac\alpha2\lVert w\rVert^2 + \text{const}, \\
y_i &= \sigma\big(w^\top\varphi(x_i)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Its gradient is the [residuals times the inputs](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs) minus the prior term. Differentiating once more, with $\partial y_i/\partial w = y_i(1 - y_i)\,\varphi(x_i)$ from $\sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\log p(w \mid t) &= \Phi^\top(t - y) - \alpha w, \\
\nabla\nabla\log p(w \mid t) &= -\big(\Phi^\top B\, \Phi + \alpha I\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The Hessian is negative definite for every $w$, because $B$ has positive entries and $\alpha \gt 0$. So the log-posterior is strictly concave: it has exactly one maximum, and [Newton's method](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization#newtons-method) finds it quickly. A Newton step solves the quadratic model of the log-posterior exactly,

$$
w^{\text{new}} = w + \big(\Phi^\top B\, \Phi + \alpha I\big)^{-1}\big(\Phi^\top(t - y) - \alpha w\big),
$$

with $B$ and $y$ evaluated at the current $w$. Each step is a weighted least-squares problem with weights $B$, which is why the method is also called **iteratively reweighted least squares** (IRLS). At convergence, the Laplace approximation is

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p(w \mid t) &\approx \mathcal N\big(w \mid \hat w,\ \Sigma\big), \\
\Sigma &= \big(\Phi^\top B\, \Phi + \alpha I\big)^{-1}\ \text{with } B \text{ at } \hat w
\end{aligned}
}
$$

the same form as in regression, with the noise precision $\beta$ replaced by a precision $y_i(1 - y_i)$ per point. A point the model classifies confidently ($y_i$ near 0 or 1) contributes little curvature; points near the boundary, with $y_i$ near $\frac12$, contribute the most. The prior keeps the mode finite even on separable data, where maximum likelihood would diverge.

**Worked example.** One weight and no bias, $p(t = 1 \mid x) = \sigma(wx)$, five points $x = (-1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1, 2)$ with $t = (0, 1, 0, 1, 1)$, and $\alpha = 1$. From $w = 0$, Newton's method gives $0.596$, $0.630$ and $0.6305$, after which the digits stop changing. At the mode, $\sum_i x_i^2\, y_i(1 - y_i) + \alpha = 2.490$, so the Laplace approximation is $\mathcal N(0.6305,\ 1/2.490)$, a standard deviation of $0.634$, as large as the mode itself: the five points favour a positive weight but say little about its size.

<LaplaceFlow />

1. Newton's method climbs the concave log-posterior to its mode $\hat w$.
2. The curvature at the mode is the precision $A$ of the Gaussian approximation.
3. At a new input the activation $a = w x_*$ is Gaussian under the approximation.
4. Averaging the sigmoid over that Gaussian gives the moderated probability of the next section.

## The predictive distribution and the moderated output

Under the approximation, the activation $a = w^\top\varphi_*$ is an [affine map](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps) of a Gaussian, so it is Gaussian too, with mean and variance

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_a &= \hat w^\top\varphi_*, \\
s_a^2 &= \varphi_*^\top\Sigma\,\varphi_* .
\end{aligned}
$$

The predictive probability is a one-dimensional integral, $p(t_* = 1 \mid x_*, t) \approx \int \sigma(a)\, \mathcal N(a \mid \mu_a, s_a^2)\, da$, which has no closed form either, but has an excellent approximation. The trick: the sigmoid is very close to a rescaled normal distribution function, and a normal distribution function averaged over a Gaussian is again one.

**Step 1: the probit approximation.** Approximate $\sigma(a) \approx \Phi_{\mathcal N}(\lambda a)$ and choose $\lambda$ so that the two curves have the same slope at 0: $\sigma'(0) = \frac14$ and $\frac{d}{da}\Phi_{\mathcal N}(\lambda a)\big|_0 = \lambda/\sqrt{2\pi}$, so $\lambda^2 = \pi/8$.

**Step 2: average the normal distribution function.** Let $Z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ be independent of $a = \mu_a + s_a\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$. Then $\Phi_{\mathcal N}(\lambda a) = P(Z \le \lambda a \mid a)$, and averaging over $a$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int \Phi_{\mathcal N}(\lambda a)\, \mathcal N(a \mid \mu_a, s_a^2)\, da &= P\big(Z - \lambda s_a\varepsilon \le \lambda\mu_a\big) \\
&= \Phi_{\mathcal N}\Big(\frac{\lambda\mu_a}{\sqrt{1 + \lambda^2 s_a^2}}\Big) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

because $Z - \lambda s_a\varepsilon$ is a sum of independent Gaussians with variance $1 + \lambda^2 s_a^2$.

**Step 3: back to the sigmoid.** Using $\Phi_{\mathcal N}(\lambda u) \approx \sigma(u)$ once more, with $u = \mu_a/\sqrt{1 + \lambda^2 s_a^2}$:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p(t_* = 1 \mid x_*, t) &\approx \sigma\big(\kappa(s_a^2)\, \mu_a\big), \\
\kappa(s^2) &= \Big(1 + \frac{\pi s^2}{8}\Big)^{-1/2}
\end{aligned}
}
$$

This is the **moderated output** of [MacKay (1992b)](#references). Since $0 \lt \kappa \le 1$, it is the plug-in prediction with its activation shrunk towards 0 by an amount that depends on the uncertainty at that input. Two consequences follow. The sign of $\kappa\mu_a$ is the sign of $\mu_a$, so the decision boundary $p = \frac12$ is the same as for the plug-in, and so is any classification at that threshold; what changes is the confidence, and with it every decision at another threshold and every log-loss. And where $s_a^2$ is large, far from the data, the probability stays near $\frac12$ instead of running to 0 or 1.

**Worked example.** In the one-weight example at $x_* = 3$, $\mu_a = 3 \cdot 0.6305 = 1.891$ and $s_a^2 = 9/2.490 = 3.614$. Then $\kappa = (1 + \pi \cdot 3.614/8)^{-1/2} = 0.643$, and the moderated probability is $\sigma(0.643 \cdot 1.891) = 0.771$, against the plug-in $\sigma(1.891) = 0.869$. The exact Gaussian average $\int\sigma(a)\,\mathcal N(a \mid 1.891, 3.614)\, da = 0.769$ shows that the probit step costs only $0.002$. The fully Bayesian answer, the sigmoid averaged over the true posterior of $w$ by numerical integration, is $0.790$: the Laplace approximation recovers most of the correction, not all of it.

<ModeratedOutput />

With a bias and six points, the pattern is the same along the whole axis. At $x = 3$ the plug-in gives $0.890$, the moderated output $0.790$ and the exact predictive $0.814$; at $x = 1$, inside the data, $0.669$, $0.649$ and $0.663$. The moderation goes in the right direction; on these small examples it goes slightly too far. In the one-weight example the reason can be read off the exact posterior: its mean is $0.709$, to the right of the mode $0.6305$, and its skewness is $0.21$. Larger weights, and so more confident predictions, are more probable than the symmetric Gaussian around the mode allows.

## The evidence and relevance vector classification

The Laplace estimate of $Z$ gives the evidence of the prior precisions. With a prior $\mathcal N(0, A_\alpha^{-1})$, $A_\alpha = \operatorname{diag}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m)$, one precision per weight as in the relevance vector machine, and $\hat w$ the mode for the current $\alpha$:

$$
\log p(t \mid \alpha) \approx \log p(t \mid \hat w) + \log \mathcal N\big(\hat w \mid 0, A_\alpha^{-1}\big) + \frac m2\log 2\pi - \frac12\log\det\big(\Phi^\top B\,\Phi + A_\alpha\big) .
$$

This has the shape of the regression evidence, and [Tipping (2001)](#references), following [MacKay (1992b)](#references), maximizes it with the same updates, using the Laplace mean and covariance in place of the exact ones. Relevance vector classification alternates:

1. for the current $\alpha$, find the mode $\hat w$ by Newton's method (warm-started from the previous mode);
2. compute $\Sigma = (\Phi^\top B\,\Phi + A_\alpha)^{-1}$ at the mode;
3. update $\gamma_i = 1 - \alpha_i\Sigma_{ii}$ and $\alpha_i = \gamma_i/\hat w_i^2$, and prune the features whose $\alpha_i$ passes a large threshold;
4. repeat until the precisions settle.

The analogy with regression is exact in one sense. Setting the gradient to zero at the mode, $\Phi^\top(t - y) = A_\alpha\hat w$, and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat w &= \Sigma\,\Phi^\top B\,\hat t, \\
\hat t &= \Phi\hat w + B^{-1}(t - y) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

the regression posterior mean for the targets $\hat t$ with a noise precision $y_i(1 - y_i)$ of its own for every point: the Laplace approximation maps classification to a regression with heteroscedastic noise ([Tipping, 2001](#references), Section 3). Because the log-posterior of this linear model is concave, the posterior is unimodal and the Gaussian approximation is much safer than for neural networks, whose posteriors have many modes.

**Worked example.** On 200 points of the two-moons data (scikit-learn's `make_moons` with noise $0.3$), with a bias and one Gaussian kernel of width $0.5$ per point, 201 features, the loop ran 279 iterations and kept 7 relevance vectors. On 2000 test points from the same generator the test error is $0.109$, and the test log-loss is $0.277$ for the plug-in probabilities and $0.257$ for the moderated ones. An SVM with the same kernel and $C$ chosen by 5-fold cross-validation keeps 94 support vectors and has test error $0.0965$. In Tipping's own comparison, on 100 points of Ripley's two-class mixture data with a Gaussian kernel of width $0.5$, the RVM used 4 relevance vectors and the SVM 38, with test errors of $9.3\%$ and $10.6\%$ on 1000 test points; Tipping also observed that the relevance vectors lie away from the decision boundary, as prototypes of their classes rather than as boundary points ([Tipping, 2001](#references), Section 4.2). The two experiments differ in data and settings, and neither ranks the methods; both show the difference in sparsity.

## What the Laplace approximation misses

The approximation looks at one point of the posterior: its location and its curvature there. Whatever the posterior does away from the mode is invisible to it.

<LaplaceMisses />

- **Skewness and bounded support.** The coin's $\mathrm{Beta}(4, 2)$ has its mode at $0.75$ but its mean at $0.667$, and its density is zero above 1. The Gaussian is symmetric, centred at the mode, and puts $12.4\%$ of its mass on the impossible values $p \gt 1$. In the logistic example the same skewness made the moderation slightly too strong.
- **Several modes.** If only $\theta^2$ is observed ($t = 2$ with noise standard deviation $0.5$, prior $\mathcal N(0, 2^2)$), the posterior has two equal peaks at $\pm 1.403$, and its standard deviation is $1.377$. The Laplace approximation at the positive mode, $\mathcal N(1.403,\ 0.178^2)$, represents half of the mass and reports a standard deviation eight times too small. The same symmetry appears in mixtures (relabelling the components) and in neural networks (permuting hidden units, flipping signs), whose posteriors have very many equivalent modes.
- **The parametrization.** The mode and the curvature depend on the coordinates. Applying the method to the log-odds $\log\frac{p}{1 - p}$ instead of $p$ gives a different approximation of the same posterior, and the choice is a modelling decision.
- **Heavy tails and flat directions.** The curvature at the mode says nothing about how slowly the density decays far away, and a nearly flat direction gives a nearly singular $A$.

The approximation is good when the posterior is close to Gaussian: unimodal, with many observations per parameter. The [Bernstein–von Mises theorem](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#priors-and-large-samples) says that this is what happens as the sample grows, under regularity conditions, and log-concave posteriors such as that of logistic regression have a single mode and light tails. When these conditions fail, the next part of the course offers two alternatives: [variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference), which chooses the Gaussian, or another tractable family, by minimizing a divergence to the whole posterior rather than by matching one point, and sampling methods such as [Gibbs sampling](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling) and the [Markov chain Monte Carlo](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo) of the probability course, which can represent any shape at the price of computation.

## What changed

| | Logistic regression with a point estimate | Bayesian logistic regression with Laplace |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The weights | the mode $\hat w$ | $\mathcal N(\hat w, \Sigma)$, $\Sigma = (\Phi^\top B\,\Phi + \alpha I)^{-1}$ |
| Fitting | Newton's method (IRLS) | the same, plus one Hessian at the mode |
| Prediction | $\sigma(\hat w^\top\varphi_*)$ | $\sigma\big(\kappa(s_a^2)\,\hat w^\top\varphi_*\big)$, $s_a^2 = \varphi_*^\top\Sigma\varphi_*$ |
| Far from the data | confident, towards 0 or 1 | towards $\frac12$ |
| Decision boundary at $\frac12$ | $\hat w^\top\varphi = 0$ | the same |
| Evidence | not available | $\tilde p(\hat w)\,(2\pi)^{m/2}\det(A)^{-1/2}$ |
| Sparse kernel classifier | the SVM, with $C$ by cross-validation | the RVM, with one $\alpha_i$ per kernel by the evidence |

## Limitations

- **One point of the posterior.** Skewness, bounded support, heavy tails and multiple modes are invisible to it; the approximation can be far off with few data or many parameters.
- **Not invariant.** The result depends on the parametrization, and the mode of a density is not a natural summary of it.
- **The Hessian.** It costs $O(m^2)$ memory and $O(m^3)$ time to invert; for large models it is approximated (by its diagonal, or by low-rank or Kronecker-factored forms), which adds another layer of approximation.
- **Approximate evidence, approximate sparsity.** In relevance vector classification the evidence that drives the pruning is itself an approximation, evaluated at a mode that moves as the precisions change.

## What you should remember

- Laplace: $q(\theta) = \mathcal N(\hat\theta, A^{-1})$ with $\hat\theta$ the mode and $A = -\nabla\nabla\log\tilde p(\hat\theta)$; the evidence is $Z \approx \tilde p(\hat\theta)\,(2\pi)^{d/2}\det(A)^{-1/2}$. It is exact for linear-Gaussian models.
- Bayesian logistic regression: gradient $\Phi^\top(t - y) - \alpha w$, Hessian $-(\Phi^\top B\,\Phi + \alpha I)$ with $B = \operatorname{diag}(y_i(1 - y_i))$; the log-posterior is concave, Newton's method finds the mode, and $\Sigma = (\Phi^\top B\,\Phi + \alpha I)^{-1}$.
- The activation is Gaussian, $\mathcal N(\hat w^\top\varphi_*, \varphi_*^\top\Sigma\varphi_*)$; the probit approximation gives the moderated output $\sigma(\kappa\mu_a)$, $\kappa = (1 + \pi s_a^2/8)^{-1/2}$, which keeps the boundary and lowers the confidence where the weights are uncertain.
- Relevance vector classification runs the regression updates with the Laplace mean and covariance; classification becomes regression with a noise precision $y_i(1 - y_i)$ per point.
- The approximation misses skewness, bounded support and multiple modes; it is good for unimodal, nearly Gaussian posteriors with many data.

## Code

The Laplace approximation of two posteriors, Bayesian logistic regression with one weight and with two, the moderated output against the exact predictive, and relevance vector classification on two moons with an SVM on the same data. The script runs in about a second; exact numbers can differ in the last digit across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, integrate
from scipy.special import expit
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV

# Laplace on a skewed posterior: Beta(4, 2), a coin after 3 heads and 1 tail under a uniform prior
mode = 3 / 4                                          # the maximum of 3 log p + log(1 - p)
A = 3 / mode ** 2 + 1 / (1 - mode) ** 2               # minus its second derivative at the mode
Z = mode ** 3 * (1 - mode) * np.sqrt(2 * np.pi / A)   # Laplace's estimate of the normalizer B(4, 2) = 1/20
print(round(A, 3), round(1 / np.sqrt(A), 4), round(Z, 5), round(Z / 0.05, 3))       # 21.333 0.2165 0.05724 1.145
print(round(stats.beta(4, 2).mean(), 4), round(stats.norm(mode, 1 / np.sqrt(A)).sf(1), 4))   # 0.6667 0.1241: true mean; Laplace mass above 1

# a bimodal posterior: t = theta^2 + noise, t = 2 observed with noise sd 0.5, prior N(0, 2^2)
logp = lambda th: -(2 - th ** 2) ** 2 / (2 * 0.25) - th ** 2 / 8
th_hat = np.sqrt(2 - 1 / 32)                          # the positive mode: 8 (2 - theta^2) = 1/4
H = 8 * (2 - th_hat ** 2) - 16 * th_hat ** 2 - 0.25   # second derivative of logp at the mode
Zp = integrate.quad(lambda v: np.exp(logp(v)), -6, 6)[0]
sd_exact = np.sqrt(integrate.quad(lambda v: v ** 2 * np.exp(logp(v)), -6, 6)[0] / Zp)
print(round(th_hat, 4), round(H, 3), round(1 / np.sqrt(-H), 4), round(sd_exact, 4))   # 1.4031 -31.5 0.1782 1.377: the Laplace sd against the exact posterior sd

# Bayesian logistic regression with one weight, prior N(0, 1): Newton's method to the mode, then the Hessian
x = np.array([-1.5, -0.5, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0]); t = np.array([0, 1, 0, 1, 1.0]); alpha = 1.0
w = 0.0
for k in range(1, 5):
    y = expit(w * x)
    w = w + (x @ (t - y) - alpha * w) / (x ** 2 @ (y * (1 - y)) + alpha)   # w - gradient / Hessian
    print(k, round(w, 5))                             # 1 0.59574 | 2 0.63021 | 3 0.63048 | 4 0.63048
y = expit(w * x)
A1 = x ** 2 @ (y * (1 - y)) + alpha                   # the precision of the Laplace approximation
mu_a, s2 = 3 * w, 9 / A1                              # the activation w x* at x* = 3: mean and variance
kappa = 1 / np.sqrt(1 + np.pi * s2 / 8)
gauss = integrate.quad(lambda a: expit(a) * stats.norm(mu_a, np.sqrt(s2)).pdf(a), -40, 40)[0]
post = lambda v: np.exp(np.sum(t * np.log(expit(v * x)) + (1 - t) * np.log(expit(-v * x))) - alpha * v * v / 2)
exact = integrate.quad(lambda v: expit(3 * v) * post(v), -20, 20)[0] / integrate.quad(post, -20, 20)[0]
print(round(A1, 4), round(mu_a, 4), round(s2, 4), round(kappa, 4))                 # 2.4902 1.8914 3.6141 0.6429
print(round(expit(mu_a), 4), round(expit(kappa * mu_a), 4), round(gauss, 4), round(exact, 4))   # 0.8689 0.7714 0.7693 0.7899

# the same in one dimension with a bias, phi(x) = (1, x): MAP, moderated and exact predictive probabilities
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
x = np.concatenate([rng.normal(-1, 1, 3), rng.normal(1, 1, 3)]).round(2); t = np.repeat([0, 1.0], 3)
Phi = np.stack([np.ones(6), x], axis=1)
w = np.zeros(2)
for _ in range(30):                                   # Newton's method in two dimensions (IRLS)
    y = expit(Phi @ w)
    w = w + np.linalg.solve(Phi.T @ (Phi * (y * (1 - y))[:, None]) + np.eye(2), Phi.T @ (t - y) - w)
y = expit(Phi @ w)
S = np.linalg.inv(Phi.T @ (Phi * (y * (1 - y))[:, None]) + np.eye(2))
print(x, w.round(4), S.round(4).tolist())            # [-1.65 -1.17  0.66  1.66 -0.64  0.99] [0.0099 0.694 ] [[0.4393, 0.0047], [0.0047, 0.3687]]
g = np.linspace(-8, 8, 321); U, V = np.meshgrid(g, g)
W = w[:, None] + np.linalg.cholesky(S) @ np.stack([U.ravel(), V.ravel()])   # a grid over the weights, in whitened coordinates
lp = (t[:, None] * np.log(expit(Phi @ W)) + (1 - t[:, None]) * np.log(expit(-(Phi @ W)))).sum(0) - (W ** 2).sum(0) / 2
p = np.exp(lp - lp.max()); p /= p.sum()               # the exact posterior on the grid
for xs in (0, 1, 3):

# 0 0.502 0.502 0.502 | 1 0.669 0.649 0.663 | 3 0.89 0.79 0.814: MAP, moderated, exact

# RVM classification on two moons: Laplace inside the evidence loop, Gaussian kernels of width 0.5
X, t = make_moons(200, noise=0.3, random_state=0)
Xt, tt = make_moons(2000, noise=0.3, random_state=1)
K = lambda P, Q: np.exp(-((P[:, None, :] - Q[None]) ** 2).sum(-1) / (2 * 0.5 ** 2))
Phi = np.hstack([np.ones((200, 1)), K(X, X)])

def laplace(P, t, a, w):
    """Newton's method to the mode of the log-posterior; returns the mode and the Laplace covariance."""
    for _ in range(50):
        y = expit(P @ w)
        step = np.linalg.solve(P.T @ (P * (y * (1 - y))[:, None]) + np.diag(a), P.T @ (t - y) - a * w)
        w = w + step
        if np.max(np.abs(step)) < 1e-8: break
    y = expit(P @ w)
    return w, np.linalg.inv(P.T @ (P * (y * (1 - y))[:, None]) + np.diag(a))

alpha, keep, w = np.ones(201), np.arange(201), np.zeros(201)
for it in range(1, 501):
    wk, S = laplace(Phi[:, keep], t, alpha[keep], w[keep])
    new = (1 - alpha[keep] * np.diag(S)) / wk ** 2    # the regression updates, with the Laplace mean and covariance
    change = np.max(np.abs(np.log(new / alpha[keep])))
    alpha[keep] = new; w[:] = 0; w[keep] = wk
    keep = keep[new < 1e9]
    if change < 1e-4: break
wk, S = laplace(Phi[:, keep], t, alpha[keep], w[keep])
Pt = np.hstack([np.ones((2000, 1)), K(Xt, X)])[:, keep]
m, v = Pt @ wk, np.einsum('ij,jk,ik->i', Pt, S, Pt)
nll = lambda q: round(-np.mean(tt * np.log(q) + (1 - tt) * np.log(1 - q)), 4)
print(it, len(keep), round(np.mean((m > 0) != tt), 4), nll(expit(m)), nll(expit(m / np.sqrt(1 + np.pi * v / 8))))
# 279 7 0.109 0.2771 0.2566: 7 relevance vectors, test error, test log-loss of the MAP and the moderated output
svc = GridSearchCV(SVC(kernel='rbf', gamma=1 / (2 * 0.5 ** 2)), {'C': [0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30, 100]}, cv=5).fit(X, t)
print(svc.best_params_, len(svc.best_estimator_.support_), round(1 - svc.score(Xt, tt), 4))   # {'C': 0.3} 94 0.0965
```

## Next

The Laplace approximation replaced an intractable posterior by a Gaussian fitted at one point. Many models are intractable for a different reason: they contain hidden variables, such as the component of a mixture or the low-dimensional coordinates of a data point, and the evidence is an integral over them. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca) returns to the EM algorithm as the general tool for such models, and uses the ARD idea of the [relevance vector machine](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine) to let Bayesian PCA choose its own number of components.
