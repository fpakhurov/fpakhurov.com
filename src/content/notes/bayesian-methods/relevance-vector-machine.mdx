[Bayesian linear regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression) put one prior precision $\alpha$ on all the weights and chose it by maximizing the evidence. One number for all weights shrinks them all by the same rule and keeps every feature in the model, relevant or not. This note gives each weight its own precision $\alpha_i$ and maximizes the evidence over all of them. The result is surprising at first sight: most of the precisions run off to infinity, their weights are pinned at zero, and the model ends up using a handful of features. The mechanism is called **automatic relevance determination** (ARD). Applied to kernel features, one per training point, it gives the **relevance vector machine** (RVM) of [Tipping (2001)](#references): a sparse kernel model like the support vector machine, but with predictive variances and without a regularization constant to cross-validate.

**Notation.** As in the previous note: $n$ training targets $t$, the design matrix $\Phi \in \mathbb R^{n \times m}$ with rows $\varphi(x_i)^\top$, weights $w$, noise precision $\beta$, posterior $\mathcal N(w \mid \mu, \Sigma)$. New here: the column $\phi_i \in \mathbb R^n$ of $\Phi$, the values of feature $i$ on all training inputs (not the feature vector $\varphi(x)$ of one input); one precision $\alpha_i$ per weight, collected in $A = \operatorname{diag}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m)$; and the $n \times n$ covariance of the targets under the prior, $C = \beta^{-1} I + \Phi A^{-1}\Phi^\top$. For kernel features, $K(x, x')$ is the kernel and the **relevance vectors** are the training inputs whose kernels stay in the model.

## Baseline: dense kernel regression and the support vector machine

Take the features to be kernels centred on the training inputs, $\varphi(x) = \big(1, K(x, x_1), \dots, K(x, x_n)\big)$, so $m = n + 1$. With a single precision this is Bayesian linear regression on $n + 1$ features, and its posterior mean is close to [kernel ridge regression](/notes/ml/kernels#kernel-ridge-regression). Every weight is nonzero, so a prediction touches all $n$ training points, and the predictive variance $\varphi_*^\top\Sigma\varphi_*$ costs $O(n^2)$ per new input.

The [support vector machine](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines) is sparse: its solution depends on the support vectors only, the points with nonzero dual variables ([support vectors](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines#support-vectors-what-the-dual-variables-tell-you)). Its regression form, the $\varepsilon$-insensitive SVR, ignores errors smaller than $\varepsilon$ and keeps as support vectors the points on or outside that tube. [Tipping (2001)](#references) lists what it pays for the sparsity:

- the number of support vectors typically grows linearly with the size of the training set;
- the output is a point estimate (in classification, a hard decision), with no error bar;
- the constants $C$ and $\varepsilon$ must be chosen by cross-validation;
- the kernel must be positive semidefinite, the condition of [which functions are kernels](/notes/ml/kernels#which-functions-are-kernels).

The RVM keeps the sparsity and removes the four costs, by changing only the prior on the weights.

## Automatic relevance determination: one precision per weight

Replace the prior $\mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$ by independent Gaussians with their own precisions:

$$
p(w \mid \alpha) = \prod_{i=1}^m \mathcal N\big(w_i \mid 0,\ \alpha_i^{-1}\big) = \mathcal N\big(w \mid 0,\ A^{-1}\big) .
$$

Nothing in the derivation of the previous note used the prior covariance being a multiple of $I$, so completing the square gives the posterior with $A$ in place of $\alpha I$, and the linear-Gaussian marginal gives the evidence:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\Sigma &= \big(A + \beta\Phi^\top\Phi\big)^{-1}, \quad \mu = \beta\,\Sigma\,\Phi^\top t, \\
p(t \mid \alpha, \beta) &= \mathcal N(t \mid 0,\ C), \quad C = \beta^{-1} I + \sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i^{-1}\, \phi_i \phi_i^\top
\end{aligned}
}
$$

The sum form of $C$ shows what a precision does. Each feature contributes $\alpha_i^{-1}\phi_i\phi_i^\top$ to the covariance of the targets: a small $\alpha_i$ (a broad prior) lets the feature explain a lot of variation along $\phi_i$, and $\alpha_i = \infty$ removes its term altogether. With $\alpha_i = \infty$ the prior on $w_i$ is a point mass at zero, so is the posterior, and the feature is out of the model. Choosing the $\alpha_i$ by maximizing the evidence therefore chooses which features to keep.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Give every weight its own prior precision and maximize the evidence over all of them. A feature that does not explain enough of the targets to pay for the extra spread it adds to $C$ is better off with $\alpha_i = \infty$: its weight is pinned at zero and it leaves the model. Sparsity is not imposed by a penalty; it is what the evidence prefers.</aside>

The idea was introduced for the inputs of neural networks, with one precision per group of weights leaving an input ([MacKay, 1994](#references); [Neal, 1996](#references)), hence the name: the data decide which inputs are relevant. A second view explains why it produces zeros. If each $\alpha_i$ has a gamma prior and is integrated out, the prior on $w_i$ becomes a Student-$t$ distribution, sharply peaked at zero with heavy tails; with flat hyperpriors it becomes $p(w_i) \propto 1/\lvert w_i\rvert$ ([Tipping, 2001](#references), Section 5.1). Like the Laplace prior behind the [lasso](/notes/ml/regularization#the-lasso), it puts much of its mass near zero while allowing a few large weights. The RVM does not integrate the $\alpha_i$ out, but maximizes over them, which keeps every step Gaussian.

## Type-II maximum likelihood: MacKay's fixed point

The logarithm of the evidence has the same form as in the previous note, with $\frac m2 \log\alpha$ replaced by $\frac12\sum_i \log\alpha_i$ and $\frac\alpha2\lVert w\rVert^2$ by $\frac12 w^\top A w$:

$$
\log p(t \mid \alpha, \beta) = \frac12\sum_{i=1}^m \log\alpha_i + \frac n2\log\beta - \frac\beta2\lVert t - \Phi\mu\rVert^2 - \frac12\mu^\top A\mu - \frac12\log\det\Sigma^{-1} - \frac n2\log 2\pi .
$$

Differentiate with respect to one $\alpha_i$. As before, the derivative through $\mu$ vanishes because $\mu$ maximizes the exponent, and $\partial_{\alpha_i}\log\det(A + \beta\Phi^\top\Phi) = \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma\, e_i e_i^\top) = \Sigma_{ii}$, with $e_i$ the $i$-th unit vector. So

$$
\frac{\partial \log p(t \mid \alpha, \beta)}{\partial \alpha_i} = \frac12\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_i} - \mu_i^2 - \Sigma_{ii}\Big) .
$$

Setting it to zero and multiplying by $2\alpha_i$ gives $1 - \alpha_i\Sigma_{ii} = \alpha_i \mu_i^2$. For $\beta$ the derivation of the previous note goes through with $\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma\,\beta\Phi^\top\Phi) = \operatorname{tr}\big(\Sigma(\Sigma^{-1} - A)\big) = \sum_i (1 - \alpha_i\Sigma_{ii})$. Together, the updates of [MacKay (1992)](#references) used by [Tipping (2001)](#references):

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\gamma_i &= 1 - \alpha_i\,\Sigma_{ii}, \\
\alpha_i^{\text{new}} &= \frac{\gamma_i}{\mu_i^2}, \\
\frac{1}{\beta^{\text{new}}} &= \frac{\lVert t - \Phi\mu\rVert^2}{n - \sum_i \gamma_i}
\end{aligned}
}
$$

$\gamma_i \in [0, 1]$ says how well the data determine weight $i$. When $\alpha_i$ is large, the prior dominates, $\Sigma_{ii} \approx 1/\alpha_i$ and $\gamma_i \approx 0$; when $\alpha_i$ is small, the data dominate and $\gamma_i \approx 1$. $\sum_i \gamma_i$ is the effective number of parameters of the previous note, now counted weight by weight.

The algorithm alternates:

1. compute $\Sigma$ and $\mu$ for the current $\alpha$ and $\beta$;
2. compute $\gamma_i$, then the new $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$;
3. remove every feature whose $\alpha_i$ exceeds a large threshold (here $10^9$): its weight is zero to machine precision, and removing it shrinks the matrices of step 1;
4. repeat until the precisions stop changing.

An alternative is the EM algorithm with $w$ as the latent variable, whose M-step is $\alpha_i = 1/(\mu_i^2 + \Sigma_{ii})$. EM never decreases the evidence; MacKay's update has no such guarantee, but Tipping observed it to converge much faster and never saw it take a downhill step ([Tipping, 2001](#references), Appendix A).

**Worked example.** Four points, two orthogonal features $\phi_1 = (1, 1, 2, 2)$ and $\phi_2 = (1, -1, -1, 1)$, targets $t = (1.05, 0.92, 2.03, 2.02)$, a known $\beta = 100$, and both precisions starting at 1. Orthogonal columns make $\Phi^\top\Phi = \operatorname{diag}(10, 4)$ diagonal, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Sigma &= \operatorname{diag}\Big(\frac{1}{1 + 1000},\ \frac{1}{1 + 400}\Big), \\
\mu &= \Big(\frac{1007}{1001},\ \frac{12}{401}\Big) = (1.006,\ 0.0299),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\beta\Phi^\top t = (1007, 12)$. Then $\gamma = (1 - 1/1001,\ 1 - 1/401) = (0.999,\ 0.9975)$ and the new precisions are $0.999/1.006^2 = 0.987$ and $0.9975/0.0299^2 = 1114$. The first feature is $t$ up to small noise; its precision settles at once. The second only matches the noise; its precision jumps by three orders of magnitude, and on the next updates it goes on growing: $4205$, then $12\,792$, by a factor that approaches $2.8$, until it passes $10^9$ after 14 updates and $\phi_2$ is pruned.

<ArdFlow />

1. For the current precisions, the posterior over the two weights.
2. $\gamma_i$: how much of each weight's prior variance the data have removed.
3. The new precisions $\gamma_i/\mu_i^2$; the loop repeats with them.
4. $\alpha_2$ grows without bound and $\phi_2$ leaves the model; $\alpha_1$ stays at $0.987$.

## Why most precisions go to infinity

The fixed point says where the precisions end up but not why so many of them end at infinity. The answer comes from looking at the evidence as a function of a single $\alpha_i$, with all the others fixed ([Faul and Tipping, 2002](#references)). Split the covariance into the part without feature $i$ and its own term, $C = C_{-i} + \alpha_i^{-1}\phi_i\phi_i^\top$, and define

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_i &= \phi_i^\top C_{-i}^{-1}\phi_i, \\
q_i &= \phi_i^\top C_{-i}^{-1} t .
\end{aligned}
$$

The determinant lemma and Woodbury's identity for a rank-one update (the $m = 1$ case of the identities in the [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#tools-matrix-derivatives-and-gaussian-identities)) give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det C &= \det C_{-i}\,\big(1 + s_i/\alpha_i\big), \\
t^\top C^{-1} t &= t^\top C_{-i}^{-1} t - \frac{q_i^2}{\alpha_i + s_i} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and substituting into $\log\mathcal N(t \mid 0, C) = -\frac12\big(n\log 2\pi + \log\det C + t^\top C^{-1}t\big)$ separates the dependence on $\alpha_i$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(t \mid \alpha, \beta) &= \log p(t \mid \alpha_{-i}, \beta) + \ell(\alpha_i), \\
\ell(\alpha_i) &= \frac12\Big(\log\frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_i + s_i} + \frac{q_i^2}{\alpha_i + s_i}\Big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term of $\ell$ is negative and grows to 0 as $\alpha_i \to \infty$: it is the Occam penalty for the spread the feature adds to $C$. The second is the gain in fit, largest for small $\alpha_i$. Differentiating,

$$
\ell'(\alpha_i) = \frac12\Big(\frac{s_i}{\alpha_i(\alpha_i + s_i)} - \frac{q_i^2}{(\alpha_i + s_i)^2}\Big) = \frac{s_i^2 - \alpha_i\,(q_i^2 - s_i)}{2\,\alpha_i\,(\alpha_i + s_i)^2} .
$$

If $q_i^2 \le s_i$, the numerator is positive for every $\alpha_i$, $\ell$ increases all the way, and the best value is infinity. If $q_i^2 \gt s_i$, the numerator changes sign once, at the unique maximum:

$$
\boxed{\; \alpha_i^{\star} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{s_i^2}{q_i^2 - s_i} & \text{if } q_i^2 \gt s_i , \\[2ex] \infty & \text{if } q_i^2 \le s_i . \end{cases} \;}
$$

$q_i$ measures how well feature $i$ lines up with the targets, judged by the model without it, and $s_i$ how much of $\phi_i$ the model without it could already produce. A feature stays only if its alignment, squared, beats its overlap. In a kernel model with one bump per training point, neighbouring bumps are nearly the same vector, so once a few of them are in the model the rest add little that is new: their $q_i^2$ falls below $s_i$, and they are pruned. In the worked example, $s_2 = \beta\lVert\phi_2\rVert^2 = 400$ and $q_2 = \beta\,\phi_2^\top t = 12$, so $q_2^2/s_2 = 0.36$ and $\alpha_2^\star = \infty$; for the first feature $s_1 = 1000$, $q_1 = 1007$, and $\alpha_1^\star = 1000^2/(1007^2 - 1000) = 0.987$, the value the iteration reached.

In the variables $u = \alpha_i/s_i$ and $r = q_i^2/s_i$ the function is $\ell = \frac12\big(\log\frac{u}{1+u} + \frac{r}{1+u}\big)$, one curve per $r$, with its maximum at $u = 1/(r - 1)$ when $r \gt 1$. At the maximum of the evidence every coordinate is at its own optimum, so the kept features satisfy $q_i^2 \gt s_i$ and $\alpha_i = s_i^2/(q_i^2 - s_i)$, and the pruned ones $q_i^2 \le s_i$; the Code section checks both conditions on the sinc example below. The analysis also gives a much faster algorithm: start from an empty model and repeatedly add, re-estimate or delete one feature, each time setting its $\alpha_i$ to $\alpha_i^\star$ in closed form, so that the matrices never grow beyond the features in the model ([Tipping and Faul, 2003](#references)).

## The RVM on kernel features, and the SVM

With kernel features $\varphi(x) = (1, K(x, x_1), \dots, K(x, x_n))$ the RVM predicts with the features that survive, the relevance vectors:

$$
p(t_* \mid x_*, t) = \mathcal N\big(t_* \mid \mu^\top\varphi_*,\ \beta^{-1} + \varphi_*^\top\Sigma\,\varphi_*\big) ,
$$

with $\mu$, $\Sigma$ and $\varphi_*$ restricted to the kept features. Since $K$ only defines a set of basis functions, it does not have to be a positive semidefinite kernel: $\Phi A^{-1}\Phi^\top$ is a valid covariance for any $\Phi$.

| | $\varepsilon$-insensitive SVR | RVM regression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sparse in | support vectors, on or outside the $\varepsilon$-tube | relevance vectors, with $q_i^2 \gt s_i$ |
| Number of them | typically grows linearly with $n$ | usually far fewer |
| Regularization | $C$ and $\varepsilon$ by cross-validation | $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$ by the evidence |
| Kernel | positive semidefinite | any basis functions |
| Output | a point estimate | a Gaussian predictive distribution |
| Training | a convex quadratic program | non-convex; $O(m^3)$ per iteration, $m = n + 1$ at the start |

Tipping's own comparisons on the sinc function $\sin(x)/x$ use 100 evenly spaced points on $[-10, 10]$ and a linear-spline kernel. Without noise, an SVR with $\varepsilon = 0.01$ used 36 support vectors and an RVM with its noise level fixed at $0.01$ used 9 relevance vectors, with largest errors $0.0100$ and $0.0070$. With uniform noise on $[-0.2, 0.2]$, the RVM used 6 relevance vectors and the SVR, with $C$ and $\varepsilon$ chosen by 5-fold cross-validation, 29; the RMS deviations from the true function were $0.0245$ and $0.0291$ ([Tipping, 2001](#references), Section 4.1).

**Worked example.** Our own run uses the same 100 inputs with Gaussian noise of standard deviation $0.1$, a bias and one Gaussian kernel $K(x, x') = \exp\big(-(x - x')^2/(2 \cdot 2^2)\big)$ per point: 101 features. Starting from all $\alpha_i = 1$, the fixed point with $\beta$ estimated ran 378 iterations and kept 6 kernels, centred at $-5.96$, $-3.13$, $-0.71$, $5.15$, $8.18$ and $10$; the bias was pruned. The estimated noise level is $1/\sqrt\beta = 0.090$, and the RMS distance of the predictive mean from $\sin(x)/x$ on a fine grid over $[-10, 10]$ is $0.040$. At the optimum, every kept feature has $q_i^2/s_i \gt 1$ and every pruned one $q_i^2/s_i \le 0.944$. On the same data, an SVR with the same kernel and $C$ and $\varepsilon$ chosen by 5-fold cross-validation from a grid of 16 pairs picked $\varepsilon = 0.01$, the smallest value offered, and kept 95 of the 100 points as support vectors, with RMS distance $0.050$. One dataset and one grid do not rank the two methods; the contrast in the number of vectors is the typical one.

The figure also shows the weak point of the RVM's error bars. The predictive standard deviation stays between $0.090$ and $0.099$ over the data and at $x = 15$, far outside them, it is $0.090$, the noise level itself. The kept kernels are localized, so far from all of them $\varphi_* \to 0$ and the weight uncertainty vanishes: the model is as confident where it has no data as where it has the most ([Rasmussen and Williams, 2006](#references), Section 6.6). The prior over functions that the RVM implies is a degenerate Gaussian process with only six basis functions; [Gaussian processes](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes) with a stationary kernel do not have this flaw.

## What changed

| | Bayesian linear regression | ARD and the RVM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prior | $\mathcal N(0, \alpha^{-1} I)$ | $\mathcal N(0, A^{-1})$, $A = \operatorname{diag}(\alpha_i)$ |
| Hyperparameters | two: $\alpha$, $\beta$ | $m + 1$: every $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$ |
| Update | $\alpha = \gamma/\lVert\mu\rVert^2$ | $\alpha_i = \gamma_i/\mu_i^2$, $\gamma_i = 1 - \alpha_i\Sigma_{ii}$ |
| Features kept | all | those with $q_i^2 \gt s_i$ |
| With kernel features | dense, like kernel ridge regression | sparse, like the SVM, with error bars |
| Sinc example | 101 features | 6 relevance vectors |

## Limitations

- **Overconfidence away from the data.** With localized kernels the predictive variance falls to the noise level far from the relevance vectors, the opposite of what it should do.
- **Non-convex optimization.** The evidence is not concave in the precisions; different starting values, pruning thresholds or stopping rules can end at different sets of relevance vectors.
- **Cost of the full algorithm.** The first iterations invert an $(n + 1) \times (n + 1)$ matrix, $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory, which Tipping notes becomes impractical for several thousand training points; the sequential algorithm of Tipping and Faul avoids most of it.
- **Kernel parameters.** The evidence sets the $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$ but not, in the basic algorithm, the width of the kernel, which is still chosen by cross-validation.
- **Point estimates of many hyperparameters.** With one hyperparameter per weight, maximizing the evidence instead of integrating over the precisions is a cruder approximation than in the previous note, and the sparsity is a property of that approximation.

## What you should remember

- ARD gives each weight its own prior precision; the posterior and evidence are those of Bayesian linear regression with $A = \operatorname{diag}(\alpha_i)$ in place of $\alpha I$.
- MacKay's fixed point: $\gamma_i = 1 - \alpha_i\Sigma_{ii}$, $\alpha_i \leftarrow \gamma_i/\mu_i^2$, $1/\beta \leftarrow \lVert t - \Phi\mu\rVert^2/(n - \sum_i\gamma_i)$; features with huge $\alpha_i$ are pruned.
- As a function of one precision the log evidence is $\frac12\big(\log\frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_i + s_i} + \frac{q_i^2}{\alpha_i + s_i}\big)$ plus a constant; its maximum is $s_i^2/(q_i^2 - s_i)$ when $q_i^2 \gt s_i$ and infinity otherwise. Redundant features fail the test.
- The RVM is this model on kernel features centred on the training points; the survivors are the relevance vectors.
- Compared with the SVM: far fewer vectors, a predictive variance, no $C$ or $\varepsilon$ to cross-validate, any basis functions; but non-convex, expensive at the start, and overconfident far from the data.

## Code

ARD by MacKay's fixed point, the toy example and its $q$ and $s$, the RVM on noisy sinc data with the sparsity condition checked at the optimum, and an SVR on the same data. The script runs in about two seconds; exact numbers, and in rare cases the set of relevance vectors, can differ across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.svm import SVR
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
np.set_printoptions(suppress=True)

def rvm(Phi, t, beta=None, iters=5000, prune=1e9, tol=1e-6):
    """Type-II maximum likelihood with one precision per weight (MacKay's fixed point).
    Weights whose precision passes `prune` are removed; beta=None also estimates the noise precision."""
    n, m = Phi.shape
    alpha, keep = np.ones(m), np.arange(m)
    b = 1 / np.var(t) if beta is None else beta
    for it in range(1, iters + 1):
        P = Phi[:, keep]
        S = np.linalg.inv(np.diag(alpha[keep]) + b * P.T @ P)     # posterior covariance of the kept weights
        mu = b * S @ P.T @ t                                       # posterior mean
        gamma = 1 - alpha[keep] * np.diag(S)                       # how well each weight is determined
        new = gamma / mu ** 2                                      # MacKay's update
        if beta is None:
            b = (n - gamma.sum()) / np.sum((t - P @ mu) ** 2)
        change = np.max(np.abs(np.log(new / alpha[keep])))
        alpha[keep] = new
        alpha[keep[new >= prune]] = np.inf                         # the weight is pinned at zero: prune it
        keep = keep[new < prune]
        if change < tol: break
    P = Phi[:, keep]
    S = np.linalg.inv(np.diag(alpha[keep]) + b * P.T @ P)
    return keep, b * S @ P.T @ t, S, alpha, b, it

# the toy: two orthogonal features, the noise precision beta = 100 known, alpha starts at (1, 1)
Phi = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1], [2, -1], [2, 1.0]])
t = np.array([1.05, 0.92, 2.03, 2.02])
alpha = np.ones(2)
for k in range(1, 15):
    S = np.linalg.inv(np.diag(alpha) + 100 * Phi.T @ Phi)
    mu = 100 * S @ Phi.T @ t
    gamma = 1 - alpha * np.diag(S)
    alpha = gamma / mu ** 2
    if k <= 2 or k == 14: print(k, mu.round(4), gamma.round(4), alpha.round(3).tolist())
# 1 [1.006  0.0299] [0.999  0.9975] [0.987, 1113.889]
# 2 [1.006  0.0079] [0.999  0.2642] [0.987, 4205.247]
# 14 [1.006 0.   ] [0.999 0.   ] [0.987, 1019386918.681]: alpha_2 passes 1e9 after 14 updates
s, q = 100 * (Phi ** 2).sum(axis=0), 100 * Phi.T @ t            # for orthogonal features s_i = beta |phi_i|^2, q_i = beta phi_i . t
print(s, q, (q ** 2 / s).round(2), round(s[0] ** 2 / (q[0] ** 2 - s[0]), 4), round(alpha[0], 4))
# [1000.  400.] [1007.   12.] [1014.05    0.36] 0.9871 0.9871: q^2 > s for phi_1 only, and alpha_1 = s^2/(q^2 - s)

# 100 noisy points of sinc(x) = sin(x)/x, a bias and one Gaussian kernel of width 2 per point: m = 101 features
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = np.linspace(-10, 10, 100)
sinc = lambda x: np.sinc(x / np.pi)
t = sinc(x) + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=100)
K = lambda a, b: np.exp(-(a[:, None] - b[None, :]) ** 2 / (2 * 2.0 ** 2))
feat = lambda z: np.hstack([np.ones((len(z), 1)), K(z, x)])
Phi = feat(x)
keep, mu, S, alpha, beta, it = rvm(Phi, t)
xg = np.linspace(-10, 10, 1000)
Pg = feat(xg)[:, keep]
print(it, len(keep), x[keep - 1].round(2), round(1 / np.sqrt(beta), 4))   # 378 6 [-5.96 -3.13 -0.71 5.15 8.18 10.  ] 0.0902
print(round(np.sqrt(np.mean((Pg @ mu - sinc(xg)) ** 2)), 4))              # 0.0403: RMS distance to the true sinc on a fine grid
sd = lambda P: np.sqrt(1 / beta + np.einsum('ij,jk,ik->i', P, S, P))
print(sd(Pg).min().round(4), sd(Pg).max().round(4), sd(feat(np.array([15.0]))[:, keep]).round(4))   # 0.0903 0.0993 [0.0903]: as confident at x = 15 as anywhere

# the sparsity condition at the optimum: q_i^2 > s_i exactly for the kept features, alpha_i = s_i^2/(q_i^2 - s_i)
C = np.eye(100) / beta + Phi[:, keep] @ np.diag(1 / alpha[keep]) @ Phi[:, keep].T
r, ok = [], True
for i in range(101):
    phi = Phi[:, i]
    Cm = C - np.outer(phi, phi) / alpha[i] if i in keep else C           # C without feature i
    s_i, q_i = phi @ np.linalg.solve(Cm, phi), phi @ np.linalg.solve(Cm, t)
    r.append(q_i ** 2 / s_i)
    if i in keep: ok &= bool(np.isclose(alpha[i], s_i ** 2 / (q_i ** 2 - s_i), rtol=1e-3))
r = np.array(r)
print(bool(np.all(r[keep] > 1)), bool(np.all(np.delete(r, keep) <= 1 + 1e-6)), ok, np.delete(r, keep).max().round(3))   # True True True 0.944

# a support vector regression with the same kernel, C and epsilon chosen by 5-fold cross-validation
svr = GridSearchCV(SVR(kernel='rbf', gamma=1 / (2 * 2.0 ** 2)), {'C': [0.1, 1, 10, 100], 'epsilon': [0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2]}, cv=5).fit(x[:, None], t)
best = svr.best_estimator_
print(svr.best_params_, len(best.support_), round(np.sqrt(np.mean((best.predict(xg[:, None]) - sinc(xg)) ** 2)), 4))
# {'C': 1, 'epsilon': 0.01} 95 0.0497: 95 of the 100 points are support vectors
```

## Next

The RVM's evidence was available in closed form because the likelihood was Gaussian. For classification it is not: the targets are 0 or 1, the likelihood is a product of sigmoids, and neither the posterior over the weights nor the evidence has a formula. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) replaces the posterior by a Gaussian centred at its mode, with the curvature there as its precision. This **Laplace approximation** gives Bayesian logistic regression, its moderated predictions, and the classification version of the RVM.
