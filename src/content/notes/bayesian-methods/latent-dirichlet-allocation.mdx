The [Dirichlet process mixture](/notes/bayesian-methods/dirichlet-processes) gave every data point exactly one cluster. A collection of documents does not fit that mould. An article about the transfer market of a football club uses the vocabulary of sports and of money in the same paragraph, and a thousand other articles use the same two vocabularies in other proportions. **Latent Dirichlet allocation** (LDA) of [Blei, Ng and Jordan (2003)](#references) models exactly this: a set of **topics**, each a distribution over words, shared by the whole collection, and for every document its own mixture of them. It is assembled from the blocks of this course, Dirichlet priors on categorical distributions and a latent label per observation, and it is fitted with the two tools of the previous section: a collapsed Gibbs sampler and mean-field variational inference.

**Notation.** A corpus of $D$ documents over a vocabulary of $V$ words; document $d$ has $N_d$ words $w_{d1}, \dots, w_{dN_d}$, each an index in $\{1, \dots, V\}$. There are $K$ topics. Topic $k$ is a distribution $\phi_k$ over the vocabulary, with probabilities $\phi_{kw}$; document $d$ has topic proportions $\theta_d$ on the simplex of $K$ probabilities; $z_{dn} \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ is the topic of the $n$-th word. $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the parameters of symmetric Dirichlet priors on $\theta_d$ and $\phi_k$. Counts: $n_{dk}$ words of document $d$ assigned to topic $k$, $n_{kw}$ occurrences of word $w$ assigned to topic $k$ in the whole corpus, $n_k = \sum_w n_{kw}$; a superscript $-i$ means "without token $i$". $\psi$ is the digamma function.

## Baseline: one topic per document

Treat each document as a [bag of words](/notes/nlp/bag-of-words-and-tf-idf), its word order ignored. The simplest probabilistic model of such bags is a **mixture of unigrams** ([Nigam et al., 2000](#references)): pick one topic $k$ for the whole document with probability $\pi_k$, then draw all its words from $\phi_k$, so that $p(w_d) = \sum_k \pi_k \prod_n \phi_{k w_{dn}}$. It is the finite mixture of the [Dirichlet process note](/notes/bayesian-methods/dirichlet-processes#baseline-a-bayesian-mixture-with-k-chosen-in-advance) with documents as the points.

It fails on documents that mix subjects. Take the two topics of the figure below, sports and economics, and the three-word document "team goal market". The best single topic, sports, explains it with probability $0.34 \cdot 0.30 \cdot 0.02 = 0.00204$: the word "market" must come from the sports topic, which gives it 2%. A document-specific mixture with proportions $0.7$ and $0.3$ gives each word a probability of $0.7\,\phi_{1w} + 0.3\,\phi_{2w}$, that is $0.244$, $0.216$ and $0.116$, and the document $0.0061$, three times more. (This toy comparison fixes the proportions; the model below puts a prior on them.) The **probabilistic latent semantic analysis** of [Hofmann (1999)](#references) gives each training document its own proportions, but as parameters: their number grows with the corpus, they overfit, and a new document has no proportions at all. LDA makes the proportions random, with a Dirichlet prior, which fixes both problems.

## The generative story

LDA describes how a corpus could have been produced:

1. For each topic $k = 1, \dots, K$: draw the word distribution $\phi_k \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\beta, \dots, \beta)$.
2. For each document $d$: draw its topic proportions $\theta_d \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha, \dots, \alpha)$.
3. For each position $n$ of document $d$: draw a topic $z_{dn} \sim \mathrm{Categorical}(\theta_d)$,
4. and then the word $w_{dn} \sim \mathrm{Categorical}(\phi_{z_{dn}})$.

<LdaGenerative />

The joint distribution of everything is the product of these steps:

$$
p(w, z, \theta, \phi \mid \alpha, \beta) = \prod_{k=1}^K \mathrm{Dir}(\phi_k \mid \beta) \prod_{d=1}^D \Big( \mathrm{Dir}(\theta_d \mid \alpha) \prod_{n=1}^{N_d} \theta_{d z_{dn}}\, \phi_{z_{dn} w_{dn}} \Big) .
$$

Summing out the topic of one word gives the probability of the word in its document,

$$
p(w_{dn} = w \mid \theta_d, \phi) = \sum_{k=1}^K \theta_{dk}\, \phi_{kw} ,
$$

so the $D \times V$ matrix of word probabilities per document is the product of a $D \times K$ matrix of proportions and a $K \times V$ matrix of topics: LDA is a probabilistic, non-negative low-rank factorization of the document–word matrix. **Worked example.** In the figure, $p(\text{goal} \mid d) = 0.7 \cdot 0.30 + 0.3 \cdot 0.02 = 0.216$.

The Dirichlet process note's mixture has one level: points share clusters. LDA has two: words share topics within a document through $\theta_d$, and documents share topics through the common $\phi$. The proportions are a "small" mixture per document; the topics are what the corpus has in common.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Each document is a mixture of topics, and each topic is a distribution over words; the topics are shared by all documents, the proportions are private to each. With Dirichlet priors on both, conjugacy lets the proportions and the topics be integrated out exactly, and inference reduces to assigning each word token to a topic, by sampling or by a variational approximation.</aside>

## Inference: the posterior over topic assignments

The quantity of interest is the posterior $p(z, \theta, \phi \mid w)$: which topic each word belongs to, what the topics are, and what each document is about. Its normalizer $p(w) = \sum_z \int\!\!\int p(w, z, \theta, \phi)\, d\theta\, d\phi$ sums over $K^{\sum_d N_d}$ assignments and has no closed form. The two standard approximations are the collapsed Gibbs sampler of [Griffiths and Steyvers (2004)](#references) and the variational method of the original paper.

### Collapsed Gibbs sampling

Both $\theta$ and $\phi$ can be integrated out, because each appears only in a Dirichlet prior and in categorical terms: this is the Dirichlet–categorical conjugacy of the [conjugate priors note](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#dirichlet-categorical). Write $B(a) = \prod_j \Gamma(a_j) / \Gamma(\sum_j a_j)$ for the normalizer of a Dirichlet, so that $\int \prod_j \pi_j^{a_j - 1}\, d\pi = B(a)$. For document $d$, collecting the factors that contain $\theta_d$,

$$
\int \mathrm{Dir}(\theta_d \mid \alpha) \prod_{n} \theta_{d z_{dn}}\, d\theta_d = \frac{1}{B(\alpha)} \int \prod_k \theta_{dk}^{\,n_{dk} + \alpha - 1}\, d\theta_d = \frac{B(n_{d\cdot} + \alpha)}{B(\alpha)} ,
$$

where $n_{d\cdot} = (n_{d1}, \dots, n_{dK})$ and $B(\alpha)$ is the normalizer at $(\alpha, \dots, \alpha)$. The same computation for each topic gives

$$
p(w, z \mid \alpha, \beta) = \prod_{k=1}^K \frac{B(n_{k\cdot} + \beta)}{B(\beta)} \prod_{d=1}^D \frac{B(n_{d\cdot} + \alpha)}{B(\alpha)} ,
$$

a function of the counts alone. The Gibbs sampler resamples one token's topic at a time from $p(z_i = k \mid z_{-i}, w) \propto p(w, z_i = k, z_{-i})$. Let token $i$ be word $v$ in document $d$. Changing $z_i$ changes only three counts, $n_{dk}$, $n_{kv}$ and $n_k$, each by one. With the token removed, setting $z_i = k$ raises them from $n^{-i}$ to $n^{-i} + 1$, and the Gamma functions that change are

$$
\frac{\Gamma(n_{dk}^{-i} + \alpha + 1)}{\Gamma(n_{dk}^{-i} + \alpha)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(n_{kv}^{-i} + \beta + 1)}{\Gamma(n_{kv}^{-i} + \beta)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(n_k^{-i} + V\beta)}{\Gamma(n_k^{-i} + V\beta + 1)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(N_d - 1 + K\alpha)}{\Gamma(N_d + K\alpha)} .
$$

By $\Gamma(x + 1) = x\, \Gamma(x)$ each ratio is its argument or its inverse, and the last one does not depend on $k$. So

$$
\boxed{\ p(z_i = k \mid z_{-i}, w) \;\propto\; \big(n_{dk}^{-i} + \alpha\big)\, \frac{n_{kv}^{-i} + \beta}{n_k^{-i} + V\beta} \ }
$$

([Griffiths and Steyvers, 2004](#references), Eq. 5). The two factors are two posterior predictive probabilities: how much document $d$ already uses topic $k$, and how often topic $k$ already produces word $v$, each smoothed by its prior. A sweep visits every token, removes it from the counts, draws a new topic and adds it back: $O(K)$ work per token, with nothing but integer counts in memory. After the chain has mixed, a sample of $z$ gives estimates of the integrated-out quantities as posterior means given that sample,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat\phi_{kw} &= \frac{n_{kw} + \beta}{n_k + V\beta}, \\
\hat\theta_{dk} &= \frac{n_{dk} + \alpha}{N_d + K\alpha} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The sampler is an instance of the collapsed Gibbs sampling of the [Gibbs sampling note](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling), and the twin of the restaurant sampler of the previous note: there a point was reseated in proportion to the cluster's size times its predictive density, here a token in proportion to the document's use of a topic times the topic's predictive probability of the word.

**Worked example.** A token of the word "goal" in a document whose other words are 4 in topic 1 and 2 in topic 2. Over the corpus, topic 1 has 500 tokens, 30 of them "goal"; topic 2 has 400 tokens, 2 of them "goal". With $V = 1000$, $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\beta = 0.01$, the two unnormalized weights are $4.1 \cdot 30.01 / 510 = 0.2413$ and $2.1 \cdot 2.01 / 410 = 0.0103$, and the token goes to topic 1 with probability $0.959$.

Label switching is harmless here: topics are interchangeable, so a single chain gives consistent labels while it stays in one mode, but averaging $\hat\phi$ over several chains is not meaningful without matching the topics first.

### The variational alternative

The original paper took the other route of this course, [mean-field variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference#mean-field-and-the-general-update). With a Dirichlet prior on the topics, as in its Section 5.4, the factorized family is

$$
q(\theta, z, \phi) = \prod_{k} \mathrm{Dir}(\phi_k \mid \lambda_k) \prod_d \Big( \mathrm{Dir}(\theta_d \mid \gamma_d) \prod_n \mathrm{Categorical}(z_{dn} \mid r_{dn}) \Big) .
$$

Each factor's update is the exponentiated expected log-joint under the others. The expectations needed are those of logarithms of Dirichlet variables, $\mathbb E_q[\log \theta_{dk}] = \psi(\gamma_{dk}) - \psi(\sum_j \gamma_{dj})$ and the same for $\phi$, a property of the exponential family. The updates are

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_{dnk} &\propto \exp\big(\mathbb E_q[\log \theta_{dk}] + \mathbb E_q[\log \phi_{k w_{dn}}]\big), \\
\gamma_{dk} &= \alpha + \sum_{n} r_{dnk}, \\
\lambda_{kw} &= \beta + \sum_{d, n} r_{dnk}\, [w_{dn} = w] .
\end{aligned}
$$

They are the Gibbs update with counts replaced by expected counts and with the probabilities replaced by exponentials of expected logarithms. Coordinate ascent alternates a **local step**, which iterates $r_d$ and $\gamma_d$ for every document with the topics fixed, and a **global step**, which recomputes $\lambda$ from all the expected counts. Blei et al. write the topics as $\beta_k$, their prior parameter as $\eta$ and the assignment probabilities as $\phi_{dn}$, and the [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference) follows them; $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ are the same in both. The updates are deterministic, vectorize over the document–word count matrix, and come with an objective, the ELBO, to monitor convergence. In exchange they are biased: mean field underestimates the posterior variance and, like the sampler, can get stuck in local optima. With hyperparameters tuned for each method, the two give similar topics and similar held-out perplexity ([Asuncion et al., 2009](#references)).

## The hyperparameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ \{#the-hyperparameters-alpha-and-beta\}

The two Dirichlet parameters set what kind of documents and topics the model expects. With $\alpha \lt 1$, the prior on $\theta_d$ puts its mass near the corners and edges of the simplex: each document uses few topics. With $\alpha = 1$ it is uniform, and with $\alpha \gt 1$ it concentrates near the centre: every document mixes all topics evenly. $\beta$ plays the same role for topics: a small $\beta$ gives topics concentrated on few words.

<DirichletAlpha />

In the posterior, $\alpha$ is a pseudo-count added to every topic of every document. **Worked example.** A document whose ten tokens are assigned $8, 2, 0$ to three topics has the estimate $\hat\theta = (8.1, 2.1, 0.1)/10.3 = (0.786, 0.204, 0.010)$ with $\alpha = 0.1$ and $(9, 3, 1)/13 = (0.692, 0.231, 0.077)$ with $\alpha = 1$: the larger prior spreads the document over a topic it never used. For short documents, such as tweets or search queries, $\alpha$ dominates the counts.

Common defaults are $\alpha = 50/K$ and $\beta = 0.1$ ([Griffiths and Steyvers, 2004](#references)), or $\alpha = \beta = 1/K$ (scikit-learn). The hyperparameters can also be learned, by maximizing the evidence with a fixed-point iteration or by sampling them. [Wallach, Mimno and McCallum (2009)](#references) found that an asymmetric, learned prior on $\theta$, letting some topics be common in every document, improves held-out likelihood and topic quality markedly, while an asymmetric prior on the topics brings little. The experiment below shows how much $\alpha$ matters when it is wrong by a factor of ten.

## Evaluation: perplexity and coherence

A topic model is a model of text, so it can be scored like a [language model](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models#perplexity-from-probability-to-a-number), by the **perplexity** of held-out documents:

$$
\mathrm{PPL} = \exp\Big(-\frac{\sum_{d} \log p(w_d)}{\sum_d N_d}\Big) ,
$$

the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood per word. A perplexity of 17 means the model is as uncertain about each word as a uniform choice among 17 words. Two complications are specific to topic models. First, $p(w_d) = \int p(\theta_d) \prod_n \sum_k \theta_{dk} \phi_{k w_{dn}}\, d\theta_d$ is itself intractable; it is estimated by sampling or, as in the experiment below, by **document completion**: estimate $\theta_d$ from the first half of a test document and score the second half ([Wallach et al., 2009](#references)). Different estimators give different numbers, so perplexities are comparable only under one protocol. Second, likelihood is not what people want from topics. In a study with human judges, models with better held-out likelihood often had less interpretable topics ([Chang et al., 2009](#references)).

**Coherence** measures interpretability directly from co-occurrence: the top words of a good topic appear in the same documents. The UMass coherence of a topic with top words $v_1, \dots, v_M$ ([Mimno et al., 2011](#references)) is

$$
C = \sum_{m=2}^{M} \sum_{l=1}^{m-1} \log \frac{D(v_m, v_l) + 1}{D(v_l)} ,
$$

where $D(v)$ counts the documents containing $v$ and $D(v, u)$ those containing both; the $+1$ avoids the logarithm of zero. Each term is a smoothed log conditional probability that a document with $v_l$ also contains $v_m$. **Worked example.** Three top words in 40, 30 and 20 documents, pairwise co-occurring in 25 (words 1 and 2), 15 (1 and 3) and 12 (2 and 3) documents: $C = \log\frac{26}{40} + \log\frac{16}{40} + \log\frac{13}{30} = -0.431 - 0.916 - 0.836 = -2.18$. Values closer to zero mean a more coherent topic. Variants based on pointwise mutual information over a large reference corpus, such as Wikipedia, agree better with human judgements ([Newman et al., 2010](#references)).

## An experiment: recovering known topics

The Code section uses the "bars" corpus of [Griffiths and Steyvers (2004)](#references): a vocabulary of 25 words laid out on a $5 \times 5$ grid, and 10 true topics, the five rows and the five columns, each uniform over its five words. 200 training documents of 50 words each are generated with $\alpha = 0.3$, so that most documents use a few bars, and 50 more documents are held out. Because the true topics are known, recovery can be measured directly: each learned topic is paired with a true bar by the Hungarian algorithm, and the distance is the total variation between their distributions.

- **Collapsed Gibbs.** 200 sweeps with $\alpha = 0.3$, $\beta = 0.1$, from random assignments. The log joint $\log p(w, z)$ climbs from $-54{,}604$ after the first sweep to $-39{,}588$ after 10, $-33{,}406$ after 50 and $-33{,}376$ after 200. All ten bars are recovered, the worst within a total variation of $0.070$. The held-out perplexity by document completion is $17.11$, against $17.09$ with the true topics and $25.10$ for a unigram model, essentially the uniform distribution over 25 words. The UMass coherence of the top five words averages $-3.52$, against $-3.59$ for the true bars and $-4.51$ for random topics. Coherence measures co-occurrence in this corpus, not truth: a learned topic can score slightly above the true one.
- **Mean-field variational inference**, implemented by hand with 100 rounds of local and global steps, recovers the bars within $0.054$ with held-out perplexity $17.16$; scikit-learn's `LatentDirichletAllocation` in batch mode, the same algorithm, gives $0.051$ and $17.12$.
- **The concentration $\alpha$.** Variational inference from five random starts for each value, with recovery counted as a total variation below $0.2$:

| $\alpha$ | bars recovered, of 10 | held-out perplexity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.03 | 0, 0, 0, 0, 0 | 18.5 – 19.5 |
| 0.3 (true) | 8, 8, 10, 10, 10 | 17.1 – 17.7 |
| 3 | 6, 2, 10, 10, 9 | 19.6 – 20.5 |

With $\alpha$ ten times too small, every document is forced toward a single topic, and the topics become typical combinations of bars rather than bars. With $\alpha$ ten times too large, the documents are spread over all topics, perplexity suffers, and recovery depends on the start. Even with the true $\alpha$, two of five starts end in a local optimum that misses two bars.
- **The number of topics.** With $K = 5$, $10$ and $20$, the average held-out perplexity over five starts is $21.33$, $17.34$ and $17.51$, and the average coherence $-3.51$, $-3.50$ and $-3.64$. Too few topics cost perplexity; too many cost little perplexity and split bars into less coherent pieces.

## What changed

| | Mixture of unigrams | Latent Dirichlet allocation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topics per document | one | a mixture, $\theta_d \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$ |
| Word probability in a document | $\phi_{z_d w}$ | $\sum_k \theta_{dk} \phi_{kw}$ |
| Latent variable | one label per document | one label per word token |
| New documents | a posterior over one topic | a posterior over proportions |
| Inference | EM or Gibbs over documents | collapsed Gibbs over tokens, or mean-field VI |
| Number of parameters | fixed, $K V$ | fixed, $K V$; the $\theta_d$ are latent, not parameters as in pLSA |

## Limitations

- **Bag of words.** Word order, syntax and negation are ignored; "not good" and "good" are the same evidence.
- **$K$ must be chosen.** Perplexity and coherence often disagree. The hierarchical Dirichlet process makes the number of topics a posterior quantity ([Teh et al., 2006](#references)), at the price of a more complex sampler.
- **Local optima and instability.** Different starts give different topics, some of them merged or split, as the experiment shows; reported topics should be checked across runs.
- **Evaluation is unsettled.** Held-out likelihood needs an estimator and does not track human judgements; coherence depends on the reference corpus. Neither says whether a topic is useful for the task at hand.
- **Frequent words dominate.** Without a stop-word list or a learned asymmetric prior, every topic's top words include "the" and "of".
- **Scale.** Collapsed Gibbs visits every token in every sweep, and batch variational inference updates the topics once per pass over the corpus. For millions of documents both are slow.

## What you should remember

- LDA: topics $\phi_k \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\beta)$, proportions $\theta_d \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$, then for each word a topic $z \sim \theta_d$ and a word $w \sim \phi_z$; $p(w \mid d) = \sum_k \theta_{dk} \phi_{kw}$.
- Dirichlet–categorical conjugacy integrates out $\theta$ and $\phi$: $p(w, z) = \prod_k B(n_{k\cdot} + \beta)/B(\beta) \cdot \prod_d B(n_{d\cdot} + \alpha)/B(\alpha)$.
- Collapsed Gibbs: $p(z_i = k \mid z_{-i}, w) \propto (n_{dk}^{-i} + \alpha)(n_{kv}^{-i} + \beta)/(n_k^{-i} + V\beta)$; estimate $\phi$ and $\theta$ by smoothed counts.
- Mean-field VI replaces counts by expected counts and probabilities by $\exp \mathbb E[\log \cdot]$, with digamma functions; it alternates a local step per document and a global step for the topics.
- $\alpha \lt 1$ means few topics per document, $\beta$ small means few words per topic; a wrong $\alpha$ can hide the true topics.
- Evaluate by held-out perplexity under a stated protocol and by coherence; they measure different things.

## Code

A collapsed Gibbs sampler and mean-field variational inference for LDA on the bars corpus, with topic recovery, held-out perplexity, UMass coherence, and the effect of $\alpha$ and $K$. It runs in about 35 seconds; exact numbers can vary across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import digamma, gammaln
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment
from sklearn.decomposition import LatentDirichletAllocation

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# the collapsed Gibbs update for one token: two topics, alpha = 0.1, beta = 0.01, V = 1000
p = np.array([(4 + 0.1) * (30 + 0.01) / (500 + 1000 * 0.01), (2 + 0.1) * (2 + 0.01) / (400 + 1000 * 0.01)])
print(p.round(4), (p / p.sum()).round(3))                             # [0.2413 0.0103] [0.959 0.041]

# the "bars" corpus: 25 words on a 5 x 5 grid, 10 topics (5 rows, 5 columns), each uniform over its 5 words
V, K = 25, 10
grid = np.arange(V).reshape(5, 5)
phi_true = np.zeros((K, V))
for r in range(5):
    phi_true[r, grid[r]] = 0.2                           # a horizontal bar
    phi_true[5 + r, grid[:, r]] = 0.2                    # a vertical bar
D, N, alpha, beta = 200, 50, 0.3, 0.1
theta_true = rng.dirichlet(np.full(K, alpha), size=D)
docs = [rng.choice(V, size=N, p=theta_true[d] @ phi_true) for d in range(D)]   # w ~ sum_k theta_dk phi_kw
test = [rng.choice(V, size=N, p=t @ phi_true) for t in rng.dirichlet(np.full(K, alpha), size=50)]
counts = np.stack([np.bincount(w, minlength=V) for w in docs])               # D x V document-word counts

def log_joint(ndk, nkw):                                 # log p(w, z) with theta and phi integrated out
    topics = (gammaln(nkw + beta).sum(axis=1) - gammaln(nkw.sum(axis=1) + V * beta)).sum() + K * (gammaln(V * beta) - V * gammaln(beta))
    docs_ = (gammaln(ndk + alpha).sum(axis=1) - gammaln(ndk.sum(axis=1) + K * alpha)).sum() + len(ndk) * (gammaln(K * alpha) - K * gammaln(alpha))
    return topics + docs_

# collapsed Gibbs sampling (Griffiths and Steyvers, 2004)
def lda_gibbs(docs, K, alpha, beta, sweeps, rng):
    z = [rng.integers(K, size=len(w)) for w in docs]     # random initial topics
    ndk, nkw, nk = np.zeros((len(docs), K)), np.zeros((K, V)), np.zeros(K)
    for d, w in enumerate(docs):
        np.add.at(ndk[d], z[d], 1); np.add.at(nkw, (z[d], w), 1); np.add.at(nk, z[d], 1)
    trace = []
    for sweep in range(sweeps):
        for d, w in enumerate(docs):
            u = rng.random(len(w))
            for i, (v, k) in enumerate(zip(w, z[d])):
                ndk[d, k] -= 1; nkw[k, v] -= 1; nk[k] -= 1                # take token i out
                p = (ndk[d] + alpha) * (nkw[:, v] + beta) / (nk + V * beta)
                c = np.cumsum(p)
                k = int(np.searchsorted(c, u[i] * c[-1]))                 # draw its new topic
                z[d][i] = k; ndk[d, k] += 1; nkw[k, v] += 1; nk[k] += 1
        trace.append(log_joint(ndk, nkw))
    return ndk, nkw, np.array(trace)

def match(phi):                                          # pair learned topics with true bars; total variation distances
    cost = 0.5 * np.abs(phi[:, None, :] - phi_true[None]).sum(axis=2)
    r, c = linear_sum_assignment(cost)
    return cost[r, c]

def heldout_perplexity(docs, phi, alpha, iters=100):     # document completion: theta from the first half, score the second
    ll, n = 0.0, 0
    for w in docs:
        a, b = w[: len(w) // 2], w[len(w) // 2:]
        th = np.full(len(phi), 1 / len(phi))
        for _ in range(iters):
            r = th[:, None] * phi[:, a]; r /= r.sum(axis=0)
            th = (r.sum(axis=1) + alpha) / (len(a) + len(phi) * alpha)
        ll += np.log(th @ phi[:, b]).sum(); n += len(b)
    return np.exp(-ll / n)

def umass(phi, top=5):                                   # UMass coherence of the top words, averaged over topics
    df = (counts > 0).astype(float)
    co = df.T @ df                                       # co[v, u]: documents containing both v and u
    score = []
    for k in range(len(phi)):
        t = np.argsort(-phi[k])[:top]
        score.append(sum(np.log((co[t[m], t[l]] + 1) / co[t[l], t[l]]) for m in range(1, top) for l in range(m)))
    return np.mean(score)

ndk, nkw, trace = lda_gibbs(docs, K, alpha, beta, 200, rng)
print(trace[[0, 9, 49, 199]].round(0))                  # [-54604. -39588. -33406. -33376.]: log p(w, z) climbs, then levels off
phi_gibbs = (nkw + beta) / (nkw.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) + V * beta)
print(match(phi_gibbs).max().round(3))                   # 0.07: every bar recovered within 7% total variation
uni = (counts.sum(axis=0) + 1.0) / (counts.sum() + V)
print(round(heldout_perplexity(test, phi_gibbs, alpha), 2), round(heldout_perplexity(test, phi_true, alpha), 2),
      round(np.exp(-np.mean([np.log(uni[w]).mean() for w in test])), 2))      # Gibbs topics, true topics, unigram
# 17.11 17.09 25.1
rand = np.array([umass(rng.dirichlet(np.ones(V), size=K)) for _ in range(20)])
print(round(umass(phi_gibbs), 2), round(umass(phi_true), 2), round(rand.mean(), 2))   # -3.52 -3.59 -4.51: learned, true, random

# mean-field variational inference (Blei, Ng and Jordan, 2003, with a Dirichlet prior on the topics)
def lda_cavi(counts, K, alpha, beta, iters, rng, inner=20):
    lam = beta + rng.gamma(100.0, 0.01, size=(K, V))     # q(phi_k) = Dir(lam_k)
    gam = np.ones((len(counts), K))                      # q(theta_d) = Dir(gam_d)
    for _ in range(iters):
        Elog_phi = digamma(lam) - digamma(lam.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))
        for _ in range(inner):                           # local step: every document, topics fixed
            Elog_theta = digamma(gam) - digamma(gam.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))
            r = np.exp(Elog_theta[:, None, :] + Elog_phi.T[None])           # D x V x K, unnormalized q(z = k) per word type
            r /= r.sum(axis=2, keepdims=True)
            gam = alpha + (counts[:, :, None] * r).sum(axis=1)
        lam = beta + (counts[:, :, None] * r).sum(axis=0).T                 # global step: the topics
    return lam / lam.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True), gam

phi_vi, gam = lda_cavi(counts, K, alpha, beta, 100, rng)
print(match(phi_vi).max().round(3), round(heldout_perplexity(test, phi_vi, alpha), 2))     # 0.054 17.16
skl = LatentDirichletAllocation(n_components=K, doc_topic_prior=alpha, topic_word_prior=beta, learning_method='batch', max_iter=100, random_state=0).fit(counts)
phi_skl = skl.components_ / skl.components_.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
print(match(phi_skl).max().round(3), round(heldout_perplexity(test, phi_skl, alpha), 2))   # 0.051 17.12

# the hyperparameters and the number of topics: variational inference from five random starts each
for a in (0.03, 0.3, 3.0):
    fits = [lda_cavi(counts, K, a, beta, 100, np.random.default_rng(s))[0] for s in range(5)]
    print('alpha', a, [int((match(f) < 0.2).sum()) for f in fits], [float(round(heldout_perplexity(test, f, a), 1)) for f in fits])
# alpha 0.03 [0, 0, 0, 0, 0] [18.5, 19.0, 18.9, 19.5, 19.3]
# alpha 0.3 [8, 8, 10, 10, 10] [17.7, 17.7, 17.1, 17.1, 17.1]
# alpha 3.0 [6, 2, 10, 10, 9] [20.2, 20.5, 19.6, 19.6, 19.8]
for k in (5, 10, 20):
    fits = [lda_cavi(counts, k, alpha, beta, 100, np.random.default_rng(s))[0] for s in range(5)]
    print('K', k, round(np.mean([heldout_perplexity(test, f, alpha) for f in fits]), 2), round(np.mean([umass(f) for f in fits]), 2))
# K 5 21.33 -3.51 | K 10 17.34 -3.5 | K 20 17.51 -3.64
```

## Next

Both inference methods of this note touch every word of the corpus before the topics change once: the sampler in every sweep, coordinate ascent in every global step. With a few hundred documents that takes seconds; with millions of news articles, days. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference) keeps the variational family and the updates of this note and changes only the order of work: it updates the topics after each small random batch of documents, as a step along a noisy natural gradient, and so fits topic models to corpora that do not fit in memory.
