[Black-box variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference) can fit a variational posterior to any model whose log density can be differentiated, and the VAE used it for latent variables with a network as the likelihood. This last note of the course applies the same machinery to the weights of the network itself. A network trained by gradient descent returns one weight vector, a point estimate in the sense of the [first note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-approach), and its predictions carry no information about how far an input is from what it was trained on. We ask what a posterior over millions of weights would add, and look at four approximations that are used in practice: Bayes by Backprop, Monte Carlo dropout, deep ensembles and the Laplace approximation. One small experiment compares them on a regression problem with a gap in the data.

**Notation.** $f(x; w)$ is a network with weights $w \in \mathbb R^P$, trained on $\mathcal D = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$. For regression $p(y \mid x, w) = \mathcal N(y \mid f(x; w), \sigma^2)$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$; for classification $p(y \mid x, w)$ is the softmax of the logits. $p(w)$ is the prior, $p(w \mid \mathcal D)$ the posterior, $q(w)$ an approximation of it, and $w_1, \dots, w_T$ are $T$ samples from $q$. The dropout probability is $p$, the probability that a unit is set to zero.

## Baseline: one network, one answer

Take 40 points on $[-4, -1]$ and 40 on $[1, 4]$, with $y = \sin x$ plus noise of standard deviation $0.1$, and a network $1 \to 128 \to 128 \to 1$ with ReLU units trained by Adam on the squared error. Its only notion of uncertainty is the standard deviation of its residuals, $0.077$, and it attaches that number to every input. In the gap, at $x = 0$, it predicts $0.53$ where $\sin 0 = 0$, seven of its standard deviations away. Beyond the data, at $x = 6$, it predicts $-2.15$ where $\sin 6 = -0.28$, and at $x = -7$ it predicts $3.02$ where $\sin(-7) = -0.66$: a ReLU network continues linearly outside its training range, and nothing in its output says that it is guessing. The rest of the note asks how to make the uncertainty grow where the data end.

## Why put uncertainty in the weights

Many weight vectors fit 80 points equally well; they agree where there are data and disagree in the gap. The Bayesian answer keeps all of them, weighted by the posterior, and averages their predictions:

$$
p(y \mid x, \mathcal D) = \int p(y \mid x, w)\, p(w \mid \mathcal D)\, dw \approx \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T p(y \mid x, w_t), \qquad w_t \sim q(w) .
$$

For regression with a Gaussian likelihood, the law of total variance splits the predictive variance into two parts:

$$
\operatorname{Var}[y \mid x, \mathcal D] = \underbrace{E_{w}\big[\sigma^2\big]}_{\text{noise in the data}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}_{w}\big[f(x; w)\big]}_{\text{uncertainty about } w},
$$

with both moments under the posterior. The first term, **aleatoric** uncertainty, does not shrink with more data. The second, **epistemic** uncertainty, is the disagreement between plausible networks: it should be small near the training data, large where there are none, and it should shrink as data accumulate. [Bayesian linear regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression) and [Gaussian processes](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes) compute this term exactly. For a network the posterior over $P$ weights, with $P$ in the millions, has no closed form and many modes, and even Hamiltonian Monte Carlo ([HMC](/notes/generative-models/mcmc-samplers#hamiltonian-monte-carlo)), the reference method since [Neal (1996)](#references), is affordable only for small networks.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> A prediction should average over all the networks that fit the data, not use one of them. The spread of their predictions is the uncertainty that a single network cannot express: small where they are constrained by data, large where they are not. Every practical method of this note is a cheap way to draw a few plausible networks, and they differ in how plausible, and how different, the networks they draw are.</aside>

## Bayes by Backprop

**Bayes by Backprop** ([Blundell et al., 2015](#references)) is black-box variational inference with the reparameterization gradient, applied to the weights. The family is a diagonal Gaussian, $q(w) = \prod_j \mathcal N(w_j \mid \mu_j, \sigma_j^2)$, with $\sigma_j = \log(1 + e^{\rho_j})$ so that the standard deviation stays positive for any real $\rho_j$. The objective is the negative ELBO,

$$
\mathcal F(\mu, \rho) = \mathrm{KL}\big(q(w) \,\Vert\, p(w)\big) - E_{q}\big[\log p(\mathcal D \mid w)\big],
$$

estimated with a draw $w = \mu + \log(1 + e^{\rho}) \odot \varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$. With $g = \partial \tilde{\mathcal F}/\partial w$ the ordinary backpropagation gradient of the sampled cost $\tilde{\mathcal F} = \log q(w) - \log p(w) - \log p(\mathcal D \mid w)$, the chain rule gives the two gradients of the variational parameters:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_\mu &= g + \frac{\partial \tilde{\mathcal F}}{\partial \mu}, \\
\Delta_\rho &= g \odot \frac{\varepsilon}{1 + e^{-\rho}} + \frac{\partial \tilde{\mathcal F}}{\partial \rho},
\end{aligned}
$$

where the second terms differentiate $\log q$ directly and $1/(1 + e^{-\rho})$ is the derivative of $\log(1 + e^\rho)$ (their equations 3 and 4). The same backward pass serves both; the price is twice the parameters and a noisy gradient. Three details matter in practice.

- **The prior.** Blundell et al. use a scale mixture of two zero-mean Gaussians per weight, $\pi\, \mathcal N(0, \sigma_1^2) + (1 - \pi)\, \mathcal N(0, \sigma_2^2)$ with $\sigma_1 > \sigma_2$ and $\sigma_2 \ll 1$: a heavy-tailed prior with a spike near zero. Its KL has no closed form and is estimated from the same samples.
- **Mini-batches.** With $M$ mini-batches per epoch, each batch cost carries a share of the KL, for example $\tfrac1M \mathrm{KL} - E_q[\log p(\mathcal D_i \mid w)]$, so that the costs of an epoch sum to $\mathcal F$; they also propose the weights $\pi_i = 2^{M-i}/(2^M - 1)$, which put most of the KL on the first batches of an epoch (their section 3.4).
- **Variance.** One weight sample per mini-batch is shared by all its examples, which correlates their gradients. The **local reparameterization trick** ([Kingma, Salimans and Welling, 2015](#references)) samples the pre-activations of each example instead, whose distribution is Gaussian when the weights are, and lowers the variance at no extra cost.

**Worked example.** One weight with $\mu = 0.3$ and $\rho = -3$ has $\sigma = \log(1 + e^{-3}) = 0.0486$; the draw $\varepsilon = 0.5$ gives $w = 0.3 + 0.0486 \cdot 0.5 = 0.3243$, and the factor in $\Delta_\rho$ is $0.5/(1 + e^{3}) = 0.024$. Against the prior $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ this weight costs $\mathrm{KL} = \log(1/\sigma) + (\sigma^2 + \mu^2)/2 - \tfrac12 = 2.571$ nats. A network with a million such weights pays millions of nats of KL, which the likelihood of the data has to justify; weights the data do not need are pushed back towards the prior.

On MNIST with two hidden layers of 1200 ReLU units, Blundell et al. report test errors of $1.32\%$ for Bayes by Backprop with the scale-mixture prior, $1.36\%$ for dropout and $1.88\%$ for plain SGD (their Table 1), with Bayes by Backprop about twice as slow per iteration as dropout.

## MC dropout as approximate variational inference

[Dropout](/notes/deep-learning/losses-and-regularization#dropout) zeroes each unit with probability $p$ during training. [Gal and Ghahramani (2016)](#references) read it as variational inference. Their family sets the weight matrix of layer $i$ to $W_i = M_i \operatorname{diag}(z_i)$, where $M_i$ is a learned matrix and the entries of $z_i$ are independent Bernoulli variables that keep a unit with probability $1 - p$; a draw from $q$ is a network with some input units of each layer removed. They show that the dropout objective with L2 weight decay $\lambda$ equals, up to a constant factor, a one-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the negative ELBO for this $q$ under a Gaussian prior, with the model precision $\tau = (1 - p)\, l^2/(2 N \lambda)$ for a prior length-scale $l$ (their equation 7, written there with the keep probability). The practical consequence is the **MC dropout** predictive: keep dropout on at test time, run $T$ stochastic forward passes, and use their mean and variance,

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[y \mid x] &\approx \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T f(x; w_t), \\
\operatorname{Var}[y \mid x] &\approx \tau^{-1} + \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T f(x; w_t)^2 - \Big(\frac1T \sum_{t=1}^T f(x; w_t)\Big)^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second formula is the law of total variance again: noise plus the spread of the sampled networks. It costs nothing at training time and $T$ forward passes at test time, which is why it became the most widely used of these methods.

**In the experiment.** With $p = 0.1$ and 500 passes, and the noise variance taken from the training residuals instead of from $\tau$, the predictive standard deviation is $0.098$ and $0.100$ on the data, $0.144$ in the gap and $0.20$ to $0.24$ beyond the data: it grows where the data end, by a factor of $1.4$ in the gap and $2.0$ to $2.4$ beyond.

### The debate

*Optional depth: why MC dropout is an approximate posterior only in a weak sense. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

- **The variational objective is not well defined.** A $q$ whose draws set whole columns of $W_i$ exactly to zero puts its mass on a set of measure zero under a Gaussian prior, so $\mathrm{KL}(q \Vert p)$ is infinite; the finite KL term of the derivation comes from a limiting argument with a small Gaussian around each mixture component. [Hron, Matthews and Ghahramani (2018)](#references) make this precise: the approximating distribution is singular with respect to the true posterior, and for the Gaussian-dropout variant the commonly used log-uniform prior is improper and the posterior it would define is improper too. They propose a quasi-KL divergence that is finite in this situation.
- **The approximation does not concentrate.** The dropout rate is a fixed hyperparameter, not something the data determine. [Osband, Aslanides and Cassirer (2018)](#references), Lemma 1, observe that the trained dropout distribution is unchanged when every data point is duplicated, whereas a posterior from twice the data would be narrower. With the mean squared error used here, duplication changes nothing at all. Learning the rate, as in concrete dropout ([Gal, Hron and Kendall, 2017](#references)), addresses this only partly.
- **It still often helps.** Used as a heuristic, MC dropout widens predictions where the data are thin, as in the experiment, at almost no cost. In a large comparison under dataset shift, [Ovadia et al. (2019)](#references) found it ahead of temperature scaling and of last-layer methods on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet, but consistently behind deep ensembles.

## Deep ensembles

A **deep ensemble** ([Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017](#references)) trains $M$ networks independently, from different random initializations and with different shuffling of the data, and treats them as a uniform mixture. For regression each network may output a mean $\mu_m(x)$ and a variance $\sigma_m^2(x)$ trained by the Gaussian negative log-likelihood, a proper scoring rule, and the mixture is summarized by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_*(x) &= \frac1M \sum_{m=1}^M \mu_m(x), \\
\sigma_*^2(x) &= \frac1M \sum_{m=1}^M \big(\sigma_m^2(x) + \mu_m^2(x)\big) - \mu_*^2(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The recommended default is $M = 5$; optional adversarial training smooths the predictive distribution, and the authors found that training on bootstrap samples, as in [bagging](/notes/ml/bagging-and-random-forests), made results worse than random initialization alone. Ensembles are not derived from a posterior, but each member sits near a different mode of the loss, and averaging over modes is what a single-mode approximation such as a diagonal Gaussian or a Laplace approximation cannot do; [Wilson and Izmailov (2020)](#references) argue for reading them as an approximation of Bayesian model averaging for this reason. The costs are $M$ trainings and $M$ forward passes, which can be reduced afterwards by [distilling](/notes/deep-learning/model-compression#knowledge-distillation) the ensemble into one network.

**In the experiment.** Five networks trained on the squared error, with the noise variance from the residuals of their average: the predictive standard deviation is $0.079$ on the data, $0.288$ in the gap (the figure above), $0.163$ at $x = 6$ and $0.581$ at $x = -7$. Of the three models it widens its band the most in the gap and at $x = -7$; at $x = 6$ its band is narrower than that of MC dropout, $0.163$ against $0.202$.

## The Laplace approximation for networks

The [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) of the course fits a Gaussian at the mode: $q(w) = \mathcal N\big(w_{\text{MAP}}, H^{-1}\big)$ with $H$ the Hessian of the negative log posterior at the MAP weights. For networks it goes back to [MacKay (1992)](#references), who also used the evidence of this Gaussian to choose weight decay. Two obstacles made it unpopular and have since been worked around ([Daxberger et al., 2021](#references)):

- **The Hessian is $P \times P$.** It is replaced by a positive semidefinite approximation (the generalized Gauss–Newton matrix or the Fisher) with diagonal or Kronecker-factored structure (KFAC, [Ritter, Botev and Barber, 2018](#references)), or computed only for the last layer, where it is small and the remaining layers act as fixed features.
- **Sampling weights through the network predicts poorly** with these approximations. A **linearized** predictive, which replaces $f(x; w)$ by its first-order Taylor expansion around $w_{\text{MAP}}$, turns the Gaussian over weights into a Gaussian over outputs and works better; for the last-layer approximation it is exact.

Its main practical advantage is that it is **post hoc**: a network trained in the usual way gets uncertainty afterwards, at the cost of one curvature computation. Like Bayes by Backprop it describes one mode.

## Calibration and inputs far from the data

A classifier is **calibrated** when, among the inputs to which it assigns probability $0.8$, a share of $0.8$ belongs to the class; the [logistic regression note](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#calibration-do-the-probabilities-mean-what-they-say) defines the reliability diagram and the expected calibration error. Modern networks are often miscalibrated towards overconfidence: on CIFAR-100, the average confidence of a 110-layer ResNet is well above its accuracy, while that of a 5-layer LeNet matches it ([Guo et al., 2017](#references), Figure 1). Within the training distribution a single scalar fixes most of this: **temperature scaling** divides the logits by a $T$ fitted on validation data. It does nothing for inputs unlike the training data, and that is where averaging over weights is supposed to help.

**Far from the data, single networks are confidently wrong.** A ReLU network is piecewise linear, so along any ray $x = t\,u$ its logits eventually grow linearly in $t$, and the softmax tends to a one-hot vector: almost every ReLU classifier becomes arbitrarily confident far enough from its training data ([Hein, Andriushchenko and Bitterwolf, 2019](#references)). The regression experiment shows the same mechanism in one dimension.

<GapRegression />

All three means extrapolate linearly beyond $[-4, 4]$. At $x = 6$ the error of the mean is $1.87$ for the deterministic network, $1.44$ for MC dropout and $2.28$ for the ensemble, which is $24$, $7$ and $14$ of their predictive standard deviations. MC dropout and the ensemble widen their bands where the data end, the ensemble most in the gap, but not nearly enough. The approximations are better than one network and still overconfident out of distribution, and in this run the method with the widest band in the gap also has the largest error at $x = 6$. Large studies tell the same story at scale: under increasing dataset shift, calibration degrades for every method, and deep ensembles degrade the least ([Ovadia et al., 2019](#references)). These are statements about one small run here and about the benchmarks of that study, not a ranking for every problem.

## What changed

| | Deterministic network | Bayes by Backprop | MC dropout | Deep ensemble | Laplace |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution over weights | one point | diagonal Gaussian | Bernoulli masks of trained weights | $M$ trained networks | Gaussian at the MAP |
| Extra training cost | none | about 2× per step, 2× parameters | none | $M$ trainings | one curvature computation |
| Prediction cost | 1 pass | $T$ passes | $T$ passes | $M$ passes | linearized or $T$ passes |
| Modes | one | one | one | several | one |
| Std in the gap, the experiment | 0.077 | not run | 0.144 | 0.288 | not run |

## Limitations

- **Every method is an approximation of an unknown target.** The true posterior of a network is not available for checking except on small problems with long HMC runs, so the quality of an approximation is judged by its predictions: calibration, held-out likelihood, behaviour under shift.
- **The prior over weights is hard to choose.** An isotropic Gaussian on the weights induces a prior over functions that is poorly understood, and the predictive depends on it.
- **Single-mode approximations miss the rest.** Diagonal Gaussians and Laplace describe the neighbourhood of one solution; ensembles cover a few modes and nothing between them.
- **Overconfidence far from the data remains.** None of the methods of this note guarantees that uncertainty grows as fast as the error out of distribution; in the experiment all of them were wrong by many of their standard deviations at $x = 6$.
- **Costs scale with $T$ or $M$.** Every sample of the weights is a forward pass, and ensembles multiply training too.

## What you should remember

- The predictive distribution averages $p(y \mid x, w)$ over the posterior; its variance is the noise plus the spread of plausible networks, and only the second term grows where the data end.
- Bayes by Backprop is BBVI with the reparameterization gradient on a diagonal Gaussian over weights, $\sigma = \log(1 + e^\rho)$, the negative ELBO as the loss and the KL shared across mini-batches.
- MC dropout keeps dropout on at test time and averages $T$ passes; its reading as variational inference is approximate, its objective is not a proper KL, and it does not concentrate with more data, but it is cheap and often useful.
- Deep ensembles train $M = 5$ networks from different initializations, combine them as a mixture, cover several modes and have been the strongest of these methods under dataset shift.
- The Laplace approximation adds a Gaussian at the trained weights after training, with a structured Hessian and a linearized predictive.
- Modern networks are overconfident; temperature scaling fixes calibration in distribution, and all approximate posteriors remain overconfident far from the data.

## Code

One weight of Bayes by Backprop, then the experiment: a deterministic network, MC dropout and a deep ensemble on the regression with a gap, printing the predictive mean and standard deviation in the data, in the gap and beyond the data range. PyTorch on the CPU; seeds are fixed, and exact numbers vary across platforms and library versions. The script runs in about 8 seconds.

```python
import numpy as np, torch
from torch import nn

# 0. one weight of Bayes by Backprop: sigma = softplus(rho), a reparameterized draw, and its KL to the prior N(0, 1)
mu_w, rho_w, eps_w = 0.3, -3.0, 0.5
sigma_w = np.log1p(np.exp(rho_w))
kl = np.log(1 / sigma_w) + (sigma_w ** 2 + mu_w ** 2) / 2 - 0.5
print(round(sigma_w, 4), round(mu_w + sigma_w * eps_w, 4), round(kl, 3))
# 0.0486 0.3243 2.571

# 1. data with a gap: 40 points on [-4, -1] and 40 on [1, 4], y = sin(x) + noise of standard deviation 0.1
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = np.concatenate([rng.uniform(-4, -1, 40), rng.uniform(1, 4, 40)])
y = np.sin(x) + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=x.size)
X, Y = torch.tensor(x, dtype=torch.float32)[:, None], torch.tensor(y, dtype=torch.float32)[:, None]
xs = torch.tensor([-2.5, 2.5, 0.0, 6.0, -7.0])[:, None]        # two points in the data, the gap, two beyond the range

def net(p=0.0):                                  # 1 -> 128 -> 128 -> 1, ReLU, optional dropout after each hidden layer
    drop = (lambda: [nn.Dropout(p)]) if p else (lambda: [])
    return nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1, 128), nn.ReLU(), *drop(), nn.Linear(128, 128), nn.ReLU(), *drop(), nn.Linear(128, 1))

def train(model, weight_decay=0.0, steps=3000):  # full-batch Adam on the squared error
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-3, weight_decay=weight_decay)
    for _ in range(steps):
        opt.zero_grad(); ((model(X) - Y) ** 2).mean().backward(); opt.step()
    return model

def report(name, mean, var_model, noise_var):
    std = (var_model + noise_var).sqrt().flatten()
    print(f'{name:13}', 'mean', np.round(mean.flatten().numpy(), 2), 'std', np.round(std.numpy(), 3))

print('sin(x)       ', np.round(np.sin(xs.flatten().numpy()), 2))
# 2. a deterministic network: its only uncertainty is the noise variance estimated from the residuals
torch.manual_seed(0); det = train(net()).eval()
with torch.no_grad():
    report('deterministic', det(xs), torch.zeros(len(xs), 1), ((det(X) - Y) ** 2).mean())

# 3. MC dropout, p = 0.1: dropout stays on at test time; 500 stochastic passes give the mean and the model variance
torch.manual_seed(0); mc = train(net(0.1), weight_decay=1e-4).train()
with torch.no_grad():
    S = torch.stack([mc(torch.cat([X, xs])) for _ in range(500)])
    noise = ((S[:, :len(X)].mean(0) - Y) ** 2).mean()                # noise variance from the training residuals
    report('MC dropout', S[:, len(X):].mean(0), S[:, len(X):].var(0), noise)

# 4. a deep ensemble: five networks from different random initializations
members = []
for k in range(5):
    torch.manual_seed(k + 1); m = train(net()).eval()
    with torch.no_grad(): members.append(m(torch.cat([X, xs])))
P = torch.stack(members)
report('ensemble', P[:, len(X):].mean(0), P[:, len(X):].var(0), ((P[:, :len(X)].mean(0) - Y) ** 2).mean())
print('members at x = 0:', np.round(P[:, len(X) + 2, 0].numpy(), 2))
# sin(x)        [-0.6   0.6   0.   -0.28 -0.66]
# deterministic mean [-0.56  0.6   0.53 -2.15  3.02] std [0.077 0.077 0.077 0.077 0.077]
# MC dropout    mean [-0.54  0.56  0.04 -1.72  2.06] std [0.098 0.1   0.144 0.202 0.238]
# ensemble      mean [-0.56  0.58  0.43 -2.56  3.82] std [0.079 0.079 0.288 0.163 0.581]
# members at x = 0: [0.06 0.76 0.47 0.6  0.25]
```

## The course in one paragraph

Bayesian methods treat everything unknown as a random variable and answer every question with a distribution, and the course followed what that costs and how to pay it. The first section set up the approach: [the posterior](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-approach) as prior times likelihood, [conjugate priors](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors) that keep it in closed form, and [the evidence](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection) that compares models without a validation set. The second applied it to linear models: [Bayesian linear regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression) with its predictive variance, [the relevance vector machine](/notes/bayesian-methods/relevance-vector-machine) whose priors switch features off, and [the Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation) for classification, where conjugacy ends. The third met posteriors without formulas: [EM and Bayesian PCA](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca) for latent variables, [variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference) that optimizes a lower bound, and [Gibbs sampling](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling) that samples instead. The fourth let the model grow with the data: [Gaussian processes](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes) over functions, [Dirichlet processes](/notes/bayesian-methods/dirichlet-processes) over partitions, and [LDA](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation) over topics. The fifth made inference scale: [stochastic variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference) with mini-batches and natural gradients, [black-box variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference) with Monte Carlo gradients and the VAE, and this note's approximate posteriors over the weights of networks. The [Generative Models course](/notes/generative-models/generative-modeling) builds on exactly these tools: the [variational autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/vae) and its [variants](/notes/generative-models/vae-variants) are amortized variational inference, [HMC and NUTS](/notes/generative-models/mcmc-samplers) are the samplers that Gibbs sampling led to, and [diffusion models](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective) are trained with an evidence lower bound on a chain of latent variables.
