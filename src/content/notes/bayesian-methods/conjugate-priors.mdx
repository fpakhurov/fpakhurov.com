A coin is flipped seven times: $1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1$. Before the flips you believed it was probably close to fair, a $\mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)$ prior on its probability of heads $f$. After them, your belief is $\mathrm{Beta}(7, 4)$: two pseudo-counts went up by the five heads and the two tails. Two numbers carry the whole posterior, and the next flip is heads with probability $7/11$, the [posterior mean](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-approach#a-coin-with-an-unknown-bias).

Most posteriors are not like that. The [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-approach) answered every question by a sum over eleven urns. With a continuous parameter the sum becomes an integral, and the normalizing integral of Bayes' formula, the evidence, usually has no closed form.

Without a formula, the posterior can still be computed on a grid: evaluate likelihood × prior at $G$ points per parameter and normalize. The cost is $G^d$ evaluations for $d$ parameters. The Code section computes the posterior of a normal mean and precision on a $1201 \times 1600$ grid, about two million evaluations for two parameters. Ten parameters with a coarse 100 points each would need $10^{20}$. Exact Bayesian inference in higher dimensions needs integrals done on paper, and the coin is the simplest case in which paper suffices.

## Conjugacy: the posterior stays in the family

A family of priors $p(\theta \mid \alpha)$, indexed by **hyperparameters** $\alpha$, is **conjugate** to a likelihood $p(x \mid \theta)$ if for every prior in the family and every dataset the posterior is again in the family:

$$
p(\theta \mid X, \alpha) \propto p(X \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \alpha) \propto p(\theta \mid \alpha') .
$$

A primed hyperparameter, $\alpha'$, is the value after the update. Learning is the map $\alpha \to \alpha'$ on a few numbers. Because the posterior is again a prior of the family, the map can be applied one observation at a time, and by the [sequential updating argument](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-approach#sequential-updating) the result does not depend on the order or the batching of the data.

How do we find a conjugate prior before any theory? We read the likelihood of one observation as a function of the parameter and look for densities of the same functional form, since multiplying two such functions keeps the form. Take a normal with mean zero and an unknown precision $\lambda$:

$$
\mathcal N(x \mid 0, \lambda^{-1}) = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}}\, e^{-\lambda x^2/2} .
$$

As a function of $\lambda$ it is a power of $\lambda$ times the exponential of a linear function of $\lambda$. The gamma density $\lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda}$, up to its constant, has the same form. Their product adds $1/2$ to $a$ and $x^2/2$ to $b$, so the gamma prior is conjugate. The [exponential family](#the-exponential-family) turns this reading into a rule.

Two more quantities come free. Write the prior as $p(\theta \mid \alpha) = \tilde p(\theta \mid \alpha) / Z(\alpha)$, with $\tilde p$ unnormalized and $Z$ its normalizing constant. Conjugacy means that the likelihood times $\tilde p(\theta \mid \alpha)$ equals $c(X)\, \tilde p(\theta \mid \alpha')$ for a factor $c(X)$ that does not depend on $\theta$. The evidence is the sum rule over $\theta$ followed by the product rule; substituting this identity and integrating,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(X \mid \alpha)
&= \int p(X \mid \theta)\, \frac{\tilde p(\theta \mid \alpha)}{Z(\alpha)}\, d\theta \\
&= \frac{c(X)}{Z(\alpha)} \int \tilde p(\theta \mid \alpha')\, d\theta && \text{(conjugacy)} \\
&= c(X)\, \frac{Z(\alpha')}{Z(\alpha)} && \text{(definition of } Z) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The evidence is a ratio of normalizing constants. The predictive of a new point is the evidence of one more observation divided by the evidence of the data, $p(x_* \mid X, \alpha) = p(X, x_* \mid \alpha) / p(X \mid \alpha)$ by the definition of a conditional distribution, and so again a ratio of normalizing constants.

## Beta–Bernoulli and the evidence \{#beta-bernoulli-and-the-evidence\}

The $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ density on $[0, 1]$ is $f^{a-1}(1-f)^{b-1} / B(a, b)$, with the normalizing constant $B(a, b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$, where $\Gamma(a) = \int_0^\infty t^{a-1} e^{-t}\, dt$ and $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$ for integers.

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the beta constant</summary>

Write $\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)$ as a double integral over $s, r > 0$ and change variables to $s = tf$, $r = t(1 - f)$ with $t > 0$, $f \in (0, 1)$, whose Jacobian is $t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)
&= \int_0^\infty \!\! \int_0^\infty s^{a-1} r^{b-1} e^{-s-r}\, ds\, dr \\
&= \int_0^\infty t^{a+b-1} e^{-t}\, dt \int_0^1 f^{a-1} (1-f)^{b-1}\, df \\
&= \Gamma(a+b)\, B(a, b) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The same substitution with $K$ variables gives the Dirichlet constant below.

</details>

For flips $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ with probability of heads $f$, the posterior after $k$ heads in $n$ flips is $\mathrm{Beta}(a + k,\ b + n - k)$. The [Bayesian inference note](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#conjugate-priors-the-beta-binomial) derived this update and read $a$ and $b$ as pseudo-counts.

The new ingredient is the evidence. The likelihood of the sequence is $f^k (1-f)^{n-k}$, so $c(X) = 1$ and

$$
p(X \mid a, b) = \frac{B(a + k,\ b + n - k)}{B(a, b)} .
$$

For the seven flips of the opening, $k = 5$ and the prior is $\mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)$; with $\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!$,

$$
\frac{B(7, 4)}{B(2, 2)} = \frac{6!\, 3! / 10!}{1!\, 1! / 3!} = \frac{1/840}{1/6} = \frac{1}{140} = 0.00714 .
$$

The same number is the product of the seven one-step predictives, each the current posterior mean $a/(a + b)$ or its complement, with $a$ or $b$ raised by one after each flip:

$$
\frac{2}{4} \cdot \frac{2}{5} \cdot \frac{3}{6} \cdot \frac{4}{7} \cdot \frac{3}{8} \cdot \frac{5}{9} \cdot \frac{6}{10} = \frac{1}{140} .
$$

By the product rule, $p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})$: the evidence is the probability with which the model predicted each observation from the ones before it. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection) builds model comparison on this reading.

## Dirichlet–categorical \{#dirichlet-categorical\}

A categorical variable takes one of $K$ values with probabilities $\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K)$. The **Dirichlet distribution** $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)$ on such probability vectors has density proportional to $\prod_k \pi_k^{\alpha_k - 1}$; write $\alpha_0 = \sum_k \alpha_k$. With counts $n_1, \dots, n_K$ in $n$ observations, the likelihood is $\prod_k \pi_k^{n_k}$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\pi \mid X)
&\propto \prod_{k=1}^K \pi_k^{n_k} \cdot \prod_{k=1}^K \pi_k^{\alpha_k - 1} \\
&= \prod_{k=1}^K \pi_k^{\alpha_k + n_k - 1} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

so $\pi \mid X \sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_1 + n_1, \dots, \alpha_K + n_K)$. The Dirichlet is the beta distribution for $K$ categories. Its normalizing constant is $Z(\alpha) = \prod_k \Gamma(\alpha_k) / \Gamma(\alpha_0)$. Both quantities below are ratios of such constants. The mean of $\pi_k$ multiplies the density by one more $\pi_k$, so it is $Z(\alpha + e_k)/Z(\alpha) = \alpha_k/\alpha_0$ by $\Gamma(x + 1) = x\,\Gamma(x)$, with $e_k$ the $k$-th unit vector. The posterior mean is the predictive probability of category $k$, as for the coin, and the evidence is $Z(\alpha + n)/Z(\alpha)$ with $c(X) = 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(x_* = k \mid X) &= \frac{\alpha_k + n_k}{\alpha_0 + n}, \\
p(X \mid \alpha) &= \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\Gamma(\alpha_0 + n)} \prod_{k=1}^K \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_k + n_k)}{\Gamma(\alpha_k)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Take four categories, a uniform prior $\mathrm{Dir}(1, 1, 1, 1)$ and counts $(5, 2, 0, 1)$. The posterior is $\mathrm{Dir}(6, 3, 1, 2)$, and the predictive probabilities are $0.5, 0.25, 0.083, 0.167$. The category never seen keeps probability $1/12$ instead of the maximum likelihood value 0. The evidence, with $\Gamma(4) = 3!$, $\Gamma(12) = 11!$ and $\Gamma(1 + n_k) = n_k!$, is

$$
\frac{3!}{11!} \cdot 5!\, 2!\, 0!\, 1! = \frac{1}{27720} .
$$

Adding $\alpha$ to every count is the add-$\alpha$ [smoothing of n-gram language models](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models#smoothing), seen as a posterior mean. The total $\alpha_0$ is the strength of the prior in pseudo-counts. Values $\alpha_k < 1$ make the exponents $\alpha_k - 1$ negative, so the density grows without bound as any $\pi_k \to 0$: the prior mass sits near the edges and corners of the simplex and favours sparse probability vectors, which is how [latent Dirichlet allocation](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation) makes each document use few topics.

## Normal–normal: an unknown mean \{#normal-normal-an-unknown-mean\}

For observations from $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with known $\sigma^2$ and a prior $\mathcal N(\mu_0, \tau^2)$, the [Bayesian inference note](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#normal-data-and-shrinkage) derived the posterior $\mathcal N(\mu_n, \sigma_n^2)$ with

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\sigma_n^2} &= \frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \\
\frac{\mu_n}{\sigma_n^2} &= \frac{\mu_0}{\tau^2} + \frac{\sum_i x_i}{\sigma^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The inverse of a variance is a **precision**. Written this way, both updates are additions: the precision grows by $1/\sigma^2$ per observation, and precision × mean grows by $x_i/\sigma^2$. The data enter only through $n$ and $\sum_i x_i$, a first example of the sufficient statistics below.

The predictive of a new point $x_* = \mu + \varepsilon$ adds the noise to the posterior uncertainty of the mean. Given $X$, the mean $\mu$ and the new noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)$ are independent, and the sum of two independent Gaussians is Gaussian with the means and the variances added ([linear-Gaussian marginal](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#5-the-linear-gaussian-marginal)):

$$
p(x_* \mid X) = \mathcal N(x_* \mid \mu_n,\ \sigma_n^2 + \sigma^2) .
$$

With the prior $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, noise variance $\sigma^2 = 4$ and four observations with mean $1.5$, the posterior precision is $1 + 4/4 = 2$. So $\sigma_n^2 = 0.5$ and, with $\sum_i x_i = 6$, $\mu_n = 0.5 \cdot (0 + 6/4) = 0.75$, and the predictive is $\mathcal N(0.75, 4.5)$. For vectors, the same update with precision matrices is the [conditional of a joint Gaussian](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals), and [Bayesian linear regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression) is this update for the weights of a linear model.

## Normal–gamma: unknown mean and precision \{#normal-gamma-unknown-mean-and-precision\}

When the noise level is unknown too, both $\mu$ and the precision $\lambda = 1/\sigma^2$ need a prior. The likelihood of $n$ observations is

$$
p(X \mid \mu, \lambda) = \Big(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\Big)^{n/2} \exp\Big(-\frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2\Big) .
$$

Why not take independent priors, a normal for $\mu$ and a gamma for $\lambda$? Expand the square: the exponent contains $\lambda \mu x_i$ and $\lambda \mu^2$, products of the two unknowns, so the likelihood does not factorize into a function of $\mu$ times a function of $\lambda$. A conjugate prior must have the same shape, so it cannot factorize either. With independent priors the posterior leaves every standard family; the [variational inference note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference) starts from exactly that case.

The conjugate prior ties the two together. Write the **gamma distribution** with shape $a$ and rate $b$ as $\mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda \mid a, b) = b^a \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda} / \Gamma(a)$, with mean $a/b$. The **normal–gamma** prior is

$$
p(\mu, \lambda) = \mathcal N\big(\mu \mid \mu_0, (\kappa_0 \lambda)^{-1}\big)\, \mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda \mid a_0, b_0) ,
$$

in which, given $\lambda$, the mean has the precision of $\kappa_0$ observations: the more precise the data, the more precisely the mean is pinned down.

Two identities do the update. With $\bar x$ the sample mean and $S = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2$, the sum of squares splits as $\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 = S + n(\bar x - \mu)^2$: write $x_i - \mu = (x_i - \bar x) + (\bar x - \mu)$ and expand; the cross term vanishes because $\sum_i (x_i - \bar x) = 0$. Completing the square in $\mu$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2 &+ n(\mu - \bar x)^2 \\
&= \kappa_n (\mu - \mu_n)^2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n}{\kappa_n} (\bar x - \mu_0)^2 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\kappa_n = \kappa_0 + n$ and $\mu_n = (\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n \bar x)/\kappa_n$. To check it, compare the two sides: the coefficients of $\mu^2$ are both $\kappa_n$, those of $\mu$ both $-2\kappa_n \mu_n$, and the constants agree because $\kappa_n(\kappa_0 \mu_0^2 + n \bar x^2) - (\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n \bar x)^2 = \kappa_0 n (\bar x - \mu_0)^2$.

Now multiply the prior by the likelihood and keep only what depends on $\mu$ or $\lambda$. The prior contributes $\lambda^{1/2} e^{-\kappa_0 \lambda (\mu - \mu_0)^2/2}\, \lambda^{a_0 - 1} e^{-b_0 \lambda}$, the likelihood $\lambda^{n/2} e^{-\lambda (S + n(\bar x - \mu)^2)/2}$. By the two identities the product is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\lambda^{1/2} e^{-\kappa_n \lambda (\mu - \mu_n)^2 / 2} \\
&\times \lambda^{a_0 + n/2 - 1} e^{-\lambda \left(b_0 + \frac S2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n (\bar x - \mu_0)^2}{2\kappa_n}\right)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is a normal density in $\mu$ with precision $\kappa_n \lambda$, up to its constant; the second is a gamma density in $\lambda$. The posterior is normal–gamma with

$$
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_n &= \kappa_0 + n, \\
\mu_n &= \frac{\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n \bar x}{\kappa_n}, \\
a_n &= a_0 + \frac n2, \\
b_n &= b_0 + \frac S2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n (\bar x - \mu_0)^2}{2 \kappa_n} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The rate $b_n$ collects three sources of squared deviation: the prior's, the spread of the data around their mean, and the disagreement between the data mean and the prior mean. Writing $\sigma^2 = 1/\lambda$ instead gives the same model as the normal–inverse-gamma prior.

The marginal posterior of the mean integrates out the precision. For a normal with precision $\tau\lambda$ and a $\mathrm{Gamma}(a, b)$ precision, using $\int_0^\infty \lambda^{c-1} e^{-d\lambda}\, d\lambda = \Gamma(c)/d^c$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty \lambda^{1/2} e^{-\tau\lambda (\mu - m)^2 / 2}&\, \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda}\, d\lambda \\
&\propto \Big(b + \frac{\tau (\mu - m)^2}{2}\Big)^{-(a + 1/2)} \\
&\propto \Big(1 + \frac{(\mu - m)^2}{2a \cdot b/(a\tau)}\Big)^{-(2a+1)/2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line takes $b^{-(a + 1/2)}$ out of the bracket. The **Student-t** density with location $m$, squared scale $s^2$ and $\nu$ degrees of freedom is, by definition, $\mathrm{St}(\mu \mid m, s^2, \nu) \propto \big(1 + (\mu - m)^2/(\nu s^2)\big)^{-(\nu + 1)/2}$. Matching the last line gives $\nu = 2a$ and $s^2 = b/(a\tau)$. So

$$
p(\mu \mid X) = \mathrm{St}\Big(\mu \;\Big|\; \mu_n,\ \frac{b_n}{a_n \kappa_n},\ 2a_n\Big) .
$$

Take the prior $\mu_0 = 0$, $\kappa_0 = 1$, $a_0 = b_0 = 1$ and the data $1.2, 0.4, 2.1, 1.5, 0.8$:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| $n$, $\bar x$, $S$ | $5$, $1.2$, $1.70$ |
| $\kappa_n$, $\mu_n$, $a_n$ | $6$, $1.0$, $3.5$ |
| $b_n = 1 + 1.70/2 + 5 \cdot 1.2^2/12$ | $2.45$ |
| squared scale of $\mu$, $b_n/(a_n \kappa_n)$ | $0.117$ |

The mean has a Student-t posterior with 7 degrees of freedom, centre $1.0$ and squared scale $0.117$. The Code section checks this marginal against the two-million-point grid of the opening: the two densities agree to $10^{-4}$.

## The exponential family

Every pair so far had the same structure. The likelihood depended on the data through a few sums: $k$ and $n$; the counts $n_k$; $\sum x_i$, $\sum x_i^2$ and $n$. And the prior had the form of that likelihood. The exponential family is the class of distributions for which this always happens.

### Natural parameters and sufficient statistics

A distribution belongs to the **exponential family** if its density, or probability mass function, can be written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(x \mid \eta) &= h(x) \exp\big(\eta^\top u(x) - A(\eta)\big), \\
A(\eta) &= \log \int h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top u(x)}\, dx ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\eta$ are the **natural parameters**, $u(x)$ the **sufficient statistics**, $h(x)$ a base measure, and $A(\eta)$ the **log-partition function**, which makes the density integrate to 1. The usual parameters are a reparametrization of $\eta$:

| Distribution | $\eta$ | $u(x)$ | $A(\eta)$ | $h(x)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bernoulli$(p)$ | $\log \frac{p}{1-p}$ | $x$ | $\log(1 + e^\eta)$ | $1$ |
| Poisson$(\lambda)$ | $\log \lambda$ | $x$ | $e^\eta$ | $1/x!$ |
| $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $\big(\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}, -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\big)$ | $(x, x^2)$ | $-\frac{\eta_1^2}{4\eta_2} - \frac12 \log(-2\eta_2)$ | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ |
| Categorical$(\pi)$ | $\log \frac{\pi_k}{\pi_K}$, $k < K$ | one-hot, first $K - 1$ entries | $\log\big(1 + \sum_{k<K} e^{\eta_k}\big)$ | $1$ |
| $\mathrm{Gamma}(a, b)$ | $(a - 1, -b)$ | $(\log x, x)$ | $\log\Gamma(\eta_1 + 1) - (\eta_1 + 1)\log(-\eta_2)$ | $1$ |

For the Bernoulli, $p^x (1-p)^{1-x} = \exp\big(x \log\frac{p}{1-p} + \log(1 - p)\big)$ and $-\log(1-p) = \log(1 + e^\eta)$. For the normal, expanding $-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ gives the terms $x \mu/\sigma^2$ and $-x^2/(2\sigma^2)$, and the rest, $\mu^2/(2\sigma^2) + \log\sigma$, is $A$ written in $\eta$. The beta, Dirichlet, multivariate normal and Wishart distributions belong to the family too: the beta density is $\exp\big((a-1)\log f + (b-1)\log(1-f) - \log B(a, b)\big)$, and the others expand the same way. The uniform distribution on $[0, \theta]$ with unknown $\theta$ does not: its support moves with $\theta$, while $h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top u(x) - A(\eta)}$ is positive wherever $h$ is, whatever $\eta$. The Student-t and mixtures do not either: their log-densities, $\log\big(1 + (x - m)^2/(\nu s^2)\big)$ or a log of a sum, do not split into a sum of products of a function of the parameters and a function of $x$.

For i.i.d. data the log-likelihood is

$$
\log p(X \mid \eta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log h(x_i) + \eta^\top \sum_{i=1}^n u(x_i) - n A(\eta) .
$$

The data enter only through $\sum_i u(x_i)$ and $n$, a vector whose size does not grow with $n$. Two datasets with the same sums have proportional likelihoods and therefore, under any prior, the same posterior.

### Derivatives of the log-partition function

Differentiating the normalization $e^{A(\eta)} = \int h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top u(x)}\, dx$ under the integral sign gives the moments of $u(x)$. The exchange of derivative and integral is valid for $\eta$ in the interior of the set where $A$ is finite; [Murphy (2023](#references), Section 2.4.3) derives the same identities. The first derivative:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla A(\eta)\, e^{A(\eta)} &= \int u(x)\, h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top u(x)}\, dx, \\
\nabla A(\eta) &= \int u(x)\, p(x \mid \eta)\, dx = \mathbb E[u(x)] .
\end{aligned}
$$

Differentiating $\nabla A(\eta) = \int u(x)\, h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top u(x) - A(\eta)}\, dx$ once more, the exponent contributes $u(x) - \nabla A(\eta)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^2 A(\eta)
&= \int u(x) \big(u(x) - \nabla A(\eta)\big)^\top p(x \mid \eta)\, dx \\
&= \mathbb E[u u^\top] - \mathbb E[u]\, \mathbb E[u]^\top \\
&= \operatorname{Cov}[u(x)] .
\end{aligned}
$$

Moments therefore come from derivatives. For the Bernoulli, $A'(\eta) = e^\eta/(1 + e^\eta) = p$ and $A''(\eta) = p(1 - p)$. For the normal, $\partial A/\partial \eta_1 = -\eta_1/(2\eta_2) = \mu$ and $\partial A/\partial\eta_2 = \mu^2 + \sigma^2 = \mathbb E[x^2]$. For the gamma, $\partial A/\partial\eta_1 = \psi(a) - \log b = \mathbb E[\log x]$, with $\psi$ the digamma function, a moment that is hard to get any other way and that every variational update with a gamma factor needs.

A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite, so $A$ is convex, by the [second-order condition](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-second-order-condition-a-positive-semidefinite-hessian) for convexity. The log-likelihood above is then concave in $\eta$. It is strictly concave when no linear combination of the statistics $u(x)$ is constant, because $c^\top \nabla^2 A\, c = \operatorname{Var}[c^\top u(x)]$ is then positive for every $c \ne 0$; a strictly concave function has at most one maximum. Setting its gradient to zero gives the maximum likelihood equation

$$
\nabla A(\hat\eta) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n u(x_i) .
$$

This is **moment matching**: the fitted model reproduces the sample averages of the sufficient statistics. The Gaussian fit by sample mean and sample second moment is an instance, and so is the [logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression) gradient, predicted minus observed.

## Why the exponential family has conjugate priors

Look at the likelihood as a function of $\eta$: it is $\exp\big(\eta^\top \sum_i u(x_i) - n A(\eta)\big)$ times a factor free of $\eta$. A prior of the same shape,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\eta \mid \chi, \nu) &= \frac{1}{Z(\chi, \nu)} \exp\big(\eta^\top \chi - \nu A(\eta)\big), \\
Z(\chi, \nu) &= \int \exp\big(\eta^\top \chi - \nu A(\eta)\big)\, d\eta ,
\end{aligned}
$$

multiplies with it into the same shape again:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\eta \mid X) &\propto \exp\big(\eta^\top \chi' - \nu' A(\eta)\big), \\
\chi' &= \chi + \sum_{i=1}^n u(x_i), \\
\nu' &= \nu + n .
\end{aligned}
$$

The prior is the likelihood of $\nu$ imaginary observations whose sufficient statistics sum to $\chi$, and the update adds the real ones ([Bishop, 2006](#references), Section 2.4.2). It is a proper prior only when $Z(\chi, \nu)$ is finite; for the Bernoulli below this means $0 < \chi < \nu$. With $c(X) = \prod_i h(x_i)$, the general formulas of the conjugacy section become

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(X \mid \chi, \nu) &= \prod_{i=1}^n h(x_i) \cdot \frac{Z(\chi', \nu')}{Z(\chi, \nu)}, \\
p(x_* \mid X) &= h(x_*)\, \frac{Z\big(\chi' + u(x_*),\ \nu' + 1\big)}{Z(\chi', \nu')} .
\end{aligned}
$$

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> For an exponential-family likelihood, the conjugate prior is the likelihood of $\nu$ imaginary observations with summed statistics $\chi$. Learning adds the real statistics; evidence and predictive are ratios of the prior's normalizer.</aside>

The beta prior is an instance. For the Bernoulli, $A(\eta) = \log(1 + e^\eta)$ with $e^\eta = p/(1-p)$, so $e^{\eta\chi} = \big(\frac{p}{1-p}\big)^\chi$ and $e^{-\nu A(\eta)} = (1 - p)^\nu$. The prior density in $\eta$ is proportional to $p^\chi (1-p)^{\nu - \chi}$. As a density in $p$ it gains the Jacobian $d\eta/dp = 1/\big(p(1-p)\big)$:

$$
p(p \mid \chi, \nu) \propto p^{\chi - 1} (1 - p)^{\nu - \chi - 1} = \mathrm{Beta}(p \mid \chi,\ \nu - \chi) .
$$

So $a = \chi$ and $b = \nu - \chi$, and the update $\chi' = \chi + k$, $\nu' = \nu + n$ is $a' = a + k$, $b' = b + n - k$. The Jacobian is why the beta exponents are $a - 1$ rather than $a$, the offset between the two pseudo-count conventions of the [Bayesian inference note](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#conjugate-priors-the-beta-binomial). For the seven flips, $\mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)$ is four imaginary flips with two heads; adding the real $\sum_i x_i = 5$ and $n = 7$ gives $\chi' = 7$, $\nu' = 11$:

<ConjugateUpdate />

Conjugacy is not limited to the exponential family. The uniform distribution on $[0, \theta]$ has the likelihood $\theta^{-n}$ for $\theta \ge \max_i x_i$ and 0 otherwise; the Pareto prior $\propto \theta^{-(\alpha + 1)}$ for $\theta \ge x_m$ multiplies with it into a Pareto with $\alpha + n$ and $\max(x_m, \max_i x_i)$. For families with a support that does not depend on the parameter, though, a sufficient statistic of fixed size for every $n$, and with it this kind of conjugate prior, exists only in the exponential family. This is the Pitman–Koopman–Darmois theorem, which also needs smooth densities; its proof is in [Koopman (1936)](#references), and [Murphy (2023](#references), Section 3.4.5) discusses it.

### Mixtures of conjugate priors

*Optional depth: how to keep conjugacy with a more flexible prior. The last section uses it once.*

A single conjugate prior can be too rigid: "the coin is probably fair, but it might be anything" is not a beta distribution. A mixture of conjugate priors, $p(\theta) = \sum_j w_j\, p(\theta \mid \alpha_j)$, is conjugate too. Bayes' formula for one component, rearranged, says $p(X \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \alpha_j) = p(X \mid \alpha_j)\, p(\theta \mid \alpha_j')$. Summing over the components,

$$
p(\theta \mid X) \propto \sum_j w_j\, p(X \mid \alpha_j)\, p(\theta \mid \alpha_j') ,
$$

so each component updates as before, and the weights are reweighted by each component's evidence and normalized to sum to 1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\theta \mid X) &= \sum_j w_j'\, p(\theta \mid \alpha_j'), \\
w_j' &= \frac{w_j\, p(X \mid \alpha_j)}{\sum_l w_l\, p(X \mid \alpha_l)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Take the prior $0.5\, \mathrm{Beta}(20, 20) + 0.5\, \mathrm{Beta}(1, 1)$ and 9 heads in 10 flips. The components have the evidences $B(29, 21)/B(20, 20)$ and $B(10, 2)/B(1, 1)$, and the weights become $0.156$ and $0.844$: the data have moved belief from "fair" to "anything". The posterior mean is

$$
0.156 \cdot \frac{29}{50} + 0.844 \cdot \frac{10}{12} = 0.794 .
$$

With 5 heads in 10, the weights are $0.707$ and $0.293$ instead. The weight update is already a comparison of two models by their evidence, the subject of the next note.

## Posterior predictive distributions

The predictive averages the likelihood over the posterior. For conjugate pairs it has a closed form, and it is wider than the plug-in prediction at the posterior mean, because it adds the uncertainty about the parameter to the noise of the data.

### The beta–binomial

The number of heads $k$ in $m$ future flips, under a $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ posterior, has probabilities

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(k \mid m) &= \int_0^1 \binom mk f^k (1-f)^{m-k}\, \mathrm{Beta}(f \mid a, b)\, df \\
&= \binom mk \frac{B(a + k,\ b + m - k)}{B(a, b)} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

the **beta–binomial distribution**. The first line is the predictive integral, with flips independent given $f$; the second collects the powers of $f$ and $1 - f$ and uses the definition of $B$. Its mean, $m a/(a + b)$, is that of a binomial with the posterior mean plugged in. Its variance is larger by the factor $(a + b + m)/(a + b + 1)$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}[k] = m \frac{a}{a+b} \frac{b}{a+b} \cdot \frac{a + b + m}{a + b + 1} .
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the mean and variance</summary>

Write $\bar f = a/(a + b)$. Ratios of beta constants give the moments of $f$: $\mathbb E[f] = B(a + 1, b)/B(a, b) = \bar f$ and $\mathbb E[f^2] = B(a + 2, b)/B(a, b) = \bar f\, (a + 1)/(a + b + 1)$, so $\operatorname{Var}[f] = \bar f (1 - \bar f)/(a + b + 1)$. Given $f$, $k$ is binomial with mean $mf$ and variance $mf(1 - f)$. The [tower property](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation#the-tower-property) gives $\mathbb E[k] = m \bar f$, and the [law of total variance](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation#the-law-of-total-variance) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}[k]
&= \mathbb E\big[m f (1 - f)\big] + \operatorname{Var}[m f] \\
&= m \bar f (1 - \bar f) \frac{a + b}{a + b + 1} + \frac{m^2 \bar f (1 - \bar f)}{a + b + 1} \\
&= m \bar f (1 - \bar f)\, \frac{a + b + m}{a + b + 1} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the second line uses $\mathbb E[f(1 - f)] = \bar f - \operatorname{Var}[f] - \bar f^2$.

</details>

After the seven flips, $\mathrm{Beta}(7, 4)$, the predictive for $m = 10$ more flips has variance $10 \cdot \frac{7}{11} \cdot \frac{4}{11} \cdot \frac{21}{12} = 4.05$, against $2.31$ for the binomial with $p = 7/11$. The probability of 3 heads or fewer is $0.089$ under the predictive and $0.032$ under the plug-in.

### The Student-t

Under the normal–gamma posterior, a new observation is $x_* = \mu + \varepsilon$. Given $\lambda$, the mean has variance $1/(\kappa_n \lambda)$ and the independent noise $1/\lambda$, so by the sum of independent Gaussians $x_* \mid \lambda \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_n, (1 + 1/\kappa_n)/\lambda\big)$, a normal with precision $\tau \lambda$, $\tau = \kappa_n/(\kappa_n + 1)$. Integrating out $\lambda$ as for the marginal of $\mu$,

$$
p(x_* \mid X) = \mathrm{St}\Big(x_* \;\Big|\; \mu_n,\ \frac{b_n (\kappa_n + 1)}{a_n \kappa_n},\ 2a_n\Big) .
$$

In the example of five points this is a Student-t with 7 degrees of freedom, centre $1.0$ and squared scale $2.45 \cdot 7/21 = 0.817$. Plugging in the posterior means, $\mu = 1.0$ and $\lambda = a_n/b_n$, gives $\mathcal N(1.0, 0.7)$ instead:

| | Predictive Student-t | Plug-in $\mathcal N(1.0, 0.7)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| central 95% interval | $[-1.14, 3.14]$ | $[-0.64, 2.64]$ |
| $P(x_* > 3)$ | $0.031$ | $0.0084$ |

The predictive gives almost four times the probability to an observation above 3, because it keeps the possibility that the noise is larger than its estimate. Plugging in the maximum likelihood fit, mean $1.2$ and variance $S/n = 0.34$, would give $0.001$.

## Where it breaks: a prior with light tails

A conjugate prior is chosen for its algebra, and its shape comes with the algebra. The normal prior has light tails, and they decide what happens when the data disagree with it. The example follows [Murphy (2023](#references), Section 3.4.6.2), after Berger.

One observation $x = 5$ comes from $\mathcal N(\theta, 1)$. The prior belief is that $\theta$ has median 0 and quartiles $-1$ and $1$. The normal prior with these quartiles has standard deviation $1/0.674 = 1.48$, variance $2.20$. The Cauchy prior, a Student-t with one degree of freedom, has the same quartiles but heavy tails. Both state the same belief about the middle of the distribution, and they give different answers:

| Prior | Posterior mean of $\theta$ |
| --- | --- |
| normal, variance $2.20$ | $3.44$ |
| Cauchy | $4.56$ |
| $0.8 \cdot$ normal, variance $2.20$, $+\ 0.2 \cdot \mathcal N(0, 100)$ | $4.44$ |

The normal posterior is the precision-weighted average, $5 \cdot 2.20/3.20 = 3.44$. It sits where neither the prior nor the likelihood puts much belief: the likelihood says $\theta$ is within about 2 of 5, and the prior gives $\theta > 3.4$ a probability of 1%. Under a normal prior, a datum is always pulled towards the prior mean by the same fraction, however surprising it is. A heavy-tailed prior gives up when the data are far away; the Cauchy posterior stays near the observation. The posterior mode shows why. It solves $(x - \theta) + \frac{d}{d\theta} \log p(\theta) = 0$, so the prior pulls the estimate back by the slope of its log-density. For the normal prior the slope $-\theta/2.20$ grows with $\theta$, and the pull is $1.56$. For the Cauchy, the slope $-2\theta/(1 + \theta^2)$ fades for large $\theta$: the mode is $4.58$, a pull of only $0.42$.

The Cauchy prior is not conjugate, and its posterior needs numerical integration. A mixture of conjugate priors is a conjugate compromise: a broad component with a small weight absorbs a surprising observation. In the third row, each component's evidence is the linear-Gaussian marginal $\mathcal N(5 \mid 0, v + 1)$ with $v = 2.20$ or $100$; reweighting by them moves the weight of the broad component from $0.2$ to $0.66$, and the posterior mean to $4.44$.

Two other limits follow from the same algebra. Models built from blocks rarely have a usable conjugate prior as a whole: for a logistic regression, a mixture or a neural network, no prior family with a known normalizer stays closed under the update. Conjugacy then survives inside the blocks, conditional on the other variables, which is what [variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference) and [Gibbs sampling](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling) exploit. And the normal–gamma prior ties the uncertainty of the mean to the noise variance. When prior knowledge about the mean is independent of the noise level, the conjugate prior cannot express it.

## What you should remember

- Conjugate: the posterior stays in the prior's family, and learning updates a few hyperparameters, in any order and batching.
- Beta–Bernoulli: $a + k$, $b + n - k$; the evidence $B(a + k, b + n - k)/B(a, b)$ is the product of the one-step predictives. Dirichlet: $(\alpha_k + n_k)/(\alpha_0 + n)$ is add-$\alpha$ smoothing.
- Unknown mean and precision need the normal–gamma prior, which ties them; the mean and the predictive are Student-t.
- Exponential family: $h(x) \exp(\eta^\top u(x) - A(\eta))$, with $\nabla A = \mathbb E[u]$ and $\nabla^2 A = \operatorname{Cov}[u]$; maximum likelihood matches the sample means of $u$.
- Its conjugate prior is $\propto \exp(\eta^\top \chi - \nu A(\eta))$: add $\sum_i u(x_i)$ to $\chi$ and $n$ to $\nu$.
- Predictives are wider than plug-ins. A normal prior shrinks every datum by the same fraction, however far it lies.

## Code

Sequential updates checked against the batch formulas, the evidence as a product of predictives, the Dirichlet example, the normal–gamma posterior against the grid and its predictive against Monte Carlo, the derivatives of the log-partition function, the beta–binomial predictive, a mixture prior, and the normal against the Cauchy prior. Seeds are fixed; the script runs in about a second, and exact numbers can differ in the last digit across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, special, integrate, optimize

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
lbeta = special.betaln

# beta-Bernoulli: one flip at a time gives the batch posterior, and the product of the
# one-step predictives is the evidence B(a + k, b + n - k) / B(a, b)
x = np.array([1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1])                    # 5 heads in 7 flips
a, b, log_ev = 2.0, 2.0, 0.0                           # prior Beta(2, 2)
for xi in x:
    p_head = a / (a + b)                               # predictive of the next flip
    log_ev += np.log(p_head if xi else 1 - p_head)
    a, b = a + xi, b + 1 - xi                          # conjugate update
print(a, b, round(a / (a + b), 3))                     # 7.0 4.0 0.636
print(round(np.exp(log_ev), 6), round(np.exp(lbeta(7, 4) - lbeta(2, 2)), 6))   # 0.007143 0.007143: 1/140 both ways

# Dirichlet-categorical: counts (5, 2, 0, 1) with a uniform Dir(1, 1, 1, 1) prior
alpha, counts = np.ones(4), np.array([5, 2, 0, 1])
post = alpha + counts
print((post / post.sum()).round(3))                    # [0.5   0.25  0.083 0.167]: the unseen category keeps 1/12
log_ev = (special.gammaln(alpha.sum()) - special.gammaln(post.sum())
          + (special.gammaln(post) - special.gammaln(alpha)).sum())
print(round(1 / np.exp(log_ev)))                       # 27720: the evidence of the sequence is 1/27720

# normal-gamma: unknown mean and precision, prior mu | lam ~ N(m0, 1/(k0 lam)), lam ~ Gamma(a0, rate b0)
def ng_update(m, k, a, b, xs):
    n, xbar = len(xs), np.mean(xs)
    S = ((xs - xbar) ** 2).sum()
    return ((k * m + n * xbar) / (k + n), k + n, a + n / 2,
            b + S / 2 + k * n * (xbar - m) ** 2 / (2 * (k + n)))

xs = np.array([1.2, 0.4, 2.1, 1.5, 0.8])
batch = ng_update(0.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, xs)
seq = (0.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0)
for xi in xs:
    seq = ng_update(*seq, np.array([xi]))
print(np.round(batch, 4), np.allclose(batch, seq))     # [1.   6.   3.5  2.45] True

# check against brute force: the posterior on a grid of (mu, lam), marginalized over lam
mn, kn, an, bn = batch
mu, lam = np.linspace(-2, 4, 1201), np.linspace(1e-4, 8, 1600)
M, L = np.meshgrid(mu, lam, indexing='ij')
log_joint = (stats.norm.logpdf(M, 0, 1 / np.sqrt(L)) + stats.gamma.logpdf(L, 1, scale=1)
             + stats.norm.logpdf(xs[:, None, None], M, 1 / np.sqrt(L)).sum(axis=0))
grid = np.exp(log_joint - log_joint.max()).sum(axis=1)
grid /= grid.sum() * (mu[1] - mu[0])                   # the marginal posterior density of mu on the grid
exact = stats.t(2 * an, loc=mn, scale=np.sqrt(bn / (an * kn))).pdf(mu)
print(round(float(np.abs(grid - exact).max()), 4))     # 0.0001: the closed-form Student-t marginal matches

# the posterior predictive is a Student-t; a Monte Carlo check by sampling lam, mu, then x
scale2 = bn * (kn + 1) / (an * kn)                     # the predictive scale squared
pred = stats.t(2 * an, loc=mn, scale=np.sqrt(scale2))
lam_s = rng.gamma(an, 1 / bn, size=400_000)
x_s = rng.normal(rng.normal(mn, 1 / np.sqrt(kn * lam_s)), 1 / np.sqrt(lam_s))
print(round(scale2, 4), round(pred.sf(3), 4), round((x_s > 3).mean(), 4))   # 0.8167 0.0313 0.0312
plug = stats.norm(mn, np.sqrt(bn / an))                # plug in the posterior means of mu and of lam
print(pred.ppf([0.025, 0.975]).round(2), plug.ppf([0.025, 0.975]).round(2), round(plug.sf(3), 4))
# [-1.14  3.14] [-0.64  2.64] 0.0084
print(round(stats.norm(1.2, np.sqrt(0.34)).sf(3), 4))  # 0.001: the plug-in maximum likelihood fit, variance S/n

# the log-partition function: its gradient is E[u(x)], its second derivative Var[u(x)]
A = lambda eta: np.log1p(np.exp(eta))                  # Bernoulli, eta = log(p / (1 - p))
eta, h = np.log(0.3 / 0.7), 1e-4
print(round((A(eta + h) - A(eta - h)) / (2 * h), 4), round((A(eta + h) - 2 * A(eta) + A(eta - h)) / h ** 2, 4))   # 0.3 0.21

# beta-binomial predictive for the next 10 flips after Beta(7, 4), against the plug-in binomial
bb, bi = stats.betabinom(10, 7, 4), stats.binom(10, 7 / 11)
print(round(bb.var(), 3), round(bi.var(), 3), round(bb.cdf(3), 3), round(bi.cdf(3), 3))   # 4.05 2.314 0.089 0.032

# a mixture of conjugate priors is conjugate: 0.5 Beta(20, 20) + 0.5 Beta(1, 1), then 9 heads in 10
w = np.array([0.5 * np.exp(lbeta(29, 21) - lbeta(20, 20)), 0.5 * np.exp(lbeta(10, 2) - lbeta(1, 1))])
w /= w.sum()
print(w.round(3), round(w @ [29 / 50, 10 / 12], 3))     # [0.156 0.844] 0.794
w5 = np.array([np.exp(lbeta(25, 25) - lbeta(20, 20)), np.exp(lbeta(6, 6) - lbeta(1, 1))])
print((w5 / w5.sum()).round(3))                        # [0.707 0.293]: with 5 heads in 10, "fair" gains

# a light-tailed prior: x = 5 from N(theta, 1), priors with median 0 and quartiles -1 and 1
x, v = 5.0, (1 / stats.norm.ppf(0.75)) ** 2            # the normal prior's variance, 2.198
lik = lambda th: stats.norm.pdf(x, th, 1)
Z = integrate.quad(lambda th: stats.cauchy.pdf(th) * lik(th), -60, 60, points=[0, 5])[0]
m_cauchy = integrate.quad(lambda th: th * stats.cauchy.pdf(th) * lik(th), -60, 60, points=[0, 5])[0] / Z
ev = np.array([0.8 * stats.norm.pdf(x, 0, np.sqrt(v + 1)), 0.2 * stats.norm.pdf(x, 0, np.sqrt(101))])
w = ev / ev.sum()                                      # posterior weights of the two normal components
print(round(v, 2), round(v / (v + 1) * x, 2), round(m_cauchy, 2), w.round(2), round(w @ [v / (v + 1) * x, 100 / 101 * x], 2))
# 2.2 3.44 4.56 [0.34 0.66] 4.44
mode = lambda logprior: optimize.minimize_scalar(lambda th: -(stats.norm.logpdf(x, th, 1) + logprior(th)),
                                                 bounds=(0, 6), method='bounded').x
print(round(stats.norm(0, np.sqrt(v)).sf(3.4), 2), round(mode(stats.cauchy.logpdf), 2))   # 0.01 4.58
```
