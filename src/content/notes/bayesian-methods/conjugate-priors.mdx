The [previous note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-approach) answered every question by a sum: over eleven urns, or over the sixteen states of a burglar alarm. With a continuous parameter the sum becomes an integral, and the normalizing integral of Bayes' formula, the evidence, usually has no closed form. This note collects the cases in which it does. A prior is **conjugate** to a likelihood when the posterior belongs to the same family as the prior; learning then reduces to updating a few numbers, and the evidence and the predictive distribution come out as ratios of normalizing constants. The standard pairs are worked through first. Then the **exponential family** explains why they exist: for every distribution of that family, the data enter the likelihood only through a fixed number of sums, and a prior shaped like the likelihood of imaginary data is conjugate.

**Notation.** $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ has density $\propto f^{a-1}(1-f)^{b-1}$ on $[0, 1]$, and $B(a, b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$ is its normalizing constant. $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_K)$ is the Dirichlet distribution on probability vectors $\pi$, with density $\propto \prod_k \pi_k^{\alpha_k - 1}$ and $\alpha_0 = \sum_k \alpha_k$. $\mathrm{Gamma}(a, b)$ has shape $a$ and rate $b$: density $\frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)} \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda}$, mean $a/b$. $\mathcal N(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2)$ takes the variance as its second argument, and $\lambda = 1/\sigma^2$ is a **precision**. $\mathrm{St}(x \mid \mu, s^2, \nu)$ is the Student-t distribution with location $\mu$, squared scale $s^2$ and $\nu$ degrees of freedom. A primed hyperparameter, $a'$, is the value after the update.

## Baseline: the posterior on a grid

Without a formula, the posterior can always be computed numerically. Evaluate likelihood × prior at $G$ points of the parameter, divide by the sum, and every integral becomes a weighted sum over the grid. The urns of the previous note were such a grid with $G = 11$, and for one parameter this is a fine method.

The cost is $G^d$ evaluations for $d$ parameters. The Code section computes the posterior of a normal mean and precision on a $1201 \times 1600$ grid: about two million evaluations of the likelihood for two parameters. With ten parameters and a coarse 100 points each, the grid has $100^{10} = 10^{20}$ points, and a linear regression with ten features is a small model. Exact Bayesian inference needs integrals that are done on paper, and conjugate priors are the cases in which paper suffices.

## Conjugacy: the posterior stays in the family

A family of priors $\mathcal F = \{p(\theta \mid \alpha)\}$ indexed by **hyperparameters** $\alpha$ is **conjugate** to a likelihood $p(x \mid \theta)$ if for every prior in the family and every dataset the posterior is again in the family:

$$
p(\theta \mid X, \alpha) \propto p(X \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid \alpha) = \text{const} \cdot p(\theta \mid \alpha') .
$$

Learning is then a map $\alpha \to \alpha'$ on a few numbers. Because the posterior is again a prior of the family, the map can be applied one observation at a time, and by the [sequential updating argument](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-approach#sequential-updating) the result does not depend on the order or the batching of the data.

Two more quantities come free. The evidence follows from the normalizing constants: if $p(\theta \mid \alpha) = \tilde p(\theta \mid \alpha) / Z(\alpha)$ with $\tilde p$ unnormalized, and the likelihood times $\tilde p(\theta \mid \alpha)$ equals $c(X)\, \tilde p(\theta \mid \alpha')$ for a factor $c(X)$ that does not depend on $\theta$, then integrating over $\theta$,

$$
p(X \mid \alpha) = \int p(X \mid \theta)\, \frac{\tilde p(\theta \mid \alpha)}{Z(\alpha)}\, d\theta = c(X)\, \frac{Z(\alpha')}{Z(\alpha)} .
$$

The predictive of a new point is the evidence of one more observation divided by the evidence of the data, $p(x_* \mid X, \alpha) = p(X, x_* \mid \alpha) / p(X \mid \alpha)$, again a ratio of normalizing constants.

## Beta–Bernoulli and the evidence \{#beta-bernoulli-and-the-evidence\}

For flips $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$ with probability of heads $f$ and a $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ prior, the posterior after $k$ heads in $n$ flips is $\mathrm{Beta}(a + k,\ b + n - k)$, as derived in the [Bayesian inference note](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#conjugate-priors-the-beta-binomial), which also reads $a$ and $b$ as pseudo-counts. The new ingredient is the evidence. The likelihood of the sequence is $f^k (1-f)^{n-k}$, so $c(X) = 1$ and

$$
p(X \mid a, b) = \int_0^1 f^k (1-f)^{n-k}\, \frac{f^{a-1}(1-f)^{b-1}}{B(a, b)}\, df = \frac{B(a + k,\ b + n - k)}{B(a, b)} .
$$

**Worked example.** The seven flips $1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1$ have $k = 5$ heads. Under a $\mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)$ prior the posterior is $\mathrm{Beta}(7, 4)$, the probability of heads on the next flip is $7/11 = 0.636$, and the evidence of the sequence is

$$
\frac{B(7, 4)}{B(2, 2)} = \frac{6!\, 3! / 10!}{1!\, 1! / 3!} = \frac{1/840}{1/6} = \frac{1}{140} = 0.00714 .
$$

The same number is the product of the seven one-step predictives, $\frac{2}{4} \cdot \frac{2}{5} \cdot \frac{3}{6} \cdot \frac{4}{7} \cdot \frac{3}{8} \cdot \frac{5}{9} \cdot \frac{6}{10} = \frac{1}{140}$: by the product rule, $p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})$, the evidence is the probability with which the model predicted each observation from the ones before it. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection) builds model comparison on this reading.

## Dirichlet–categorical \{#dirichlet-categorical\}

A categorical variable takes one of $K$ values with probabilities $\pi = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_K)$. With counts $n_1, \dots, n_K$ in $n$ observations, the likelihood is $\prod_k \pi_k^{n_k}$, and a $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$ prior gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\pi \mid X) &\propto \prod_{k=1}^K \pi_k^{n_k} \cdot \prod_{k=1}^K \pi_k^{\alpha_k - 1} = \prod_{k=1}^K \pi_k^{\alpha_k + n_k - 1}, \\
\pi \mid X &\sim \mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_1 + n_1, \dots, \alpha_K + n_K) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The Dirichlet is the beta distribution for $K$ categories. Its normalizing constant is $\prod_k \Gamma(\alpha_k) / \Gamma(\alpha_0)$, and the posterior mean, which is also the predictive probability of category $k$, and the evidence of the sequence are

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(x_* = k \mid X) &= \frac{\alpha_k + n_k}{\alpha_0 + n}, \\
p(X \mid \alpha) &= \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\Gamma(\alpha_0 + n)} \prod_{k=1}^K \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_k + n_k)}{\Gamma(\alpha_k)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example.** Four categories, a uniform prior $\mathrm{Dir}(1, 1, 1, 1)$ and counts $(5, 2, 0, 1)$. The posterior is $\mathrm{Dir}(6, 3, 1, 2)$, and the predictive probabilities are $6/12, 3/12, 1/12, 2/12 = 0.5, 0.25, 0.083, 0.167$. The category never seen keeps probability $1/12$ instead of the maximum likelihood value 0. The evidence is $\frac{3!}{11!} \cdot 5!\, 2!\, 0!\, 1! = 1/27720$. Adding $\alpha$ to every count is the add-$\alpha$ [smoothing of n-gram language models](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models#smoothing), seen as a posterior mean. The total $\alpha_0$ is the strength of the prior in pseudo-counts; values $\alpha_k < 1$ put the prior mass near the corners of the simplex and favour sparse probability vectors, which is how [latent Dirichlet allocation](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation) makes each document use few topics.

## Normal–normal: an unknown mean \{#normal-normal-an-unknown-mean\}

For observations from $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with known $\sigma^2$ and a prior $\mathcal N(\mu_0, \tau^2)$, the [Bayesian inference note](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#normal-data-and-shrinkage) derived the posterior $\mathcal N(\mu_n, \sigma_n^2)$ with

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\sigma_n^2} &= \frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \\
\frac{\mu_n}{\sigma_n^2} &= \frac{\mu_0}{\tau^2} + \frac{\sum_i x_i}{\sigma^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Written this way, both updates are additions: the precision grows by $1/\sigma^2$ per observation, and precision × mean grows by $x_i/\sigma^2$. The data enter only through $n$ and $\sum_i x_i$, a first example of the sufficient statistics below. The predictive of a new point $x_* = \mu + \varepsilon$ adds the noise to the posterior uncertainty of the mean: the sum of two independent Gaussians is Gaussian with the variances added,

$$
p(x_* \mid X) = \mathcal N(x_* \mid \mu_n,\ \sigma_n^2 + \sigma^2) .
$$

**Worked example.** Prior $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, noise variance $\sigma^2 = 4$, four observations with mean $1.5$. The posterior precision is $1 + 4/4 = 2$, so $\sigma_n^2 = 0.5$ and $\mu_n = 0.5 \cdot (0 + 6/4) = 0.75$; the predictive is $\mathcal N(0.75, 4.5)$. For vectors, the same update with precision matrices is the [conditional of a joint Gaussian](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals), and [Bayesian linear regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression) is this update for the weights of a linear model.

## Normal–gamma: unknown mean and precision \{#normal-gamma-unknown-mean-and-precision\}

When the noise level is unknown too, both $\mu$ and the precision $\lambda = 1/\sigma^2$ need a prior. The likelihood of $n$ observations is

$$
p(X \mid \mu, \lambda) = \Big(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\Big)^{n/2} \exp\Big(-\frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2\Big) .
$$

The scale of $\mu$ and the precision are tied together: the more precise the data, the more precisely the mean is pinned down. The conjugate prior builds this in. It is the **normal–gamma** distribution,

$$
p(\mu, \lambda) = \mathcal N\big(\mu \mid \mu_0, (\kappa_0 \lambda)^{-1}\big)\, \mathrm{Gamma}(\lambda \mid a_0, b_0) ,
$$

in which, given $\lambda$, the mean has the precision of $\kappa_0$ observations. With $\bar x$ the sample mean and $S = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2$, two identities do the update. The sum of squares splits as $\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 = S + n(\bar x - \mu)^2$, and completing the square in $\mu$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2 + n(\mu - \bar x)^2 &= \kappa_n (\mu - \mu_n)^2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n}{\kappa_n} (\bar x - \mu_0)^2, \\
\kappa_n &= \kappa_0 + n,\quad \mu_n = \frac{\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n \bar x}{\kappa_n} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying prior and likelihood, the terms in $\mu$ form a normal density with precision $\kappa_n \lambda$, which uses one factor $\lambda^{1/2}$; the remaining powers of $\lambda$ and the terms free of $\mu$ form a gamma density. The posterior is normal–gamma with

$$
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_n &= \kappa_0 + n, \\
\mu_n &= \frac{\kappa_0 \mu_0 + n \bar x}{\kappa_n}, \qquad a_n = a_0 + \frac n2, \\
b_n &= b_0 + \frac S2 + \frac{\kappa_0 n (\bar x - \mu_0)^2}{2 \kappa_n} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The rate $b_n$ collects three sources of squared deviation: the prior's, the spread of the data around their mean, and the disagreement between the data mean and the prior mean. Writing $\sigma^2 = 1/\lambda$ instead gives the same model as the normal–inverse-gamma prior.

The marginal posterior of the mean integrates out the precision. For a normal with precision $\tau\lambda$ and a $\mathrm{Gamma}(a, b)$ precision,

$$
\int_0^\infty \lambda^{1/2} e^{-\tau\lambda (\mu - m)^2 / 2}\, \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda}\, d\lambda \;\propto\; \Big(b + \frac{\tau (\mu - m)^2}{2}\Big)^{-(a + 1/2)} \;\propto\; \Big(1 + \frac{(\mu - m)^2}{2a \cdot b/(a\tau)}\Big)^{-(2a+1)/2} ,
$$

using $\int_0^\infty \lambda^{c-1} e^{-d\lambda}\, d\lambda = \Gamma(c)/d^c$. This is the Student-t density with $2a$ degrees of freedom and squared scale $b/(a\tau)$. So

$$
p(\mu \mid X) = \mathrm{St}\Big(\mu \;\Big|\; \mu_n,\ \frac{b_n}{a_n \kappa_n},\ 2a_n\Big) .
$$

**Worked example.** Prior $\mu_0 = 0$, $\kappa_0 = 1$, $a_0 = b_0 = 1$; data $1.2, 0.4, 2.1, 1.5, 0.8$, so $n = 5$, $\bar x = 1.2$ and $S = 1.70$. The update gives $\kappa_n = 6$, $\mu_n = 6/6 = 1.0$, $a_n = 3.5$ and

$$
b_n = 1 + \frac{1.70}{2} + \frac{1 \cdot 5 \cdot 1.2^2}{2 \cdot 6} = 1 + 0.85 + 0.6 = 2.45 .
$$

The mean has a Student-t posterior with 7 degrees of freedom, centre $1.0$ and squared scale $2.45/21 = 0.117$. The Code section checks this marginal against the two-million-point grid of the baseline: the two densities agree to $10^{-4}$.

## The exponential family

Every pair so far had the same structure: the likelihood depended on the data through a few sums ($k$ and $n$; the counts $n_k$; $\sum x_i$, $\sum x_i^2$ and $n$), and the prior had the form of that likelihood. The exponential family is the class of distributions for which this always happens.

### Natural parameters and sufficient statistics

A distribution belongs to the **exponential family** if its density, or probability mass function, can be written as

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(x \mid \eta) &= h(x) \exp\big(\eta^\top u(x) - A(\eta)\big), \\
A(\eta) &= \log \int h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top u(x)}\, dx ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with **natural parameters** $\eta$, **sufficient statistics** $u(x)$, a base measure $h(x)$, and the **log-partition function** $A(\eta)$, which makes the density integrate to 1. The usual parameters are a reparametrization of $\eta$:

| Distribution | $\eta$ | $u(x)$ | $A(\eta)$ | $h(x)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bernoulli$(p)$ | $\log \frac{p}{1-p}$ | $x$ | $\log(1 + e^\eta)$ | $1$ |
| Poisson$(\lambda)$ | $\log \lambda$ | $x$ | $e^\eta$ | $1/x!$ |
| $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $\big(\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}, -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\big)$ | $(x, x^2)$ | $-\frac{\eta_1^2}{4\eta_2} - \frac12 \log(-2\eta_2)$ | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ |
| Categorical$(\pi)$ | $\log \frac{\pi_k}{\pi_K}$, $k < K$ | one-hot, first $K - 1$ entries | $\log\big(1 + \sum_{k<K} e^{\eta_k}\big)$ | $1$ |
| $\mathrm{Gamma}(a, b)$ | $(a - 1, -b)$ | $(\log x, x)$ | $\log\Gamma(a) - a \log b$ | $1$ |

For the Bernoulli, $p^x (1-p)^{1-x} = \exp\big(x \log\frac{p}{1-p} + \log(1 - p)\big)$ and $-\log(1-p) = \log(1 + e^\eta)$. For the normal, expanding $-(x - \mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ gives the terms $x \mu/\sigma^2$ and $-x^2/(2\sigma^2)$, and the rest, $\mu^2/(2\sigma^2) + \log\sigma$, is $A$ written in $\eta$. The beta, Dirichlet, multivariate normal and Wishart distributions belong to the family too. The Student-t distribution, mixtures, and the uniform distribution on $[0, \theta]$ with unknown $\theta$ do not.

For i.i.d. data the log-likelihood is

$$
\log p(X \mid \eta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log h(x_i) + \eta^\top \sum_{i=1}^n u(x_i) - n A(\eta) .
$$

The data enter only through $\sum_i u(x_i)$ and $n$, a vector whose size does not grow with $n$. These are the sufficient statistics: two datasets with the same sums have proportional likelihoods and therefore, under any prior, the same posterior.

### Derivatives of the log-partition function

Differentiating the normalization $e^{A(\eta)} = \int h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top u(x)}\, dx$ under the integral sign gives the moments of $u(x)$. The first derivative:

$$
\nabla A(\eta)\, e^{A(\eta)} = \int u(x)\, h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top u(x)}\, dx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla A(\eta) = \int u(x)\, p(x \mid \eta)\, dx = \mathbb E[u(x)] .
$$

Differentiating $\nabla A(\eta) = \int u(x)\, h(x)\, e^{\eta^\top u(x) - A(\eta)}\, dx$ once more, the exponent contributes $u(x) - \nabla A(\eta)$:

$$
\nabla^2 A(\eta) = \int u(x) \big(u(x) - \nabla A(\eta)\big)^\top p(x \mid \eta)\, dx = \mathbb E[u u^\top] - \mathbb E[u]\, \mathbb E[u]^\top = \operatorname{Cov}[u(x)] .
$$

A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite, so $A$ is convex, by the [second-order condition](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-second-order-condition-a-positive-semidefinite-hessian) for convexity. Three consequences follow. Moments come from derivatives: for the Bernoulli, $A'(\eta) = e^\eta/(1 + e^\eta) = p$ and $A''(\eta) = p(1 - p)$; for the normal, $\partial A/\partial \eta_1 = -\eta_1/(2\eta_2) = \mu$ and $\partial A/\partial\eta_2 = \mu^2 + \sigma^2 = \mathbb E[x^2]$. The log-likelihood above is concave in $\eta$, strictly so when no linear combination of the statistics $u(x)$ is constant, and then it has at most one maximum. And setting its gradient to zero gives the maximum likelihood equation

$$
\nabla A(\hat\eta) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n u(x_i) ,
$$

**moment matching**: the fitted model reproduces the sample averages of the sufficient statistics. The Gaussian fit by sample mean and sample second moment, and the [logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression) gradient, predicted minus observed, are instances.

## Why the exponential family has conjugate priors

Look at the likelihood as a function of $\eta$: it is $\exp\big(\eta^\top \sum_i u(x_i) - n A(\eta)\big)$ times a factor free of $\eta$. A prior of the same shape,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\eta \mid \chi, \nu) &= \frac{1}{Z(\chi, \nu)} \exp\big(\eta^\top \chi - \nu A(\eta)\big), \\
Z(\chi, \nu) &= \int \exp\big(\eta^\top \chi - \nu A(\eta)\big)\, d\eta ,
\end{aligned}
$$

multiplies with it into the same shape again:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\eta \mid X) &\propto \exp\Big(\eta^\top \Big(\chi + \sum_{i=1}^n u(x_i)\Big) - (\nu + n) A(\eta)\Big), \\
\chi' &= \chi + \sum_{i=1}^n u(x_i), \quad \nu' = \nu + n .
\end{aligned}
$$

The prior is the likelihood of $\nu$ imaginary observations whose sufficient statistics sum to $\chi$, and the update adds the real ones ([Diaconis and Ylvisaker, 1979](#references)). The general formulas of the conjugacy section become, with $c(X) = \prod_i h(x_i)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(X \mid \chi, \nu) &= \prod_{i=1}^n h(x_i) \cdot \frac{Z(\chi', \nu')}{Z(\chi, \nu)}, \\
p(x_* \mid X) &= h(x_*)\, \frac{Z\big(\chi' + u(x_*),\ \nu' + 1\big)}{Z(\chi', \nu')} .
\end{aligned}
$$

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> For a likelihood in the exponential family, the conjugate prior is a likelihood of imaginary data: $\nu$ pseudo-observations with summed statistics $\chi$. Bayesian learning adds the real sufficient statistics to these, and the evidence and the predictive are ratios of the prior's normalizing constant before and after.</aside>

**The beta prior as an instance.** For the Bernoulli, $A(\eta) = \log(1 + e^\eta)$ with $e^\eta = p/(1-p)$, so $e^{\eta\chi} = \big(\frac{p}{1-p}\big)^\chi$ and $e^{-\nu A(\eta)} = (1 - p)^\nu$, and the prior density in $\eta$ is proportional to $p^\chi (1-p)^{\nu - \chi}$. As a density in $p$ it gains the Jacobian $d\eta/dp = 1/\big(p(1-p)\big)$:

$$
p(p \mid \chi, \nu) \propto p^{\chi - 1} (1 - p)^{\nu - \chi - 1} = \mathrm{Beta}(p \mid \chi,\ \nu - \chi) .
$$

So $a = \chi$ and $b = \nu - \chi$, and the update $\chi' = \chi + k$, $\nu' = \nu + n$ is $a' = a + k$, $b' = b + n - k$. The Jacobian is why the beta exponents are $a - 1$ rather than $a$, the offset between the two pseudo-count conventions of the [Bayesian inference note](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#conjugate-priors-the-beta-binomial).

1. **Sufficient statistics.** The seven flips reduce to $\sum_i x_i = 5$ and $n = 7$.
2. **The prior as pseudo-observations.** $\mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)$ is $\chi = 2$, $\nu = 4$: four imaginary flips, two of them heads.
3. **Add.** $\chi' = 7$, $\nu' = 11$, the posterior $\mathrm{Beta}(7, 4)$.
4. **Read off.** The predictive probability of heads, $\chi'/\nu' = 7/11 = 0.636$, and the evidence $B(7, 4)/B(2, 2) = 1/140$.

Conjugacy is not limited to the exponential family. The uniform distribution on $[0, \theta]$ has the Pareto distribution as a conjugate prior, although its support depends on the parameter. But for families with a fixed support, the exponential family is where sufficient statistics of fixed size, and with them conjugate priors in this simple form, are found.

### Mixtures of conjugate priors

*Optional depth: how to keep conjugacy with a more flexible prior. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

A single conjugate prior can be too rigid: "the coin is probably fair, but it might be anything" is not a beta distribution. A mixture of conjugate priors, $p(\theta) = \sum_j w_j\, p(\theta \mid \alpha_j)$, is conjugate too. Each component updates as before, and the weights are re-weighted by each component's evidence:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\theta \mid X) &= \sum_j w_j'\, p(\theta \mid \alpha_j'), \\
w_j' &= \frac{w_j\, p(X \mid \alpha_j)}{\sum_l w_l\, p(X \mid \alpha_l)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

With the prior $0.5\, \mathrm{Beta}(20, 20) + 0.5\, \mathrm{Beta}(1, 1)$, 9 heads in 10 flips give the components the evidences $B(29, 21)/B(20, 20)$ and $B(10, 2)/B(1, 1)$, and the weights become $0.156$ and $0.844$: the data have moved belief from "fair" to "anything". The posterior mean is $0.156 \cdot 29/50 + 0.844 \cdot 10/12 = 0.794$. With 5 heads in 10, the weights are $0.707$ and $0.293$ instead. The weight update is already a comparison of two models by their evidence, the subject of the next note.

## Posterior predictive distributions

The predictive averages the likelihood over the posterior. For conjugate pairs it has a closed form, and it is always wider than the likelihood at any single parameter value, because it adds the uncertainty about the parameter to the noise of the data.

**Beta–binomial.** The number of heads $k$ in $m$ future flips, under a $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ posterior, has probabilities

$$
P(k \mid m) = \int_0^1 \binom mk f^k (1-f)^{m-k}\, \mathrm{Beta}(f \mid a, b)\, df = \binom mk \frac{B(a + k,\ b + m - k)}{B(a, b)} ,
$$

the **beta–binomial distribution**. Its mean, $m a/(a + b)$, is that of a binomial with the posterior mean plugged in, and its variance is larger by the factor $(a + b + m)/(a + b + 1)$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}[k] = m \frac{a}{a+b} \frac{b}{a+b} \cdot \frac{a + b + m}{a + b + 1} .
$$

After the seven flips of the worked example, $\mathrm{Beta}(7, 4)$, the predictive for $m = 10$ more flips has variance $10 \cdot \frac{7}{11} \cdot \frac{4}{11} \cdot \frac{21}{12} = 4.05$, against $2.31$ for the binomial with $p = 7/11$. The probability of 3 heads or fewer is $0.089$ under the predictive and $0.032$ under the plug-in.

**Student-t.** Under the normal–gamma posterior, a new observation is $x_* = \mu + \varepsilon$. Given $\lambda$, the mean has variance $1/(\kappa_n \lambda)$ and the noise $1/\lambda$, so $x_* \mid \lambda \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_n, (1 + 1/\kappa_n)/\lambda\big)$, a normal with precision $\tau \lambda$, $\tau = \kappa_n/(\kappa_n + 1)$. Integrating out $\lambda$ as for the marginal of $\mu$,

$$
p(x_* \mid X) = \mathrm{St}\Big(x_* \;\Big|\; \mu_n,\ \frac{b_n (\kappa_n + 1)}{a_n \kappa_n},\ 2a_n\Big) .
$$

In the worked example this is a Student-t with 7 degrees of freedom, centre $1.0$ and squared scale $2.45 \cdot 7/21 = 0.817$. Plugging in the posterior means, $\mu = 1.0$ and $\lambda = a_n/b_n$, gives instead $\mathcal N(1.0, 0.7)$. The central 95% intervals are $[-1.14, 3.14]$ and $[-0.64, 2.64]$, and the probability of an observation above 3 is $0.031$ against $0.0084$: almost four times more, because the predictive keeps the possibility that the noise is larger than its estimate. Plugging in the maximum likelihood fit, mean $1.2$ and variance $S/n = 0.34$, would give $0.001$.

| Likelihood | Conjugate prior | Posterior hyperparameters | Predictive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bernoulli, binomial | $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ | $a + k,\ b + n - k$ | beta–binomial |
| Categorical, multinomial | $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$ | $\alpha_k + n_k$ | Dirichlet–multinomial |

## What changed

| | Posterior on a grid | Conjugate prior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Posterior | numbers at $G^d$ points | a few hyperparameters |
| Cost of an update | $G^d$ likelihood evaluations | add the sufficient statistics |
| Normal mean and precision, 5 points | about $2 \cdot 10^6$ evaluations | $\kappa_n = 6$, $\mu_n = 1$, $a_n = 3.5$, $b_n = 2.45$ |
| Evidence | a sum over the grid | a ratio of normalizing constants |
| Predictive | a sum over the grid | a closed form: beta–binomial, Student-t |
| Applies to | any model with few parameters | exponential-family likelihoods with their priors |

## Limitations

- **Few likelihoods qualify.** Conjugacy needs an exponential-family likelihood, and models built from blocks rarely have one as a whole: a logistic regression, a mixture or a neural network has no conjugate prior. Conjugacy then survives inside the blocks, conditional on the other variables, which is what [variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference) and [Gibbs sampling](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling) exploit.
- **The prior is chosen for convenience.** A conjugate prior has a fixed shape, with a tail and a number of modes that the family dictates. Mixtures of conjugate priors loosen this at the cost of components.
- **Pseudo-counts can mislead.** Reading the prior as $\nu$ imaginary observations is exact, but the strength it implies is easy to set too high: a $\mathrm{Beta}(20, 20)$ prior outweighs ten real flips by four to one.
- **Tied parameters.** In the normal–gamma prior, the uncertainty of the mean is proportional to the noise variance. When prior knowledge about the mean is independent of the noise level, the conjugate prior cannot express it, and the posterior loses its closed form.

## What you should remember

- A prior is conjugate if the posterior stays in its family; learning updates a few hyperparameters, in any order and batching.
- Beta–Bernoulli: $a + k$, $b + n - k$; evidence $B(a + k, b + n - k)/B(a, b)$, the product of the one-step predictives.
- Dirichlet–categorical: $\alpha_k + n_k$; the predictive $(\alpha_k + n_k)/(\alpha_0 + n)$ is add-$\alpha$ smoothing.
- Normal–gamma for unknown mean and precision: $\kappa_n = \kappa_0 + n$, $\mu_n$ a weighted mean, $a_n = a_0 + n/2$, $b_n = b_0 + S/2 + \kappa_0 n (\bar x - \mu_0)^2/(2\kappa_n)$; the mean and the predictive are Student-t.
- Exponential family: $p(x \mid \eta) = h(x) \exp(\eta^\top u(x) - A(\eta))$; $\nabla A = \mathbb E[u]$, $\nabla^2 A = \operatorname{Cov}[u]$; maximum likelihood matches the sample means of $u$.
- Its conjugate prior is $\propto \exp(\eta^\top \chi - \nu A(\eta))$; the update is $\chi + \sum_i u(x_i)$, $\nu + n$; evidence and predictive are ratios of its normalizer.
- Predictives are wider than plug-ins: beta–binomial variance larger by $(a + b + m)/(a + b + 1)$, Student-t tails instead of Gaussian ones.

## Code

Sequential updates checked against the batch formulas, the evidence as a product of predictives, the Dirichlet example, the normal–gamma posterior against the grid of the baseline and its predictive against Monte Carlo, the derivatives of the log-partition function, the beta–binomial predictive and a mixture prior. Seeds are fixed; the script runs in about a second, and exact numbers can differ in the last digit across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats, special

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
lbeta = special.betaln

# beta-Bernoulli: one flip at a time gives the batch posterior, and the product of the
# one-step predictives is the evidence B(a + k, b + n - k) / B(a, b)
x = np.array([1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1])                    # 5 heads in 7 flips
a, b, log_ev = 2.0, 2.0, 0.0                           # prior Beta(2, 2)
for xi in x:
    p_head = a / (a + b)                               # predictive of the next flip
    log_ev += np.log(p_head if xi else 1 - p_head)
    a, b = a + xi, b + 1 - xi                          # conjugate update
print(a, b, round(a / (a + b), 3))                     # 7.0 4.0 0.636
print(round(np.exp(log_ev), 6), round(np.exp(lbeta(7, 4) - lbeta(2, 2)), 6))   # 0.007143 0.007143: 1/140 both ways

# Dirichlet-categorical: counts (5, 2, 0, 1) with a uniform Dir(1, 1, 1, 1) prior
alpha, counts = np.ones(4), np.array([5, 2, 0, 1])
post = alpha + counts
print((post / post.sum()).round(3))                    # [0.5   0.25  0.083 0.167]: the unseen category keeps 1/12
log_ev = (special.gammaln(alpha.sum()) - special.gammaln(post.sum())
          + (special.gammaln(post) - special.gammaln(alpha)).sum())
print(round(1 / np.exp(log_ev)))                       # 27720: the evidence of the sequence is 1/27720

# normal-gamma: unknown mean and precision, prior mu | lam ~ N(m0, 1/(k0 lam)), lam ~ Gamma(a0, rate b0)
def ng_update(m, k, a, b, xs):
    n, xbar = len(xs), np.mean(xs)
    S = ((xs - xbar) ** 2).sum()
    return ((k * m + n * xbar) / (k + n), k + n, a + n / 2,
            b + S / 2 + k * n * (xbar - m) ** 2 / (2 * (k + n)))

xs = np.array([1.2, 0.4, 2.1, 1.5, 0.8])
batch = ng_update(0.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0, xs)
seq = (0.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0)
for xi in xs:
    seq = ng_update(*seq, np.array([xi]))
print(np.round(batch, 4), np.allclose(batch, seq))     # [1.   6.   3.5  2.45] True

# check against brute force: the posterior on a grid of (mu, lam), marginalized over lam
mn, kn, an, bn = batch
mu, lam = np.linspace(-2, 4, 1201), np.linspace(1e-4, 8, 1600)
M, L = np.meshgrid(mu, lam, indexing='ij')
log_joint = (stats.norm.logpdf(M, 0, 1 / np.sqrt(L)) + stats.gamma.logpdf(L, 1, scale=1)
             + stats.norm.logpdf(xs[:, None, None], M, 1 / np.sqrt(L)).sum(axis=0))
grid = np.exp(log_joint - log_joint.max()).sum(axis=1)
grid /= grid.sum() * (mu[1] - mu[0])                   # the marginal posterior density of mu on the grid
exact = stats.t(2 * an, loc=mn, scale=np.sqrt(bn / (an * kn))).pdf(mu)
print(round(float(np.abs(grid - exact).max()), 4))     # 0.0001: the closed-form Student-t marginal matches

# the posterior predictive is a Student-t; a Monte Carlo check by sampling lam, mu, then x
scale2 = bn * (kn + 1) / (an * kn)                     # the predictive scale squared
pred = stats.t(2 * an, loc=mn, scale=np.sqrt(scale2))
lam_s = rng.gamma(an, 1 / bn, size=400_000)
x_s = rng.normal(rng.normal(mn, 1 / np.sqrt(kn * lam_s)), 1 / np.sqrt(lam_s))
print(round(scale2, 4), round(pred.sf(3), 4), round((x_s > 3).mean(), 4))   # 0.8167 0.0313 0.0312
plug = stats.norm(mn, np.sqrt(bn / an))                # plug in the posterior means of mu and of lam
print(pred.ppf([0.025, 0.975]).round(2), plug.ppf([0.025, 0.975]).round(2), round(plug.sf(3), 4))
# [-1.14  3.14] [-0.64  2.64] 0.0084

# the log-partition function: its gradient is E[u(x)], its second derivative Var[u(x)]
A = lambda eta: np.log1p(np.exp(eta))                  # Bernoulli, eta = log(p / (1 - p))
eta, h = np.log(0.3 / 0.7), 1e-4
print(round((A(eta + h) - A(eta - h)) / (2 * h), 4), round((A(eta + h) - 2 * A(eta) + A(eta - h)) / h ** 2, 4))   # 0.3 0.21

# beta-binomial predictive for the next 10 flips after Beta(7, 4), against the plug-in binomial
bb, bi = stats.betabinom(10, 7, 4), stats.binom(10, 7 / 11)
print(round(bb.var(), 3), round(bi.var(), 3), round(bb.cdf(3), 3), round(bi.cdf(3), 3))   # 4.05 2.314 0.089 0.032

# a mixture of conjugate priors is conjugate: 0.5 Beta(20, 20) + 0.5 Beta(1, 1), then 9 heads in 10
w = np.array([0.5 * np.exp(lbeta(29, 21) - lbeta(20, 20)), 0.5 * np.exp(lbeta(10, 2) - lbeta(1, 1))])
w /= w.sum()
print(w.round(3), round(w @ [29 / 50, 10 / 12], 3))     # [0.156 0.844] 0.794
```

## Next

The evidence appeared in every section of this note as a by-product: the normalizer of the posterior, a ratio of two constants, the product of one-step predictions. The [next note](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-model-selection) makes it the main object. Comparing the evidence of two models compares how well each predicted the data before seeing them, and this comparison prefers the simplest model that explains the data without any penalty added by hand. Maximizing the evidence over hyperparameters, instead of fixing them, is the first step from exact conjugate models to the linear models of the next section.
