The [previous note](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions) ended with a concrete task: fit the distribution we are training, $Q$, to a target distribution $P$. To do that we need a number that measures how far $Q$ is from $P$. The standard first choice is the **Kullback–Leibler divergence**:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal X} P(x)\,\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} .
$$

What does this formula mean? This note builds it from the ground up: surprise → entropy → cross-entropy → KL. Along the way it explains why cross-entropy is the loss of classifiers and language models. The intermediate quantities are the entropy and the cross-entropy:

$$
\begin{gathered}
H(P) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal X} P(x)\log P(x),
\\
H(P, Q) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal X} P(x)\log Q(x).
\end{gathered}
$$

**Notation.** $P$ is the real (data, target) distribution and $Q$ the believed (model) distribution. $H(P)$ is the entropy of $X \sim P$, sometimes also written $H(X)$. For continuous distributions, lowercase $p$ and $q$ denote densities and sums become integrals. $\log_2$ gives bits and $\ln$ gives nats; a plain $\log$ means either, as long as one base is used throughout.

## Surprise

The construction below follows the intuitive route of Josh Starmer's StatQuest explanation of entropy ([Starmer](#references)); the formal treatment is in [Cover and Thomas (2006)](#references), chapter 2.

Take two boards with ten marks each. Board A has 5 noughts and 5 crosses; board B has 9 noughts and 1 cross. Draw one mark at random. On which board is getting an `X` more surprising?

<TicTacToeBoards />

Think of **surprise** informally as the feeling that something should not have happened. The probabilities of drawing each mark are

$$
\text{A:}\quad P(\text{O}) = \tfrac12,\;\; P(\text{X}) = \tfrac12
\qquad\text{vs}\qquad
\text{B:}\quad P(\text{O}) = \tfrac{9}{10},\;\; P(\text{X}) = \tfrac{1}{10}.
$$

The cross is clearly more surprising on board B, so surprise must grow as the probability falls.

**First attempt: $1/p$.** To compare $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac1{10}$, flip them: $1/P(\text{X})$ is $2$ on board A and $10$ on board B. This ranks the boards correctly, and as a function of $p$ it is a decreasing curve.

**Problem.** Take a rigged coin that always lands heads: `HHHHHHHHHH`. Getting heads should not surprise us at all, but

$$
\text{coin}\;
\begin{cases}
\xrightarrow{\;P(H)=1\;}\; H \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{1}{P(H)} = \dfrac11 = 1 & \text{no surprise, so it should be } 0, \\[10pt]
\xrightarrow{\;P(T)=0\;}\; T \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{1}{P(T)} = \dfrac10 & \text{not even a number.}
\end{cases}
$$

**Fix: take the logarithm.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{1}{P(H)} &= \log 1 = 0, \\
\log\frac{1}{P(T)} &= \log 1 - \log 0 \quad\text{is undefined}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The undefined case is harmless: an outcome with probability $0$ never happens, so its surprise is never needed. This gives the definition of **surprise**, also called self-information:

$$
s(x) = \log\frac{1}{P(x)} = -\log P(x).
$$

It equals $0$ when $P(x) = 1$ and grows without bound as $P(x) \to 0$.

<SurpriseCurve />

<aside class="callout"><strong>Units.</strong> Base 2 is the usual convention for two outcomes, because then one fair binary choice carries exactly 1 bit. The base does not change any comparison, only the unit: base 2 gives <em>bits</em>, base <em>e</em> gives <em>nats</em>, and 1 bit = ln 2 ≈ 0.693 nats. The coin examples below use bits; the cross-entropy examples later use natural logarithms.</aside>

### A biased coin

Let $P(H) = 0.9$ and $P(T) = 0.1$. Then

$$
\log_2\frac{1}{0.9} \approx 0.152,
\qquad
\log_2\frac{1}{0.1} \approx 3.322 .
$$

Tails is much rarer than heads, so its surprise is much larger.

| | Heads | Tails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Probability $P(x)$ | $0.9$ | $0.1$ |
| Surprise $\log_2 \frac{1}{P(x)}$ | $0.152$ | $3.322$ |

### Surprise is additive

Now flip the coin three times and observe `H H T`. The flips are independent, so the probability of the sequence is $0.9 \times 0.9 \times 0.1$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log_2\frac{1}{0.9\cdot 0.9\cdot 0.1}
&= -\big[\log_2 0.9 + \log_2 0.9 + \log_2 0.1\big] \\
&\approx 0.152 + 0.152 + 3.322 = 3.626 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The total surprise of a sequence is the sum of the surprises of the individual tosses. This is what the logarithm buys: it turns a product of probabilities into a sum. Up to the choice of base, it is the only continuous function of probability with this property, which is why logarithms appear in every information measure.

## Entropy: expected surprise

Now estimate the total surprise after 100 flips. We expect about $90$ heads and $10$ tails:

$$
\underbrace{(0.9\cdot 100)}_{\text{number of H}}\cdot \underbrace{0.152}_{\text{surprise of H}}
\;+\;
(0.1\cdot 100)\cdot 3.322
\;\approx\; 46.9 \text{ bits}.
$$

Dividing by 100 gives the surprise per flip:

$$
0.9 \cdot 0.152 + 0.1 \cdot 3.322 \approx 0.469 \text{ bits}.
$$

This is the surprise we expect **on average every time we flip the coin**. Each probability multiplies its own surprise, which is exactly an expected value, $\mathbb E[S] = \sum_s s\,P(S = s)$. The expected surprise is the **entropy**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P) &= \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{P(x)}
= -\sum_x P(x)\log P(x),
\end{aligned}
$$

the quantity introduced by Claude Shannon in 1948 ([Shannon, 1948](#references)).

### Back to the boards

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_A &= \tfrac12\log_2 2 + \tfrac12\log_2 2 = 1 \text{ bit}, \\
H_B &= \tfrac{9}{10}\log_2\frac{10}{9} + \tfrac{1}{10}\log_2 10 \approx 0.469 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Board B has exactly the biased coin's distribution, so it has the same entropy. The balanced board is less predictable: **higher entropy means more uncertainty**. For two outcomes the uniform distribution has the largest possible entropy, 1 bit.

<aside class="callout"><strong>The catch.</strong> To compute entropy we must know the real distribution <em>P</em>. When we fit a generative model to data, we never do.</aside>

## Cross-entropy: surprise under a wrong belief

Take a coin that our **model** believes to be fair, ${Q = (0.5,\,0.5)},$ while in **reality** it is rigged, ${P = (0.99,\,0.01)}.$ We observe ten heads in a row:

| | Probability of 10 heads | Surprise (nats) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model $Q$ | $0.5^{10} \approx 0.001$ | $\ln 0.5^{-10} \approx 6.93$ |
| Reality $P$ | $0.99^{10} \approx 0.90$ | $\ln 0.99^{-10} \approx 0.10$ |

<ModelVsReality />

The outcome was ordinary. Our surprise is caused by believing in the wrong model.

**Cross-entropy** $H(P, Q)$ is the average surprise when the real distribution is $P$ while we believe in $Q$:

$$
H(P, Q) = \sum_{x\in\mathcal X} \underbrace{P(x)}_{\substack{\text{how often } x \\ \text{is observed}}}\;
\underbrace{\log\frac{1}{Q(x)}}_{\substack{\text{how surprised we} \\ \text{are to see it}}}
$$

<CrossEntropyReading />

**No discrepancy.** If $Q = P$, cross-entropy reduces to entropy: $H(P, P) = H(P)$.

**Always** (Gibbs' inequality):

$$
H(P, Q) \;\ge\; H(P),
$$

with equality only when $Q = P$ ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), Theorem 2.6.3). Believing in a wrong distribution can only increase the average surprise.

**Asymmetry.** In general $H(P, Q) \ne H(Q, P)$. Keep $P$ as the rigged coin and $Q$ as the fair one, and use one flip and natural logarithms. The first argument is always the distribution that generates the outcomes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{fair belief, rigged reality:}\quad
H(P, Q) &= 0.99\ln 2 + 0.01\ln 2 = \ln 2 \approx 0.69, \\
\text{rigged belief, fair reality:}\quad
H(Q, P) &= 0.5\ln\frac{1}{0.99} + 0.5\ln\frac{1}{0.01} \approx 2.31 .
\end{aligned}
$$

In each sum the weights are the actual probabilities and the logarithms contain the believed ones. Believing that a fair coin is rigged is punished much harder: tails then occur half the time, and the rigged belief finds every one of them very surprising.

## Kullback–Leibler divergence

Cross-entropy mixes two sources of surprise: the surprise that the real distribution produces anyway, and the extra surprise caused by the wrong model. To isolate the second, **subtract the entropy** of the real distribution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P, Q) - H(P)
&= \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{Q(x)} - \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{P(x)} \\
&= \sum_{x\in\mathcal X} P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}
= D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q).
\end{aligned}
$$

The KL divergence ([Kullback and Leibler, 1951](#references)) is the **extra surprise from using $Q$ as the model instead of $P$**. By Gibbs' inequality it is non-negative and equals zero only when $Q = P$. It is not symmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality, so it is a divergence rather than a distance. For the two coins,

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) &\approx 0.693 - 0.056 = 0.637 \text{ nats}, \\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,P) &\approx 2.308 - 0.693 = 1.614 \text{ nats}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<CrossEntropyDecomposition />

The subtraction works whenever both terms are finite; the sum on the right is the general definition.

The two boards from the start of the note are also at a finite KL distance from each other, and the value depends on the direction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(A\,\|\,B) &= \tfrac12\log_2\frac{1/2}{9/10} + \tfrac12\log_2\frac{1/2}{1/10} \approx 0.737 \text{ bits},\\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(B\,\|\,A) &= \tfrac{9}{10}\log_2\frac{9/10}{1/2} + \tfrac{1}{10}\log_2\frac{1/10}{1/2} \approx 0.531 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

As a check, $H(B, A) = H_B + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(B\,\|\,A) = 0.469 + 0.531 = 1$ bit: measuring surprise with the uniform board A costs exactly one bit per draw, whatever the true board is.

## Why cross-entropy is the loss we minimize

For the true data distribution $P$ and a model $Q_\theta$:

$$
\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q_\theta)}_{\text{minimize w.r.t. } \theta}
=
\underbrace{H(P, Q_\theta)}_{\text{minimize w.r.t. } \theta}
-
\underbrace{H(P)}_{\substack{\text{constant: entropy} \\ \text{of the data}}}
$$

- Changing the model's parameters cannot change the entropy of the data, so $H(P)$ is a constant in the optimization.
- We usually cannot even estimate $H(P)$: it requires the true distribution, and we only have samples.
- So minimizing KL with respect to $\theta$ and minimizing cross-entropy have the same solution, and cross-entropy is the part we can compute. This is why classifiers and language models are trained with a cross-entropy loss.

Given samples $x_1, \dots, x_N$ from $P$, the cross-entropy is estimated by the average negative log-likelihood,

$$
H(P, Q_\theta) = \mathbb E_{x\sim P}\big[-\log Q_\theta(x)\big] \approx -\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \log Q_\theta(x_i).
$$

Minimizing it is **maximum-likelihood estimation**. So "minimize KL from the data to the model", "minimize cross-entropy" and "maximize likelihood" are three names for the same training objective ([Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016](#references), §5.5). The [next note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl) puts it to work on the Gaussian model from the previous note. Later families of generative models differ mostly in how they deal with the fact that $\log Q_\theta(x)$ is hard to compute: VAEs bound it, normalizing flows compute it exactly, and GANs avoid it altogether.

## Jensen–Shannon divergence

The Jensen–Shannon divergence ([Lin, 1991](#references)) uses the same building block, but it is **symmetric and bounded**. It compares both distributions with their mixture $M = \tfrac12(P + Q)$:

$$
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P, Q) = \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, M) + \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \,\|\, M),
\qquad
0 \le D_{\mathrm{JS}} \le \ln 2 \;\;(= 1 \text{ bit}).
$$

Because $M(x) > 0$ wherever $P(x) > 0$ or $Q(x) > 0$, both terms are always finite. The upper bound is reached when $P$ and $Q$ do not overlap at all. For the two coins, $D_{\mathrm{JS}} \approx 0.193$ nats ($0.279$ bits).

JS will matter twice. In the next note it is one of the losses compared on a two-mode target. In the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans) it turns out to be exactly what the original GAN objective minimizes when the discriminator is optimal, and its saturation at $\ln 2$ explains why GAN training can stall.
