To fit a generative model, we need a way to compare its distribution $Q$ with a target distribution $P$. The seminar builds this idea from **surprise**: how unexpected is an outcome under the distribution we believe in?

Throughout the coin examples, logarithms have base 2, so information is measured in **bits**. Natural logarithms give **nats** instead. The base sets the unit; it does not depend on the number of classes.

## Surprise of an outcome

An outcome with probability $0.1$ should be more surprising than one with probability $0.5$. The reciprocal $1/P(x)$ has the right direction, but assigns a value of 1 to a certain event. We want a certain event to carry zero surprise.

Taking the logarithm gives the self-information, or surprisal:

$$
I_P(x) = \log_2\frac{1}{P(x)} = -\log_2 P(x).
$$

If $P(x)=1$, then $I_P(x)=0$. As $P(x)$ approaches zero, the surprise grows without bound. An event assigned probability zero has infinite surprisal in this limiting convention.

<SurprisePlot />

For a biased coin with $P(H)=0.9$ and $P(T)=0.1$:

| Outcome | Probability | Surprise, bits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heads | 0.9 | $-\log_2(0.9) \approx 0.152$ |
| Tails | 0.1 | $-\log_2(0.1) \approx 3.322$ |

The logarithm also makes surprise additive for independent outcomes. For example, the sequence heads, heads, tails has probability $0.9\cdot0.9\cdot0.1$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_P(H,H,T)
&= -\log_2(0.9\cdot0.9\cdot0.1) \\
&= I_P(H)+I_P(H)+I_P(T) \\
&\approx 3.626\ \text{bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

## Entropy is expected surprise

Entropy averages surprise using the probabilities of the outcomes:

$$
H(P)
= \mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[I_P(X)]
= -\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} P(x)\log_2 P(x).
$$

Terms with $P(x)=0$ contribute zero, using the convention $0\log 0=0$.

For the biased coin,

$$
H(P)
= 0.9\log_2\frac{1}{0.9}
+ 0.1\log_2\frac{1}{0.1}
\approx 0.469\ \text{bits per flip}.
$$

Across 100 independent flips, the **expected** total surprise is approximately $46.90$ bits. Dividing by 100 returns the expected surprise of one flip. A particular sequence can contain a different number of heads and tails, so its total surprise can differ from this expectation.

Compare three coins:

| Distribution over heads and tails | Entropy, bits per flip |
| --- | --- |
| $(1,0)$ | 0 |
| $(0.9,0.1)$ | 0.469 |
| $(0.5,0.5)$ | 1 |

The fair coin has the most uncertainty about the next outcome. Here, higher entropy means less predictability; it need not mean that an image or a dataset contains unwanted noise.

## Cross-entropy uses the model's beliefs

Suppose outcomes actually follow $P$, but we assign them probabilities using $Q$. Cross-entropy is the average surprise under that model:

$$
H(P,Q)
= \mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[-\log_2 Q(X)]
= -\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}} P(x)\log_2 Q(x).
$$

The two distributions play different roles: $P(x)$ determines how often an outcome occurs, while $Q(x)$ determines how surprising the model considers it.

When $Q=P$, cross-entropy reduces to entropy. More generally,

$$
H(P,Q)\ge H(P).
$$

Using the wrong distribution cannot reduce the average surprise below the value obtained using the true distribution. Cross-entropy is generally asymmetric: $H(P,Q)$ and $H(Q,P)$ need not agree.

## KL divergence isolates the model mismatch

Cross-entropy includes both the uncertainty of the data and the penalty for a mismatched model. When these quantities are finite, subtracting the data's own entropy isolates the latter:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P,Q)-H(P)
&= -\sum_x P(x)\log_2 Q(x)
   +\sum_x P(x)\log_2 P(x) \\
&= \sum_x P(x)\log_2\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \\
&= D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q).
\end{aligned}
$$

KL divergence is the **extra expected surprise from using $Q$ in place of $P$**. It is nonnegative and equals zero when the distributions agree. It is generally asymmetric, so it is a divergence rather than a metric.

The identity can also be written as

$$
H(P,Q)=H(P)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q).
$$

## Why cross-entropy is a training objective

When the target distribution $P$ is fixed, changing model parameters $\theta$ changes $Q_\theta$, but cannot change $H(P)$. Therefore, minimizing cross-entropy minimizes the same distribution mismatch as forward KL, whenever these quantities are finite.

We usually have samples rather than an exact description of $P$. The empirical objective is

$$
\widehat{H}(P,Q_\theta)
= -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log Q_\theta(x_i).
$$

With natural logarithms, this is the average negative log-likelihood. Minimizing it is equivalent to maximizing the likelihood of the observed data. We do not need to calculate $H(P)$ to optimize this objective.

The source notes say that the real data entropy cannot be estimated from the training sample. More precisely, a finite sample does not give the exact underlying distribution or its exact entropy; entropy estimation is possible under additional assumptions, but is unnecessary for this optimization.

## Support and the direction of KL

For discrete distributions, if $P(x)>0$ but $Q(x)=0$ for any outcome, then

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)=\infty.
$$

Merely overlapping is insufficient: the model must assign positive probability everywhere the target does. For continuous densities, the corresponding condition concerns sets with positive probability; KL can also diverge because of tail behavior.

The seminar contrasts two directions when a restricted model is fitted to a target with several modes:

- **Forward KL**, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$, heavily penalizes assigning too little probability to regions where the target has mass. It can encourage a model to spread across modes.
- **Reverse KL**, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,P)$, averages over the model's samples. It can concentrate on one target mode while leaving others uncovered.

These are tendencies under model restrictions, not guarantees that either objective always produces a particular shape.

## Jensen-Shannon divergence

The handwritten outline introduces Jensen-Shannon divergence as symmetric and bounded. Expanding that definition, let

$$
M=\frac{P+Q}{2}.
$$

Then

$$
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P,Q)
= \frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,M)
+ \frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,M).
$$

Unlike KL, the two arguments can be swapped without changing the result. With base-2 logarithms, $0\le D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P,Q)\le 1$; with natural logarithms, the upper bound is $\ln 2$.

Symmetry and boundedness do not by themselves solve generative-model training problems. For disjoint supports, JS reaches its maximum and does not distinguish how far apart those supports are. This becomes relevant in the later seminar on GANs.
