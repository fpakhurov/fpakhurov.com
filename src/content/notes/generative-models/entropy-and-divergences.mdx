The [previous note](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions) ended with a concrete task: fit the distribution we are training, $Q$, to a target distribution $P$. To do that we need a number that measures how far $Q$ is from $P$. The standard first choice is the **Kullback–Leibler divergence**:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal X} P(x)\,\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} .
$$

What does this formula mean? This note builds it from the ground up: surprise → entropy → cross-entropy → KL. Along the way it explains why cross-entropy is the loss of classifiers and language models. The intermediate quantities are the entropy and the cross-entropy:

$$
\begin{gathered}
H(P) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal X} P(x)\log P(x),
\\
H(P, Q) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal X} P(x)\log Q(x).
\end{gathered}
$$

**Notation.** $P$ is the real (data, target) distribution and $Q$ the believed (model) distribution. $H(P)$ is the entropy of $X \sim P$, sometimes also written $H(X)$. For continuous distributions, lowercase $p$ and $q$ denote densities and sums become integrals; what changes for densities is noted at the end of the KL section. $\log_2$ gives bits and $\ln$ gives nats; a plain $\log$ means either, as long as one base is used throughout.


## Surprise

The construction below follows the intuitive route of Josh Starmer's StatQuest explanation of entropy ([Starmer, 2021](#references)); the formal treatment is in [Cover and Thomas (2006)](#references), chapter 2.

Take two boards with ten marks each. Board A has 5 noughts and 5 crosses; board B has 9 noughts and 1 cross. Draw one mark at random. On which board is getting an `X` more surprising?

<TicTacToeBoards />

Think of **surprise** informally as the feeling that something should not have happened. The probabilities of drawing each mark are

$$
\begin{gathered}
\text{A:}\quad P(\text{O}) = \tfrac12,\;\; P(\text{X}) = \tfrac12,
\\
\text{B:}\quad P(\text{O}) = \tfrac{9}{10},\;\; P(\text{X}) = \tfrac{1}{10}.
\end{gathered}
$$

The cross is clearly more surprising on board B, so surprise must grow as the probability falls.

**First attempt: $1/p$.** To compare $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac1{10}$, flip them: $1/P(\text{X})$ is $2$ on board A and $10$ on board B. This ranks the boards correctly, and as a function of $p$ it is a decreasing curve.

**Problem.** Take a rigged coin that always lands heads: `HHHHHHHHHH`. Its two outcomes give

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H) = 1 &: \quad \frac{1}{P(H)} = \frac11 = 1, \\[4pt]
P(T) = 0 &: \quad \frac{1}{P(T)} = \frac10 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Getting heads should not surprise us at all, so the first value should be $0$. The second is not even a number.

**Fix: take the logarithm.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{1}{P(H)} &= \log 1 = 0, \\
\log\frac{1}{P(T)} &= \log 1 - \log 0 \quad\text{is undefined}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The undefined case is harmless: an outcome with probability $0$ never happens, so its surprise is never needed. This gives the definition of **surprise**, also called self-information:

$$
s(x) = \log\frac{1}{P(x)} = -\log P(x).
$$

It equals $0$ when $P(x) = 1$ and grows without bound as $P(x) \to 0$.

<SurpriseCurve />

<aside class="callout"><strong>Units.</strong> Base 2 is the usual convention in information theory: then one fair binary choice carries exactly 1 bit. The base does not change any comparison, only the unit: base 2 gives <em>bits</em>, base <em>e</em> gives <em>nats</em>, and 1 bit = ln 2 ≈ 0.693 nats. The coin examples below use bits; the cross-entropy examples later use natural logarithms.</aside>

### A biased coin

Let $P(H) = 0.9$ and $P(T) = 0.1$. Then

$$
\log_2\frac{1}{0.9} \approx 0.152,
\qquad
\log_2\frac{1}{0.1} \approx 3.322 .
$$

Tails is much rarer than heads, so its surprise is much larger.

| | Heads | Tails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Probability $P(x)$ | $0.9$ | $0.1$ |
| Surprise $\log_2 \frac{1}{P(x)}$ | $0.152$ | $3.322$ |

### Surprise is additive

Now flip the coin three times and observe `H H T`. The flips are independent, so the probability of the sequence is $0.9 \times 0.9 \times 0.1$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
s(\text{HHT})
&= \log_2\frac{1}{0.9\cdot 0.9\cdot 0.1} \\
&= -\log_2 0.9 - \log_2 0.9 - \log_2 0.1 \\
&\approx 0.152 + 0.152 + 3.322 \\
&= 3.626 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The total surprise of a sequence is the sum of the surprises of the individual tosses. This is what the logarithm buys: it turns a product of probabilities into a sum. No other continuous function does this. Suppose $s(pq) = s(p) + s(q)$ for all $p, q \in (0, 1]$ and put $g(t) = s(e^{-t})$ for $t \ge 0$. Then $g(t + u) = g(t) + g(u)$, and the only continuous solutions of this equation are linear, $g(t) = ct$. Hence $s(p) = c\log\frac1p$. Requiring rare outcomes to be surprising makes $c > 0$, and the choice of $c$ is the choice of base. This is why logarithms appear in every information measure; Shannon proved the matching uniqueness result for entropy itself ([Shannon, 1948](#references), Theorem 2).

## Entropy: expected surprise

Now estimate the total surprise after 100 flips. We expect about $90$ heads and $10$ tails:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\underbrace{(0.9\cdot 100)}_{\text{number of H}}\cdot \underbrace{0.152}_{\text{surprise of H}} \\
&\quad +\; (0.1\cdot 100)\cdot 3.322 \;\approx\; 46.9 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Dividing by 100 gives the surprise per flip:

$$
0.9 \cdot 0.152 + 0.1 \cdot 3.322 \approx 0.469 \text{ bits}.
$$

This is the surprise we expect **on average every time we flip the coin**. Each probability multiplies its own surprise, which is exactly an expected value: if $S = s(X)$ is the surprise of a random outcome $X \sim P$, then $\mathbb E[S] = \sum_x P(x)\,s(x)$. The expected surprise is the **entropy**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P) &= \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{P(x)} \\
&= -\sum_x P(x)\log P(x),
\end{aligned}
$$

the quantity introduced by Claude Shannon in 1948 ([Shannon, 1948](#references)).

Outcomes with $P(x) = 0$ never occur, so their terms are left out of the sum. Equivalently, we use the convention $0 \cdot \log\frac10 = 0$, which is the limit of $p\log\frac1p$ as $p \to 0$ ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), §2.1). The rigged coin that always lands heads therefore has entropy $1 \cdot \log 1 = 0$: it holds no surprise at all.

### Back to the boards

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_A &= \tfrac12\log_2 2 + \tfrac12\log_2 2 = 1 \text{ bit}, \\
H_B &= \tfrac{9}{10}\log_2\frac{10}{9} + \tfrac{1}{10}\log_2 10 \approx 0.469 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Board B has exactly the biased coin's distribution, so it has the same entropy. The balanced board is less predictable: **higher entropy means more uncertainty**. For two outcomes no distribution has more than 1 bit of entropy, and only the uniform one reaches it; this follows from Gibbs' inequality in the next section.

<aside class="callout"><strong>The catch.</strong> To compute entropy we must know the real distribution <em>P</em>. When we fit a generative model to data, we never do.</aside>

## Cross-entropy: surprise under a wrong belief

Take a coin that our **model** believes to be fair, ${Q = (0.5,\,0.5)},$ while in **reality** it is rigged, ${P = (0.99,\,0.01)}.$ We observe ten heads in a row:

| | Probability of 10 heads | Surprise (nats) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model $Q$ | $0.5^{10} \approx 0.001$ | $\ln 0.5^{-10} \approx 6.93$ |
| Reality $P$ | $0.99^{10} \approx 0.90$ | $\ln 0.99^{-10} \approx 0.10$ |

<ModelVsReality />

The outcome was ordinary. Our surprise is caused by believing in the wrong model.

**Cross-entropy** $H(P, Q)$ is the average surprise when the real distribution is $P$ while we believe in $Q$:

$$
H(P, Q) = \sum_{x\in\mathcal X} \underbrace{P(x)}_{\substack{\text{how often } x \\ \text{is observed}}}\;
\underbrace{\log\frac{1}{Q(x)}}_{\substack{\text{how surprised we} \\ \text{are to see it}}}
$$

<CrossEntropyReading />

**No discrepancy.** If $Q = P$, cross-entropy reduces to entropy: $H(P, P) = H(P)$.

**Always** (Gibbs' inequality):

$$
H(P, Q) \;\ge\; H(P),
$$

with equality only when $Q = P$ ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), Theorem 2.6.3). Believing in a wrong distribution can only increase the average surprise. The proof takes one line once the difference $H(P, Q) - H(P)$ has a name; it is given in the next section.

One consequence settles the question left open by the boards. Let $Q$ be uniform over the $|\mathcal X|$ outcomes. Then $\log\frac{1}{Q(x)} = \log|\mathcal X|$ for every $x$, so $H(P, Q) = \log|\mathcal X|$, and Gibbs' inequality gives $H(P) \le \log|\mathcal X|$, with equality only for the uniform $P$ ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), Theorem 2.6.4). For two outcomes the bound is $\log_2 2 = 1$ bit.

**Asymmetry.** In general $H(P, Q) \ne H(Q, P)$. Keep $P$ as the rigged coin and $Q$ as the fair one, and use one flip and natural logarithms. The first argument is always the distribution that generates the outcomes. With a fair belief and a rigged reality, and then with a rigged belief and a fair reality,

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P, Q) &= 0.99\ln 2 + 0.01\ln 2 \\
&= \ln 2 \approx 0.69, \\
H(Q, P) &= 0.5\ln\frac{1}{0.99} + 0.5\ln\frac{1}{0.01} \\
&\approx 2.31 .
\end{aligned}
$$

In each sum the weights are the actual probabilities and the logarithms contain the believed ones. Believing that a fair coin is rigged is punished much harder: tails then occur half the time, and the rigged belief finds every one of them very surprising.

## Kullback–Leibler divergence

Cross-entropy mixes two sources of surprise: the surprise that the real distribution produces anyway, and the extra surprise caused by the wrong model. To isolate the second, **subtract the entropy** of the real distribution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P, Q) - H(P)
&= \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{Q(x)} \\
&\quad - \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{P(x)} \\
&= \sum_{x\in\mathcal X} P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \\
&= D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q).
\end{aligned}
$$

The KL divergence ([Kullback and Leibler, 1951](#references)) is the **extra surprise from using $Q$ as the model instead of $P$**. The subtraction needs both terms to be finite; the sum on the right is the general definition. Its terms follow the same conventions as before. A term with $P(x) = 0$ contributes $0$. A term with $P(x) > 0$ but $Q(x) = 0$ is $P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{0} = +\infty$, and then so is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$. So KL is finite only when $Q$ gives positive probability to every outcome to which $P$ does, that is, when $P$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $Q$ ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), §2.3). The [next note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#a-common-support) shows what this condition means in training.

<aside class="callout">

<strong>Why KL is non-negative.</strong> If $Q(x) = 0$ at some $x$ with $P(x) > 0$, the divergence is $+\infty$ and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise sum only over the outcomes with $P(x) > 0$ and apply Jensen's inequality to the concave logarithm, with weights $P(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)
&= \sum_{P(x) > 0} P(x)\log\frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \\
&\le \log \sum_{P(x) > 0} P(x)\,\frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \\
&= \log \sum_{P(x) > 0} Q(x) \\
&\le \log 1 = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the logarithm is strictly concave, the first inequality is an equality only if $Q(x)/P(x)$ is the same constant $c$ for all $x$ with $P(x) > 0$. The second is an equality only if $Q$ puts no mass outside those outcomes, which makes $c = 1$. So $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) \ge 0$, with equality only when $Q = P$. Adding $H(P)$ to both sides gives Gibbs' inequality, $H(P, Q) \ge H(P)$.

</aside>

So KL is non-negative and equals zero only when $Q = P$. It is not symmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), §2.3; an example follows below), so it is a divergence rather than a distance. For the two coins, in nats and with four decimals so that the parts add up,

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) &\approx 0.6931 - 0.0560 = 0.6371, \\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,P) &\approx 2.3076 - 0.6931 = 1.6145 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<CrossEntropyDecomposition />

The two boards from the start of the note give every mark positive probability, so they have a finite KL divergence in both directions, and its value depends on the direction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(A\,\|\,B) &= \tfrac12\log_2\frac{1/2}{9/10} + \tfrac12\log_2\frac{1/2}{1/10} \\
&\approx 0.737 \text{ bits},\\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(B\,\|\,A) &= \tfrac{9}{10}\log_2\frac{9/10}{1/2} + \tfrac{1}{10}\log_2\frac{1/10}{1/2} \\
&\approx 0.531 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

As a check, $H(B, A) = H_B + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(B\,\|\,A) = 0.469 + 0.531 = 1$ bit: measuring surprise with the uniform board A costs exactly one bit per draw, whatever the true board is.

<BoardsKL />

The same distributions show that KL breaks the triangle inequality. Identify O with heads and X with tails, and compare board A with the rigged coin $P = (0.99, 0.01)$, once directly and once through board B. Directly, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(A\,\|\,P) \approx 2.329$ bits. Through B, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(A\,\|\,B) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(B\,\|\,P) \approx 0.737 + 0.208 = 0.945$ bits, which is less.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Densities.</strong> For continuous distributions with densities $p$ and $q$ the sums become integrals, but not every reading survives. A density can exceed 1, so $-\log p(x)$ is negative wherever $p(x) > 1$. The differential entropy $H(p) = -\int p(x)\log p(x)\,dx$ can be negative too: for $\mathcal N(0, 0.1^2)$ it is $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi e \cdot 0.01) \approx -0.88$ nats. It is no longer a count of bits. The relative quantities keep their meaning: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = \int p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx$ is still non-negative and is zero only when $p = q$ almost everywhere, and $H(p, q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$ whenever $H(p)$ is finite ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), §8.1, §8.5–8.6).</aside>

## Why cross-entropy is the loss we minimize

For the true data distribution $P$ and a model $Q_\theta$:

$$
\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q_\theta)}_{\text{minimize w.r.t. } \theta}
=
\underbrace{H(P, Q_\theta)}_{\text{minimize w.r.t. } \theta}
-
\underbrace{H(P)}_{\substack{\text{constant: entropy} \\ \text{of the data}}}
$$

- Changing the model's parameters cannot change the entropy of the data, so $H(P)$ is a constant in the optimization.
- Nor do we need its value. For high-dimensional data such as images, estimating $H(P)$ from samples is itself a hard problem.
- So minimizing KL with respect to $\theta$ and minimizing cross-entropy have the same solution, and cross-entropy is the part we can estimate from samples. This is why classifiers and language models are trained with a cross-entropy loss. There the same argument is applied to the conditional distribution of the label (or of the next token) given the input, and the target $P(\cdot \mid x)$ is usually a one-hot label.

Given samples $x_1, \dots, x_N$ from $P$, the cross-entropy is estimated by the average negative log-likelihood,

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P, Q_\theta) &= \mathbb E_{x\sim P}\big[-\log Q_\theta(x)\big] \\
&\approx -\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \log Q_\theta(x_i).
\end{aligned}
$$

Minimizing it is **maximum-likelihood estimation**. For discrete outcomes the average negative log-likelihood is exactly the cross-entropy $H(\hat P, Q_\theta)$ between the empirical distribution $\hat P$ of the samples (probability $1/N$ on each $x_i$) and the model, so maximum likelihood minimizes $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat P\,\|\,Q_\theta)$ ([Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016](#references), §5.5). As $N$ grows, the average converges to $H(P, Q_\theta)$ by the law of large numbers, and that differs from $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q_\theta)$ only by the constant $H(P)$. So "minimize $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q_\theta)$", "minimize $H(P, Q_\theta)$" and "maximize likelihood" are three names for the same training objective: exactly for the empirical distribution, and in the limit of many samples for $P$ itself.

Later families of generative models differ mostly in how they deal with the fact that $\log Q_\theta(x)$ is hard to compute: VAEs bound it, normalizing flows compute it exactly, GANs avoid it altogether, and diffusion models bound it again, with a fixed noising process in place of a learned encoder.

## Jensen–Shannon divergence

The Jensen–Shannon divergence ([Lin, 1991](#references)) uses the same building block, but it is **symmetric and bounded**. It compares both distributions with their mixture $M = \tfrac12(P + Q)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P \,\|\, Q) &= \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, M) \\
&\quad + \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \,\|\, M).
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $M(x) > 0$ wherever $P(x) > 0$ or $Q(x) > 0$, both terms are always finite. Both are non-negative, so $D_{\mathrm{JS}} \ge 0$, with equality only when $P = M = Q$. For the upper bound, note that $P(x) \le P(x) + Q(x) = 2M(x)$. Every term of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,M)$ therefore has $\log\frac{P(x)}{M(x)} \le \log 2$, so $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,M) \le \log 2$, with equality only if $P(x) = 2M(x)$, that is $Q(x) = 0$, wherever $P(x) > 0$. The same holds for the second term. Hence

$$
0 \;\le\; D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P \,\|\, Q) \;\le\; \ln 2 = 1 \text{ bit},
$$

and the maximum is reached exactly when $P$ and $Q$ do not overlap. For the two coins, $D_{\mathrm{JS}} \approx 0.193$ nats ($0.279$ bits).

JS will matter twice. In the next note it is one of the losses compared on a two-mode target. In the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#step-2-substitute-the-optimal-discriminator-and-recognize-js), the original GAN objective with an optimal discriminator turns out to equal $2\,D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P\,\|\,Q) - 2\ln 2$, with $P$ the data distribution and $Q$ the generator's ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#references)). The saturation of JS at $\ln 2$ for non-overlapping distributions then explains why GAN training can stall ([Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017](#references)).
