We want to fit a model distribution $Q$ to a target distribution $P$. The first option is the **Kullback–Leibler divergence**:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal X} P(x)\,\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} .
$$

To see what this formula means, we build it from the ground up: surprise → entropy → cross-entropy → KL. (This is also the promised explanation of the cross-entropy loss from the NLP course.)

For reference, the two other quantities we are heading to:

$$
H(X) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal X} P(x)\log P(x),
\qquad
H(P, Q) = -\sum_{x\in\mathcal X} P(x)\log Q(x).
$$

## Surprise

Consider two boards with ten marks each:

| | Board A | Board B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noughts `O` | 5 | 9 |
| Crosses `X` | 5 | 1 |
| $P(\text{O})$ | $1/2$ | $9/10$ |
| $P(\text{X})$ | $1/2$ | $1/10$ |

Draw one mark at random. On which board is getting an `X` more surprising? Clearly on board B: surprise is about something that "should not have happened", so it must grow as the probability falls.

**First attempt: $1/p$.** Flipping the probability gives $2$ for board A and $10$ for board B, which ranks them correctly. But take a completely rigged coin that always lands heads: `H H H H H H H H H H`. Then $P(H) = 1$ and $1/P(H) = 1$, although seeing heads is no surprise at all — the answer should be $0$.

**Fix: take the logarithm.**

$$
\log\frac{1}{P(H)} = \log\frac{1}{1} = 0,
\qquad
\log\frac{1}{P(T)} = \log\frac{1}{0} \;\;\text{is undefined} .
$$

The undefined case is harmless: an outcome with probability $0$ never happens, so its surprise is never needed. This gives the definition of **surprise** (self-information):

$$
s(x) = \log\frac{1}{P(x)} = -\log P(x).
$$

It is $0$ when $P(x) = 1$ and grows without bound as $P(x) \to 0$.

Base 2 gives <em>bits</em>, base <em>e</em> gives <em>nats</em>, and 1 bit = ln 2 ≈ 0.693 nats. The coin examples below use bits; the cross-entropy slides later use natural logarithms.</aside>

### A biased coin

Let $P(H) = 0.9$ and $P(T) = 0.1$.

| | Heads | Tails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Probability $P(x)$ | $0.9$ | $0.1$ |
| Surprise $\log_2 \frac{1}{P(x)}$ | $0.152$ | $3.322$ |

Tails is much rarer than heads, so its surprise is much larger.

**Surprise is additive.** For the sequence `H H T` of independent flips,

$$
\log_2\frac{1}{0.9\cdot 0.9\cdot 0.1}
= \log_2\frac{1}{0.9} + \log_2\frac{1}{0.9} + \log_2\frac{1}{0.1}
\approx 0.152 + 0.152 + 3.322 = 3.626 \text{ bits}.
$$

The total surprise of a sequence is the sum of the surprises of the individual flips. (Adding the rounded values $0.15 + 0.15 + 3.32$ gives the $3.62$ shown on the slide.) This additivity is exactly what the logarithm buys us: it turns products of probabilities into sums.

## Entropy: expected surprise

How much total surprise should we expect over 100 flips? About $0.9 \cdot 100 = 90$ heads and $0.1 \cdot 100 = 10$ tails:

$$
\underbrace{(0.9\cdot 100)}_{\text{number of heads}}\cdot 0.152
\;+\;
\underbrace{(0.1\cdot 100)}_{\text{number of tails}}\cdot 3.322
\;\approx\; 46.9 \text{ bits per 100 flips}.
$$

Dividing by 100 — the 100s cancel — gives the surprise per flip:

$$
0.9 \cdot 0.152 + 0.1 \cdot 3.322 \approx 0.469 \text{ bits}.
$$

(With the rounded surprises $0.15$ and $3.32$ this is $46.7$ and $0.47$, the values in the handwritten notes.)

This is an ordinary expected value, $\mathbb E[S] = \sum_s s\,P(S = s)$: each possible value of the surprise times the probability of observing it. That expected surprise is the **entropy** — the surprise we expect per flip if we throw the coin many times. In general,

$$
H(P) = \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{P(x)}
= \sum_x P(x)\big[\log 1 - \log P(x)\big]
= -\sum_x P(x)\log P(x),
$$

the formula Claude Shannon published in 1948.

### Back to the boards

$$
H_A = \tfrac12\log_2 2 + \tfrac12\log_2 2 = 1 \text{ bit},
\qquad
H_B = \tfrac{9}{10}\log_2\tfrac{10}{9} + \tfrac{1}{10}\log_2 10 \approx 0.469 \text{ bits}.
$$

Board B has exactly the coin's distribution, so it has the same entropy. The balanced board is less predictable: **higher entropy means more uncertainty** ("more noise").

<aside class="callout"><strong>The catch.</strong> To compute entropy we must know the real distribution <em>P</em>. When fitting a generative model we never do.</aside>

## Cross-entropy: surprise under a wrong belief

Take a real coin and a rigged one. Suppose our **model** believes the coin is fair, $Q = (0.5, 0.5)$, while **reality** is rigged, $P = (0.99, 0.01)$. We observe ten heads in a row:

| | Probability of 10 heads | Surprise (nats) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model $Q$ | $0.5^{10} \approx 0.001$ | $-\ln 0.5^{10} \approx 6.93$ |
| Reality $P$ | $0.99^{10} \approx 0.90$ | $-\ln 0.99^{10} \approx 0.10$ |

The outcome was ordinary; our surprise is caused by believing in the wrong model.

**Cross-entropy** is the average surprise when outcomes come from $P$ but we measure surprise with our belief $Q$:

$$
H(P, Q) = \sum_{x\in\mathcal X} \underbrace{P(x)}_{\text{how often } x \text{ occurs}}\;
\underbrace{\log\frac{1}{Q(x)}}_{\text{how surprised we are to see it}} .
$$

{/* Figure to redraw (page 10): a "real" density P next to a differently shaped "believed" density Q, with arrows from P(x) and log 1/Q(x) to their meanings. */}

**No discrepancy.** If $P = Q$,

$$
H(P, P) = \sum_s p_s \log\frac{1}{p_s} = H(P):
$$

cross-entropy reduces to the entropy (uncertainty) of $P$.

**Always** (Gibbs' inequality):

$$
H(P, Q) \;\ge\; H(P),
$$

with equality only when $Q = P$. Surprise only increases when you believe in a wrong distribution.

**Asymmetry.** In general $H(P, Q) \ne H(Q, P)$. With the same two coins, one flip, natural logarithms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{believe fair, reality rigged:}\quad
H(P, Q) &= 0.99\ln\tfrac{1}{0.5} + 0.01\ln\tfrac{1}{0.5} = \ln 2 \approx 0.69, \\
\text{believe rigged, reality fair:}\quad
H(Q, P) &= 0.5\ln\tfrac{1}{0.99} + 0.5\ln\tfrac{1}{0.01} \approx 2.31 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Believing that a fair coin is rigged is punished much harder, because tails occur half the time and the rigged belief finds them very surprising.

## Kullback–Leibler divergence

How do we quantify the difference between belief and reality? Cross-entropy mixes two sources of surprise: the unavoidable one that comes from the randomness of $P$ itself, and the extra one caused by the wrong model. To isolate the second, **subtract the expected surprise** of the real distribution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P, Q) - H(P)
&= \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{Q(x)} - \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{P(x)} \\
&= \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1/Q(x)}{1/P(x)}
= \sum_{x\in\mathcal X} P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}
= D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q).
\end{aligned}
$$

The KL divergence is the **extra surprise from using $Q$ as the model instead of $P$** — it isolates the surprise due to the wrong model from the surprise inherent in the real outcomes. It is non-negative, equals zero only when $Q = P$, and is not symmetric. For the coins above: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q) \approx 0.69 - 0.056 = 0.64$ nats, while $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|P) \approx 2.31 - 0.69 = 1.61$ nats.

(The subtraction is valid when both terms are finite; the general definition is the sum on the right.)

## Why cross-entropy is the loss we minimize

For a true data distribution $P$ and a model $Q_\theta$:

$$
\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q_\theta)}_{\text{minimize w.r.t. } Q}
=
\underbrace{H(P, Q_\theta)}_{\text{minimize w.r.t. } Q}
-
\underbrace{H(P)}_{\text{constant: entropy of the training data}} .
$$

- Changing the model's weights cannot change the entropy of the training data, so $H(P)$ is a constant in the optimization.
- We also cannot really estimate the true data entropy from a finite training sample.
- Therefore minimizing KL with respect to $Q$ and minimizing cross-entropy are **equivalent**, and cross-entropy is the part we can compute. This is why language models are trained with a cross-entropy loss, and why KL-type losses work for generative models without knowing the real distribution explicitly.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Connecting to maximum likelihood.</strong> Given samples $x_1, \dots, x_N$ from $P$, the cross-entropy is estimated by $-\frac1N\sum_i \log Q_\theta(x_i)$: the average negative log-likelihood. Minimizing it is maximum-likelihood estimation.</aside>

With this, we know one way to fit generative models.

## Jensen–Shannon divergence

The JS divergence is roughly the same idea, but **symmetric and bounded**. It compares both distributions with their mixture $M = \tfrac12(P + Q)$:

$$
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P, Q) = \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, M) + \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \,\|\, M),
\qquad
0 \le D_{\mathrm{JS}} \le \ln 2 .
$$

Because $M(x) > 0$ wherever $P(x) > 0$ or $Q(x) > 0$, it is always finite. For the two coins, $D_{\mathrm{JS}} \approx 0.19$ nats ($0.28$ bits). JS reappears in the analysis of GANs.

## Limitations in practice

KL is problematic as a practical training objective:

- **Supports must overlap.** The model must put mass wherever the data does: if $Q(x) = 0$ while $P(x) > 0$ for some $x$, then $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q) = \infty$. A badly initialized model that misses part of the data gives an infinite or uninformative loss.
- **Direction matters.**
  - Forward KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)$ is *mass-covering*: it penalizes $Q$ for missing any region of $P$, so a simple $Q$ spreads out to cover all modes, with wide dispersion.
  - Reverse KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\|P)$ is *mode-seeking*: it penalizes $Q$ for putting mass where $P$ has little, so $Q$ tends to lock onto one mode.
- **JS still suffers** when the model does not cover the modes of the data. When the supports barely overlap, JS saturates near $\ln 2$ and provides little gradient signal.
- **MSE** as an alternative is sensitive to the scale of the data.
