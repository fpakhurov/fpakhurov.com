A model believes a coin is fair. We flip it ten times and get ten heads. The model is surprised; someone who knew the coin was rigged would not be. How wrong is the model, as a single number we can minimize?

The course's answer is the **Kullback–Leibler divergence**, and the trainable Gaussian of the [previous note](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions) will be fitted with it:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \sum_{x} P(x)\,\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} ,
$$

where $P$ is the real distribution that produces the outcomes and $Q$ the distribution the model believes in. Each piece of the formula has a meaning of its own, and the first piece is surprise.

## Surprise

The route below follows Josh Starmer's StatQuest ([Starmer, 2021](#references)); the formal treatment is chapter 2 of [Cover and Thomas (2006)](#references). Take two boards with ten marks each. Board A has 5 noughts and 5 crosses; board B has 9 noughts and 1 cross. Draw one mark at random. On which board is an `X` more surprising?

<TicTacToeBoards />

On board A a cross has probability $\tfrac12$; on board B, $\tfrac1{10}$. The cross is five times rarer on B, and seeing it there should surprise us more: surprise must grow as the probability falls. A first attempt is $1/P(x)$: it gives 2 on board A and 10 on board B, which ranks them correctly.

It fails on a certain outcome. A rigged coin that always lands heads has $P(H) = 1$, and heads should then carry no surprise at all, yet $1/P(H) = 1$. Taking the logarithm moves that point to zero. The **surprise**, or self-information, of an outcome is

$$
s(x) = \log\frac{1}{P(x)} = -\log P(x).
$$

It is $0$ when $P(x) = 1$ and grows without bound as $P(x) \to 0$. An outcome of probability zero never happens, so its infinite surprise is never needed.

<SurpriseCurve />

The base of the logarithm only sets the unit. Base 2 gives **bits**, in which one fair binary choice carries exactly 1 bit; base $e$ gives **nats**, and $1 \text{ bit} = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$ nats. The coin and board examples below use bits, the cross-entropy examples nats.

For a biased coin with $P(H) = 0.9$ and $P(T) = 0.1$, heads carries $\log_2 \frac{1}{0.9} \approx 0.152$ bits and tails $\log_2 \frac{1}{0.1} \approx 3.322$ bits. The rare outcome carries the most surprise.

### Surprise is additive

Flip the coin three times and observe `H H T`. The flips are independent, so the probability of the sequence is a product, and its surprise is a sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s(\text{HHT})
&= \log_2\frac{1}{0.9\cdot 0.9\cdot 0.1} \\
&= -\log_2 0.9 - \log_2 0.9 - \log_2 0.1 \\
&\approx 0.152 + 0.152 + 3.322 \\
&= 3.626 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<SurpriseAdditivity />

No other continuous function turns products into sums. If $s(pq) = s(p) + s(q)$ for all $p, q \in (0, 1]$, then $g(t) = s(e^{-t})$ satisfies $g(t + u) = g(t) + g(u)$ for all $t, u \ge 0$. Adding $t$ to itself $n$ times gives $g(nt) = n\,g(t)$; putting $t = 1/n$ gives $g(1/n) = g(1)/n$, and so $g(m/n) = \tfrac mn\, g(1)$ for every positive rational. A continuous $g$ is fixed by its values on the rationals, so $g(t) = ct$ with $c = g(1)$. Hence $s(p) = c\log\frac1p$, with $c > 0$ so that rare outcomes are surprising; choosing $c$ is choosing the base. Shannon proved the matching uniqueness result for entropy itself ([Shannon, 1948](#references), Theorem 2).

## Entropy: expected surprise

Over 100 flips of the biased coin we expect about 90 heads and 10 tails, for a total surprise of about $90 \cdot 0.152 + 10 \cdot 3.322 \approx 46.9$ bits. Per flip that is

$$
0.9 \cdot 0.152 + 0.1 \cdot 3.322 \approx 0.469 \text{ bits}.
$$

Each probability multiplies its own surprise: this is the expected value of the surprise $s(X)$ of a random outcome $X \sim P$. The expected surprise is the **entropy** ([Shannon, 1948](#references)):

$$
H(P) = \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{P(x)} = -\sum_x P(x)\log P(x).
$$

Outcomes with $P(x) = 0$ are left out of the sum, which is the convention $0 \log \frac10 = 0$, the limit of $p\log\frac1p$ as $p \to 0$: with $p = e^{-t}$ the product is $t\,e^{-t}$, which tends to 0 as $t \to \infty$ ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), §2.1). The coin that always lands heads has entropy $0$.

For the boards,

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_A &= \tfrac12\log_2 2 + \tfrac12\log_2 2 = 1 \text{ bit}, \\
H_B &= \tfrac{9}{10}\log_2\frac{10}{9} + \tfrac{1}{10}\log_2 10 \approx 0.469 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Board B has the biased coin's distribution and so its entropy. The balanced board A is less predictable, and higher entropy means more uncertainty. For two outcomes no distribution exceeds 1 bit, and only the uniform one reaches it; the next section proves this.

To compute an entropy we must know the real distribution $P$. When we fit a generative model to data, we never do.

## Cross-entropy: surprise under a wrong belief

Back to the opening coin. The model believes it is fair, $Q = (0.5, 0.5)$, while in reality it is rigged, $P = (0.99, 0.01)$. Ten heads in a row have very different probabilities under the two:

| | Probability of 10 heads | Surprise (nats) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model $Q$ | $0.5^{10} \approx 0.001$ | $6.93$ |
| Reality $P$ | $0.99^{10} \approx 0.90$ | $0.10$ |

<ModelVsReality />

The outcome was ordinary; the surprise comes from the wrong belief. The **cross-entropy** $H(P, Q)$ is the average surprise when outcomes come from $P$ while we measure surprise with $Q$:

$$
H(P, Q) = \sum_{x} P(x)\,\log\frac{1}{Q(x)} .
$$

The weights say how often $x$ happens, and the logarithms say how surprised the believer is to see it.

<CrossEntropyReading />

If $Q = P$, cross-entropy is entropy: $H(P, P) = H(P)$. Otherwise it is larger. **Gibbs' inequality** states

$$
H(P, Q) \ge H(P),
$$

with equality only when $Q = P$ ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), Theorem 2.6.3). Believing in a wrong distribution can only raise the average surprise. The proof takes one line once the difference has a name, in the next section.

Gibbs' inequality bounds entropy from above. Let $Q$ be uniform over $|\mathcal X|$ outcomes. Then $\log\frac{1}{Q(x)} = \log|\mathcal X|$ for every $x$, so $H(P, Q) = \log|\mathcal X|$ and therefore $H(P) \le \log|\mathcal X|$, with equality only for the uniform $P$ ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), Theorem 2.6.4). The Mathematics course reaches the same result with [Lagrange multipliers](/notes/math/lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt#maximum-entropy).

Cross-entropy is not symmetric, and the first argument is always the distribution that generates the outcomes. For one flip, in nats:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P, Q) &= 0.99\ln 2 + 0.01\ln 2 \approx 0.69, \\
H(Q, P) &= 0.5\ln\frac{1}{0.99} + 0.5\ln\frac{1}{0.01} \approx 2.31 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Believing that a fair coin is rigged is punished harder. Tails then come half the time, and the rigged belief finds every one of them very surprising.

## Kullback–Leibler divergence

Cross-entropy mixes two sources of surprise: what the real distribution produces anyway, and the extra caused by the wrong model. Subtracting the entropy isolates the second:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P, Q) - H(P)
&= \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{Q(x)} \\
&\quad - \sum_x P(x)\log\frac{1}{P(x)} \\
&= \sum_{x} P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \\
&= D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q).
\end{aligned}
$$

The KL divergence ([Kullback and Leibler, 1951](#references)) is the extra surprise from using $Q$ as the model instead of $P$. The sum on the right is the general definition, and the subtraction holds when both terms are finite. A term with $P(x) = 0$ contributes $0$. A term with $P(x) > 0$ and $Q(x) = 0$ is $+\infty$, and so is the divergence. KL is finite only when $Q$ gives positive probability to every outcome to which $P$ does, that is, when $P$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $Q$ ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), §2.3). The [next note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#a-common-support) shows what this condition does in training.

A second reading comes from comparing hypotheses. Bayes' rule for two hypotheses $P$ and $Q$, divided one by the other, cancels the evidence $\Pr(x)$; taking logarithms turns the product into a sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{\Pr(P \mid x)}{\Pr(Q \mid x)}
&= \log\frac{\Pr(P)\, p(x)}{\Pr(Q)\, q(x)} \\
&= \log\frac{\Pr(P)}{\Pr(Q)} + \log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

One observation $x$ adds $\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ to the log-odds of $P$ against $Q$: the **weight of evidence** it carries for $P$. $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$ is the expected weight of evidence per observation when $P$ is true. Because it is never negative, data push belief towards the true hypothesis on average ([Murphy, 2023](#references), §5.1.3).

<aside class="callout">

<strong>Why KL is non-negative.</strong> If $Q(x) = 0$ at some $x$ with $P(x) > 0$, the divergence is $+\infty$. Otherwise sum over the outcomes with $P(x) > 0$ and apply Jensen's inequality to the concave logarithm, with weights $P(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)
&= \sum_{P(x) > 0} P(x)\log\frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \\
&\le \log \sum_{P(x) > 0} Q(x) \\
&\le \log 1 = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The logarithm is strictly concave, so the first inequality is an equality only if $Q(x)/P(x)$ is one constant wherever $P(x) > 0$. The second is an equality only if $Q$ has no mass elsewhere, which makes the constant 1. So $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) \ge 0$, with equality only when $Q = P$. Adding $H(P)$ to both sides gives Gibbs' inequality.

</aside>

So KL is non-negative and zero only when $Q = P$. It is neither symmetric nor does it satisfy the triangle inequality ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), §2.3), so it is called a divergence and not a distance. For the two coins, in nats with four decimals so that the parts add up,

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) &\approx 0.6931 - 0.0560 = 0.6371, \\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,P) &\approx 2.3076 - 0.6931 = 1.6145 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<CrossEntropyDecomposition />

The two boards give every mark positive probability, so their divergence is finite in both directions, and its value depends on the direction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(A\,\|\,B) &= \tfrac12\log_2\frac{1/2}{9/10} + \tfrac12\log_2\frac{1/2}{1/10} \\
&\approx 0.737 \text{ bits},\\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(B\,\|\,A) &= \tfrac{9}{10}\log_2\frac{9/10}{1/2} + \tfrac{1}{10}\log_2\frac{1/10}{1/2} \\
&\approx 0.531 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

As a check, $H(B, A) = H_B + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(B\,\|\,A) = 0.469 + 0.531 = 1$ bit: measuring surprise with the uniform board costs exactly one bit per draw, whatever the true board.

<BoardsKL />

The same distributions break the triangle inequality. Read O as heads and X as tails and compare board A with the rigged coin $P = (0.99, 0.01)$. Directly, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(A\,\|\,P) \approx 2.329$ bits. Through board B, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(A\,\|\,B) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(B\,\|\,P) \approx 0.737 + 0.208 = 0.945$ bits, which is less.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Densities.</strong> For continuous distributions with densities $p$ and $q$ the sums become integrals, and not every reading survives. A density can exceed 1, so $-\log p(x)$ is negative where $p(x) > 1$, and the differential entropy $H(p) = -\int p\log p\,dx$ can be negative. For $\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)$, $-\ln p(x) = \tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) + x^2/(2\sigma^2)$, and averaging with $\mathbb E[x^2] = \sigma^2$ gives $H = \tfrac12\ln(2\pi e\,\sigma^2)$; for $\sigma = 0.1$ it is $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi e \cdot 0.01) \approx -0.88$ nats. The relative quantities keep their meaning. $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = \int p\log\frac{p}{q}\,dx$ is still non-negative and zero only when $p = q$ almost everywhere, by the Jensen argument of the callout above with integrals in place of sums, and $H(p, q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$ whenever $H(p)$ is finite ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), §8.1, §8.5–8.6).</aside>

## Why cross-entropy is the loss we minimize

For the true data distribution $P$ and a model $Q_\theta$, the decomposition reads

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q_\theta) = H(P, Q_\theta) - H(P).
$$

The entropy of the data does not depend on $\theta$, so minimizing KL and minimizing cross-entropy have the same solution. We never need the value of $H(P)$, which for images would be a hard estimation problem of its own. Cross-entropy is the part we can estimate from samples $x_1, \dots, x_N \sim P$, by the average negative log-likelihood:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P, Q_\theta) &= \mathbb E_{x\sim P}\big[-\log Q_\theta(x)\big] \\
&\approx -\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \log Q_\theta(x_i).
\end{aligned}
$$

Minimizing it is **maximum-likelihood estimation**. For discrete outcomes the average is exactly the cross-entropy $H(\hat P, Q_\theta)$ between the empirical distribution $\hat P$, which puts $1/N$ on each sample, and the model: $\hat P(x)$ is the share of samples equal to $x$, so $\sum_x \hat P(x)\big(-\log Q_\theta(x)\big)$ adds $-\log Q_\theta(x_i)/N$ once for each sample. Hence maximum likelihood minimizes $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat P\,\|\,Q_\theta)$ ([Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016](#references), §5.5). As $N$ grows the average converges to $H(P, Q_\theta)$ by the law of large numbers. Minimizing forward KL, minimizing cross-entropy and maximizing likelihood are one objective under three names: exactly for the empirical distribution, and in the limit for $P$ itself ([Isachenko, lecture 1](#references)). The standing of maximum likelihood rests on asymptotic results, and they need conditions. The model family must contain $P$ and be identifiable, its support must not depend on $\theta$, the log-likelihood must be smooth and the true parameter must lie inside the parameter set. Then, with the model fixed and $N$ growing, the estimate converges to the true $\theta$ and, for a single parameter, is approximately normal with variance $1/(N I(\theta))$, where $I$ is the Fisher information. That is the Cramér–Rao bound, below which no unbiased estimator goes, so the estimate is **asymptotically efficient**. The Probability course states these results in [Fisher information](/notes/probability/maximum-likelihood#fisher-information). If no member of the family equals $P$, the estimate converges instead to the member closest to $P$ in forward KL ([when maximum likelihood goes wrong](/notes/probability/maximum-likelihood#when-maximum-likelihood-goes-wrong)). With few samples per parameter none of this is promised, and that is where priors enter ([BMML lecture notes, 2026](#references), lecture 1).

The same argument explains the cross-entropy loss of classifiers and language models, applied to the conditional distribution of the label or next token given the input. Each training example supplies one observed label, a one-hot vector, and the average negative log-likelihood estimates $\mathbb E_x\, H\big(P(\cdot \mid x), Q(\cdot \mid x)\big)$. Soft targets, such as [label smoothing](/notes/deep-learning/losses-and-regularization#label-smoothing) or a [teacher model's probabilities](/notes/deep-learning/model-compression#knowledge-distillation), replace the one-hot vector by another estimate of $P(\cdot \mid x)$.

The direction matters. Forward KL averages over the data, so it heavily penalizes a model that gives low probability to points the data produces, and a model that gives zero probability to one of them pays an infinite loss ([Stanford CS236 notes](#references)). Generative models differ mostly in how they deal with $\log Q_\theta(x)$ being hard to compute: VAEs bound it, normalizing flows compute it exactly, GANs avoid it, and diffusion models bound it again.

## Jensen–Shannon divergence

The Jensen–Shannon divergence ([Lin, 1991](#references)) builds a symmetric and bounded quantity from the same pieces. It compares both distributions with their mixture $M = \tfrac12(P + Q)$:

$$
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P \,\|\, Q) = \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, M) + \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q \,\|\, M).
$$

The mixture is positive wherever $P$ or $Q$ is, so both terms are always finite, and both are non-negative, with $D_{\mathrm{JS}} = 0$ only when $P = Q$. For the upper bound, $P(x) \le P(x) + Q(x) = 2M(x)$, so every log ratio in $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,M)$ is at most $\log 2$, and equality needs $Q(x) = 0$ wherever $P(x) > 0$. The same holds for the second term. Hence

$$
0 \le D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P \,\|\, Q) \le \ln 2 \text{ nats} = 1 \text{ bit},
$$

with the maximum exactly when $P$ and $Q$ do not overlap. For the two coins, $D_{\mathrm{JS}} \approx 0.193$ nats, or $0.279$ bits.

Its square root $\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{JS}}}$ is a true metric on probability distributions: symmetric, zero only for $P = Q$, and with the triangle inequality ([Endres and Schindelin, 2003](#references); [HSE, lecture 1](#references)). The proof is beyond this course; the local picture shows why a square root is needed. For $Q = P + \delta$ with a small $\delta$, a second-order expansion of the logarithm gives $D_{\mathrm{JS}} \approx \sum_x \delta(x)^2 / (8P(x))$, a squared length, like the square of a Euclidean distance. A squared distance breaks the triangle inequality (for points 0, 1 and 2 on a line, $4 > 1 + 1$), and its root restores it.

JS matters twice later. In the [next note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#js-does-not-cover-the-modes-either) it is one of the losses compared on a two-mode target. In the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#step-2-substitute-the-optimal-discriminator-and-recognize-js), the original GAN objective with an optimal discriminator equals $2\,D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P\,\|\,Q) - 2\ln 2$, with $P$ the data and $Q$ the generator ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#references)). Its saturation at $\ln 2$ for non-overlapping distributions then explains why GAN training can stall ([where it breaks](/notes/generative-models/gans#where-it-breaks-supports-that-do-not-overlap); [Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017](#references)).

KL and JS are two members of a larger family. Every **f-divergence** $\int q\, f(p/q)\,dx$ with a convex $f$ and $f(1) = 0$ compares densities through their ratio ([HSE, lecture 1](#references)). Substituting shows the special cases: $f(u) = u\ln u$ gives $\int p \ln\frac pq\,dx = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$, $f(u) = -\ln u$ gives the reverse KL, and $f(u) = \tfrac12|u - 1|$ gives the total variation distance $\tfrac12\int |p - q|\,dx$. The [f-GAN](/notes/generative-models/gans#f-gan) lists the $f$ of JS and of other divergences and trains with any of them. The [Wasserstein GAN](/notes/generative-models/wgan) leaves the family and measures how far mass has to move, which stays informative when the supports do not overlap.

## What you should remember

- Surprise $-\log P(x)$; entropy $H(P)$ is its average under $P$.
- Cross-entropy $H(P, Q)$ averages under $P$ the surprise measured with $Q$; $H(P, Q) \ge H(P)$.
- $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = H(P, Q) - H(P) \ge 0$; infinite if $Q$ misses mass of $P$; not symmetric.
- Maximum likelihood = minimum cross-entropy = minimum forward KL from the data to the model.
- JS compares both with their mixture: symmetric, at most $\ln 2$, and flat once the supports separate.

## Code

The numbers of this note with SciPy, whose `entropy(p)` returns $H(P)$ and `entropy(p, q)` returns $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$, in nats unless a base is given.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import entropy             # entropy(p) = H(P); entropy(p, q) = KL(P || Q)

P, Q = np.array([0.99, 0.01]), np.array([0.5, 0.5])    # rigged coin, fair belief
A, B = np.array([0.5, 0.5]), np.array([0.9, 0.1])      # the two boards

cross = lambda p, q: -(p * np.log(q)).sum()            # H(P, Q) in nats
print(round(cross(P, Q), 4), round(entropy(P) + entropy(P, Q), 4))   # H(P,Q) = H(P) + KL
print(round(entropy(P, Q), 4), round(entropy(Q, P), 4))              # KL is not symmetric
print(round(entropy(B, base=2), 3), round(entropy(A, B, base=2), 3), round(entropy(B, A, base=2), 3))

M = (P + Q) / 2
js = 0.5 * entropy(P, M) + 0.5 * entropy(Q, M)
print(round(js, 3), round(js / np.log(2), 3))                         # nats, bits
print(entropy([1.0, 0.0]), entropy(P, [1.0, 0.0]))                   # 0 log 0 = 0; Q misses mass: inf
```

```text
0.6931 0.6931
0.6371 1.6145
0.469 0.737 0.531
0.193 0.279
0.0 inf
```
