The seminar pauses between autoencoders and evaluation metrics to revisit the neural-network operations used in the demonstrations. These notes explain how activations are normalized, why derivatives can grow or shrink through a chain, and what a convolution learns.

## Batch normalization starts with current batch statistics

For one feature, collect the values $x_1,\ldots,x_m$ used to calculate training statistics. The handwritten formulas are

$$
\mu_B=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_i,
\qquad
v_B=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}(x_i-\mu_B)^2,
$$

$$
\hat x_i=\frac{x_i-\mu_B}{\sqrt{v_B+\varepsilon}},
\qquad\varepsilon>0.
$$

The denominator uses a **variance plus a small constant**, followed by a square root. The constant avoids division by zero when a feature has no variation in the batch. The $1/m$ variance is the quantity used for normalization here; it is not the $1/(m-1)$ unbiased sample-variance estimator.

For a convolutional tensor with shape $N\times C\times H\times W$, ordinary BatchNorm2d computes these statistics separately for each channel, pooling its $N H W$ values. It does not mix the channel means into one global statistic.

A standard BatchNorm layer then applies learned per-channel parameters:

$$
y_{n,c,u,v}=\gamma_c\hat x_{n,c,u,v}+\beta_c.
$$

The scale $\gamma_c$ and shift $\beta_c$ let the network learn an appropriate range after normalization. This affine step expands the scan's normalization fragment; it follows the [original BatchNorm algorithm](https://arxiv.org/html/1502.03167v3#S3).

<BatchNormStatistics />

Before the affine step, the same training batch has normalized mean zero and variance

$$
\frac{v_B}{v_B+\varepsilon}.
$$

Thus its variance approaches one when $v_B$ is large relative to $\varepsilon$, rather than being exactly one for every input. After applying $\gamma_c$ and $\beta_c$, the output is not constrained to zero mean and unit variance. BatchNorm also does not turn arbitrary activations into a Gaussian distribution.

## Training statistics and evaluation statistics differ

With PyTorch's default running-statistics setup, training uses the current batch's mean and variance and updates running estimates. Evaluation uses those stored estimates. The affine parameters remain learned parameters in both modes.

This distinction matters for generative models. In training mode, a sample's output can depend on the other samples in its batch. In evaluation mode with fixed running statistics, that dependence is removed from this BatchNorm operation. Small or unrepresentative training batches can also give noisy statistics.

There are two separate controls:

- **Model mode:** calling the model's evaluation method changes the behavior of modules such as BatchNorm and dropout.
- **Gradient recording:** disabling autograd avoids recording derivative computations; it does not switch those modules to evaluation mode.

With running-statistics tracking disabled, BatchNorm uses batch statistics in evaluation too. PyTorch also uses an unbiased batch-variance estimate when updating its running variance, while the training normalization above uses the biased estimate. These implementation details are additions to the scan; see the [BatchNorm2d documentation](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/generated/torch.nn.BatchNorm2d.html) and the [autograd mode explanation](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/main/notes/autograd.html#evaluation-mode-nn-module-eval).

Normalization is one architectural tool for training. Its presence alone does not establish stable optimization, good reconstruction or useful generated samples.

## Gradients multiply through a chain

The sketch on page 14 shows a repeated recurrent weight of $w=2$, with factors $2,4,8,\ldots$. To make that example exact, first use the scalar linear recurrence

$$
h_k=w h_{k-1}.
$$

Its state sensitivity is

$$
\frac{\partial h_K}{\partial h_0}=w^K.
$$

For a loss depending on the final state,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_0}
=\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_K}\,w^K.
$$

If $w=2$, that path doubles at each step. If $w=1/2$, it halves. Over a long chain, the first can create very large derivatives, while the second weakens the learning signal reaching early states.

<GradientChainSketch />

This example concerns a derivative with respect to an earlier **state**. Because $w$ is shared across steps, its parameter derivative includes all occurrences of the weight:

$$
h_K=w^K h_0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial h_K}{\partial w}=K w^{K-1}h_0
\quad(K\ge1).
$$

The $w^K$ sketch should therefore not be copied as the complete gradient of every shared parameter.

## Nonlinear networks require the full Jacobian

For a recurrence with an activation function,

$$
h_k=\phi(a_k),
\qquad
a_k=W h_{k-1}+U x_k+b,
$$

the local state Jacobian is

$$
J_k=\frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}}
=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\phi'(a_k)\bigr)W.
$$

The total state derivative is the ordered product $J_KJ_{K-1}\cdots J_1$. Its norm satisfies

$$
\left\|\frac{\partial h_K}{\partial h_0}\right\|_2
\le\prod_{k=1}^{K}\|J_k\|_2.
$$

Repeated contraction can make long-range derivatives small. Large local norms allow growth, but this upper bound alone does not guarantee that every gradient direction grows. Activation derivatives and the directions propagated through the matrices also matter.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial qualification.</strong> The scan summarizes the problem using very large or very small individual weights. In a matrix-valued nonlinear network, the relevant quantities are products of full Jacobians, including activation derivatives. One large matrix entry does not by itself prove exploding loss gradients, and small entries do not by themselves prove contraction.</aside>

The [activation-function note](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders#activation-functions) gives familiar examples. A sigmoid can have a very small derivative when it saturates. ReLU has derivative one on its positive branch and zero on its negative branch, so a zero derivative can block a path even when its weight is large.

For a simple gradient-descent update $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla_\theta L$, large gradient norms can cause large parameter steps. A weak gradient signal can make learning through a long path slow within a finite training budget. The effect on loss also depends on the learning rate, optimizer and local curvature.

Gradient-norm clipping limits an overly large update signal; it does not restore a vanished derivative. This practical addition and the Jacobian analysis are discussed by [Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio (2013)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v28/pascanu13.html).

## A convolution learns kernels across channels

The aside on page 15 describes kernels that can respond to lines, spots and textures. For a standard ungrouped 2D convolution, the trainable tensor has shape

$$
W\in\mathbb R^{C_{\mathrm{out}}\times C_{\mathrm{in}}\times k_H\times k_W},
\qquad b\in\mathbb R^{C_{\mathrm{out}}}.
$$

Every output channel has a spatial kernel for each input channel. It sums their filtered maps and adds one bias. Using output-first indices,

$$
y_j=\sum_{c=1}^{C_{\mathrm{in}}}W_{j,c}\star x_c+b_j,
$$

where $\star$ denotes spatial **cross-correlation**, the operation implemented by ordinary deep-learning convolution layers without reversing the kernel.

With stride $(s_H,s_W)$, dilation $(d_H,d_W)$ and a padded input $\tilde x$, the operation is

$$
y_{n,j,u,v}
=b_j+\sum_c\sum_{a=0}^{k_H-1}\sum_{r=0}^{k_W-1}
W_{j,c,a,r}\,
\tilde x_{n,c,u s_H+a d_H,\;v s_W+r d_W}.
$$

The same kernel weights are reused across spatial positions. Learning them changes which local patterns a channel responds to; it does not assign a separate set of weights to every pixel location.

<ConvolutionChannels />

Kernel size, stride, padding and dilation are architecture choices in this setup. The kernel values and optional biases are learned through the loss. The scan's word “shape” refers to the pattern represented by the weights, rather than changing their tensor dimensions during ordinary training. Grouped convolutions restrict which input channels connect to an output; the all-channel sum above assumes one group. See the [Conv2d definition](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/generated/torch.nn.Conv2d.html).
