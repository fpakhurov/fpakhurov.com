An [autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders) learns to reconstruct observed inputs. That objective alone does not specify a distribution for new latent codes, or constrain every gap between the training codes. Good reconstructions therefore do not establish that drawing a random code will produce useful new data.

The **variational autoencoder** (VAE) of [Kingma and Welling (2014)](#references), proposed at the same time by [Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra (2014)](#references), builds that in. Each image is encoded to a Gaussian instead of a point, and all these Gaussians are pulled toward one fixed distribution. It is also the first neural model of the course trained by a bound on maximum likelihood, the objective the [entropy note](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#why-cross-entropy-is-the-loss-we-minimize) singled out.

## From autoencoders to generation

The autoencoder maps $x$ to $z$ and back to $\hat x$. Drawing $z$ from a convenient Gaussian is an extra modeling choice: the reconstruction objective has not required the codes to follow it. Fitting a density to the codes is possible, but its fit and the decoder's behavior on its samples need separate evaluation.

A small constructed example exposes the missing constraint. Take three scalar training observations $x\in\{-2,0,2\}$ and encoder $z=x$. Both decoders $g_0(z)=z$ and $g_1(z)=z+\tfrac34z(z^2-4)$ reconstruct every training point exactly. At the unseen code $z=1$, however, they output $1$ and $-1.25$. The training loss cannot choose between them.

This is an identifiability example with explicit functions, not a trained digit experiment. Architectural smoothness or additional regularization can restrict the alternatives; reconstruction alone does not.

## The latent-variable model and its posterior

Start from the simplest model of this kind. **Probabilistic PCA** draws a code $z \in \mathbb R^d$ from a **prior** and places the image $x \in \mathbb R^D$, with $d < D$, near a linear function of it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &\sim \mathcal N(0, I), \\
x \mid z &\sim \mathcal N(Wz + \mu, \sigma^2 I) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Everything is Gaussian, so both the evidence and the posterior have closed forms. The image $x = Wz + \mu + \varepsilon$, with noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 I)$, is an affine map of the Gaussian pair $(z, \varepsilon)$, so $x \sim \mathcal N(\mu, WW^\top + \sigma^2 I)$ by the [affine-map rule](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps). Completing the square in $z$ (folded below) gives the posterior

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(z \mid x) &= \mathcal N\big(M^{-1} W^\top (x - \mu),\ \sigma^2 M^{-1}\big), \\
M &= W^\top W + \sigma^2 I .
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the posterior</summary>

Bayes' rule keeps only the factors that depend on $z$; the rest is a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(z \mid x)
&= \log p(x \mid z) + \log p(z) + \text{const} \\
&= -\frac{\lVert x - \mu - Wz\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{z^\top z}{2} + \text{const} \\
&= -\tfrac12\, z^\top \frac{M}{\sigma^2}\, z + z^\top \frac{W^\top (x - \mu)}{\sigma^2} + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The third line expands the square and collects $z^\top z + z^\top W^\top W z/\sigma^2 = z^\top (M/\sigma^2)\, z$. A Gaussian $\mathcal N(m, P^{-1})$ has the log density $-\tfrac12 z^\top P z + z^\top P m + \text{const}$. Matching the quadratic term gives $P = M/\sigma^2$, and matching the linear term, $Pm = W^\top (x - \mu)/\sigma^2$, gives $m = M^{-1} W^\top (x - \mu)$.

</details>

Maximum likelihood sets $\mu$ to the data mean, where the gradient $\sum_n C^{-1}(x_n - \mu)$ of a Gaussian log-likelihood vanishes for any covariance $C$. EM fits $W$ and $\sigma^2$ by alternating two exact steps: set $q(z)$ to this posterior, then maximize the expected log joint ([EM as coordinate ascent](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca#em-as-coordinate-ascent-on-the-evidence-lower-bound)). Probabilistic PCA is thus a VAE with a linear decoder and an exact Gaussian encoder ([probabilistic PCA](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca)), diagonal when the columns of $W$ are orthogonal and $M$ is diagonal.

A linear subspace can be too restrictive for complex observations. The VAE keeps this two-stage model and replaces $Wz+\mu$ with a neural network. In the Gaussian version used here, the **decoder** $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ has a mean, and possibly a variance, supplied by that network. This PPCA-to-VAE route follows BMML lecture 6, §§6.1–6.2; the adapted exposition is credited under its [MIT documentation license](/licenses/bmml-vae.txt). The model distribution is the marginal of $p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)$ ([joint and marginal densities](/notes/probability/joint-distributions)), called the **evidence**:

$$
p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz .
$$

Maximum likelihood maximizes $\log p_\theta(x)$ over the training images. The reverse question, which codes explain a given image, is answered by the **posterior**:

$$
p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)}{p_\theta(x)} .
$$

A general neural decoder leaves no closed-form integral, so an exact posterior E-step is generally unavailable. Approximate inference will replace it.

<DataLatentMaps />

Why not estimate the integral by sampling? Draw $z_1, \dots, z_n$ from the prior and average $p_\theta(x \mid z_k)$. The $n$ terms are independent with mean $p(x)$, so the average is unbiased, with variance $\big(\mathbb E_{p(z)}[p(x \mid z)^2] - p(x)^2\big)/n$. Relative to $p(x)^2$ it is

$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}\big[\hat p(x)\big]}{p(x)^2} = \frac1n \left(\frac{\mathbb E_{p(z)}\big[p(x \mid z)^2\big]}{p(x)^2} - 1\right) .
$$

When prior samples rarely explain an observation well, a few large likelihoods can dominate the average. Consider this explicit example: let each of $d$ coordinates be independent, with $z_j \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $x_j \mid z_j \sim \mathcal N(z_j, 0.1)$. At $x = 0$ every coordinate multiplies the ratio inside the brackets by $11/\sqrt{21} \approx 2.40$, and $n \ge 100\big((11/\sqrt{21})^d-1\big)$ samples bring the relative error, the square root of the relative variance, to at most 10%:

| $d$ | ratio $(11/\sqrt{21})^d$ | samples for a 10% relative error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2.4 | 141 |
| 10 | 6350.83 | 634,983 |
| 100 | $1.1 \cdot 10^{38}$ | $1.1 \cdot 10^{40}$ |

<details>
<summary>Where 2.40 comes from</summary>

For one coordinate with noise variance $v = 0.1$, $p(x = 0 \mid z) = (2\pi v)^{-1/2} e^{-z^2/(2v)}$ and, by the affine-map rule, $p(x = 0) = \big(2\pi(1 + v)\big)^{-1/2}$. The square of the likelihood is again a Gaussian shape in $z$, and the Gaussian integral $\int e^{-a z^2/2} dz = \sqrt{2\pi/a}$ with $a = 2/v + 1$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{p(z)}\big[p(0 \mid z)^2\big]
&= \frac{1}{2\pi v} \int \frac{e^{-z^2/v - z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, dz \\
&= \frac{1}{2\pi v}\, \Big(1 + \frac 2v\Big)^{-1/2}, \\
\frac{\mathbb E_{p(z)}\big[p(0 \mid z)^2\big]}{p(0)^2}
&= \frac{1 + v}{\sqrt{v^2 + 2v}} = \frac{1.1}{\sqrt{0.21}} \approx 2.40 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The coordinates are independent, so with $d$ of them both numerator and denominator are products over coordinates, and the ratio is $(11/\sqrt{21})^d$.

</details>

The cost grows exponentially in this particular independent-coordinate construction, not in every latent-variable model. It illustrates the difficulty of prior sampling discussed by [Isachenko (2025)](#references), lecture 3. An encoder can concentrate samples in regions that help explain the observation.

## Variational approximation and the ELBO

The way out keeps EM's lower bound and gives up the exact E-step: $q$ is restricted to a simple family and optimized together with $\theta$. Replacing an intractable posterior by the closest member of a simple family is **variational inference**, the subject of the [variational inference note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference) of the Bayesian Methods course.

The plainest choice gives each training image $x_i$ its own diagonal Gaussian $q(z_i) = \mathcal N(m_i, S_i)$. That works, but the variational parameters grow with the data, and every new image needs its own optimization. The VAE replaces the table of $(m_i, S_i)$ by an **encoder** network with parameters $\varphi$:

$$
q_\varphi(z \mid x) = \mathcal N\big(\mu_\varphi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma_\varphi^2(x)\big) .
$$

One network that outputs $q$ for any $x$ is **amortized** inference: the cost of inference is paid once, in training the encoder ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), section 2.1; [amortization in numbers](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference#amortized-inference-and-the-vae)).

Encoder and decoder are trained together by maximizing the **evidence lower bound** (ELBO), derived [below](#where-the-elbo-comes-from):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x) ={}& \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z \mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big] \\
&- D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)\big) \\
\le{}& \log p_\theta(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term is the expected **reconstruction log likelihood**. For a fixed decoder with a finite maximum, concentrating $q$ near a maximizer of $\log p_\theta(x\mid z)$ can improve this term alone: an average cannot exceed the maximum. This does not require a unique best code, and a flat decoder is a counterexample to inevitable concentration. The second term, the **KL penalty**, charges for departures from the prior, including variance shrinking to zero. The two-term interpretation adapts BMML lecture 6 §6.3 under the [same notice](/licenses/bmml-vae.txt).

<ElboTerms />

With a Gaussian decoder of fixed variance $s^2$, whose network outputs the mean $\hat x(z)$, the reconstruction term is a squared error:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x \mid z) = \frac{\lVert x - \hat x(z)\rVert^2}{2s^2} + \frac D2 \log(2\pi s^2) .
$$

The last term does not depend on the networks. With $s^2 = \tfrac12$ the expected squared error, **summed** over the $D$ pixels, plus the KL term is the negative ELBO up to a constant; a mean over pixels corresponds to $s^2 = D/2$. Any other $s^2$ reweights the two terms: multiplying by $2s^2$ gives the squared error plus $2s^2$ times the KL, the weight $\beta$ of the [β-VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae-variants#β-vae-a-weight-on-the-kl-term).

If the decoder also learns its variance, the log-variance term must remain. For one variance shared by all observations and coordinates, its optimum depends on the **average expected squared residual**, not on one image's residual. If all relevant residuals can vanish, a Gaussian likelihood can become unbounded as the variance tends to zero; a variance floor avoids that degeneracy. Binary observations can instead use a Bernoulli decoder and binary cross-entropy ([the two readings](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders#reconstruction-loss)).

<details>
<summary>The loss with a learned shared variance</summary>

Hold the encoder and decoder mean fixed and let $R=\frac1N\sum_i\mathbb E_{q_i}\lVert x_i-\hat x(z)\rVert^2$. The mean negative reconstruction log likelihood, up to a constant, is $R/(2s^2)+(D/2)\log s^2$. Differentiating in $s^2$ gives $-R/(2s^4)+D/(2s^2)$, so for $R>0$ the unique optimum is $s^2=R/D$. Substitution gives $(D/2)\log(R/D)$ plus a constant. This is the logarithm of the overall expected MSE; it is not an average of separate per-image logarithms.

</details>

With fixed decoder variance $s^2=\tfrac12$, one sampled code per image gives an unbiased estimate of the negative ELBO up to a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\text{VAE}}(x) ={}& \big\lVert x - \hat x(z)\big\rVert^2 \\
&+ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2) \,\|\, \mathcal N(0, I)\big),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, where $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are the encoder's outputs for $x$ and the variance is applied coordinate-wise. Neither term contains the posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ or the evidence: the loss needs only the encoder, the prior and the decoder, which we can all evaluate.

The weights $\theta$ and $\varphi$ are point estimates, fitted by stochastic gradient ascent on the ELBO like the parameters of an M-step ([the VAE as variational EM](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference#amortized-inference-and-the-vae)). Only the code is integrated over and sampled, as are the noisy images of the later diffusion models. A distribution over the weights too would make a [Bayesian neural network](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-deep-learning#bayes-by-backprop).

## The reparameterization trick

For a sum of $n$ per-image ELBOs, an unbiased minibatch gradient is $n$ times the **average** per-image gradient in a uniformly sampled minibatch. For the mean objective no such factor is needed. Assume differentiation can pass through the expectation and the density has parameter-independent support. For a function $f$ with no direct $\varphi$ dependence, differentiating the encoder density gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\varphi\, \mathbb E_{q_\varphi}\big[f(z)\big]
&= \int f(z)\, \nabla_\varphi q_\varphi(z \mid x)\, dz \\
&= \mathbb E_{q_\varphi}\big[f(z)\, \nabla_\varphi \log q_\varphi(z \mid x)\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

This **score-function** or REINFORCE estimator can be used beyond Gaussian distributions when those regularity conditions hold. Its variance can be large because it uses $f(z)$ multiplied by a zero-mean score, rather than the local derivative of $f$. For $q=\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $f(z)=z^2$ and $\mu=\sigma=1$, the single-sample gradient in $\mu$ is $(1+\varepsilon)^2\varepsilon$: its mean is $2$ and variance $30$. The pathwise gradient $2(1+\varepsilon)$ has the same mean and variance $4$. These moments are recomputed by the local verifier; this comparison is not a universal variance ordering. If $f$ also depends directly on $\varphi$, its direct derivative must be added.

A Gaussian $q$ also permits a pathwise estimator. Reparameterization moves the randomness into an input, as in [sampling any Gaussian from standard noise](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions#sampling-from-standard-noise-to-any-gaussian):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon &\sim \mathcal N(0, I), \\
z &= \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\odot$ is the element-wise product. The distribution of $z$ is unchanged, but now $\partial z_j / \partial \mu_j = 1$ and $\partial z_j / \partial \sigma_j = \varepsilon_j$, so, under the differentiation assumptions, the reconstruction gradient flows back into the encoder ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), section 2.4). Expectations over $z$ become expectations over $\varepsilon$, which does not depend on $\varphi$:

$$
\nabla_\varphi\, \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z \mid x)}\big[f(z)\big] = \mathbb E_{\varepsilon}\big[\nabla_\varphi f(\mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon)\big] .
$$

<Reparameterization />

The KL term needs no sampling. Between two Gaussians it has a closed form, derived [below](#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior):

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}} = -\frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \big(1 + \log \sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2\big) .
$$

The encoder outputs $\log \sigma^2$ rather than $\sigma^2$. In exact arithmetic, exponentiating a finite $\log\sigma^2$ is positive; implementations still need to avoid floating-point underflow and overflow. A linear layer can output any real number, a tiny variance such as $10^{-4}$ becomes a moderate $-9.2$, and the KL is written directly in $\log \sigma^2$.

## What the organized latent space buys

The KL term encourages the encoder distributions toward the prior, but does not guarantee that prior samples land where the decoder is reliable. Generation follows the model: draw $z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, then $x\sim p_\theta(x\mid z)$. The encoder is not needed. Showing only a Gaussian decoder's mean $\hat x(z)$ omits observation noise; it is not a complete model sample. With $s^2=\tfrac12$, a sample adds independent noise of standard deviation $0.71$ per coordinate.

For a continuous decoder mean, $\hat x((1-\lambda)z_a+\lambda z_b)$ changes continuously, but continuity alone says nothing about semantic quality. Our three-pixel illustration below uses an explicitly defined sigmoid map; it shows the calculation, not learned image quality. In high dimension the midpoint of two independent $\mathcal N(0,I_d)$ codes follows $\mathcal N(0,\tfrac12I_d)$: its norm concentrates near $0.71\sqrt d$ rather than $\sqrt d$, inside the prior's typical [shell](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#high-dimensions-the-shell).

<LatentInterpolation />

An attribute direction can be a useful empirical heuristic. [Carter and Nielsen (2017)](#references) demonstrate a font-bolding direction, and explicitly show artifacts and limitations. Such a direction is not a consequence of the ELBO; no fonts or trained model from that example are used here.

Two separate mechanisms can hurt samples. First, the aggregate encoder distribution can differ from the prior, leaving prior regions poorly represented during reconstruction training. Second, with a fixed-variance Gaussian decoder and overlapping encoder distributions, one code can correspond to several observations; the optimal decoder mean is their conditional average. That average can blur distinct details. Diagonal Gaussian posteriors do have overlapping support; neither their separation nor a particular amount of blur is guaranteed.

A second view relates the average ELBO to a joint forward KL, under the density and finiteness assumptions stated below ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), §2.7). In a restricted model family this objective penalizes low model density where the data-encoder joint has mass; the resulting compromise depends on the family. It does not prove that every VAE overestimates variance or produces blurry images.

<details>
<summary>The average ELBO as a forward KL</summary>

Assume the population data distribution and the model have densities with respect to a common base measure, with finite terms in the identity below. Combine $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ and the encoder into $q_\varphi(x,z)=p_{\text{data}}(x)q_\varphi(z\mid x)$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(x, z) \,\|\, p_\theta(x, z)\big) \\
&\quad= \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(x, z)}\!\left[\log p_{\text{data}}(x) + \log \frac{q_\varphi(z \mid x)}{p_\theta(x, z)}\right] \\
&\quad= -H(p_{\text{data}}) - \mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}(x)}\big[\mathcal L(x)\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The data entropy is constant in the parameters, so maximizing the mean ELBO minimizes this KL. For an empirical distribution of point masses against a continuous observation model, the joint KL is instead infinite: that finite-KL interpretation must not be applied literally. The per-observation ELBO and its sample average remain well-defined whenever their own terms are finite.

</details>

Sample fidelity and coverage need separate measurement for both VAEs and adversarial models. Richer observation distributions and hierarchical latents can improve the basic Gaussian VAE. For example, [Vahdat and Kautz (2020)](#references) reported successful NVAE generation at $256\times256$ resolution; that published result is not reproduced here, and no images, weights or dataset from it are included.

## Where the ELBO comes from

Drop $\theta$ and $\varphi$ for the derivation. Multiply the integrand of the evidence by $q(z \mid x)/q(z \mid x)$, which needs $q > 0$ wherever $p(x, z) > 0$, true for a nondegenerate Gaussian $q$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(x) &= \log \int p(x, z)\, dz \\
&= \log \mathbb E_{q(z \mid x)}\!\left[\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&\ge \mathbb E_{q(z \mid x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] = \mathcal L(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

The inequality is Jensen's for the concave logarithm, $\log \mathbb E[Y] \ge \mathbb E[\log Y]$.

<JensenLog />

Writing $p(x, z) = p(x \mid z)\, p(z)$ splits the bound into the two terms used above:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x) &= \mathbb E_{q}\!\left[\log p(x \mid z) + \log \frac{p(z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p(x \mid z)\big] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Jensen shows that the bound holds; it does not say how loose it is. Writing $p(x, z) = p(z \mid x)\, p(x)$ instead gives the exact gap:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(x) - \mathcal L(x)
&= \mathbb E_{q}\!\left[\log p(x) - \log\frac{p(z\mid x)\,p(x)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb E_{q}\big[\log q(z \mid x) - \log p(z \mid x)\big] \\
&= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z \mid x)\big) \ge 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The bound is tight exactly when $q$ is the true posterior. The [variational inference note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference#the-evidence-lower-bound-and-the-reverse-kl) derives the same decomposition for any latent-variable model and explains why this objective targets the reverse KL.

The gap shows what each network does. Over the encoder, $\log p_\theta(x)$ is fixed, so raising $\mathcal L$ only brings $q_\varphi$ closer to the posterior in reverse KL. Over the decoder, $\mathcal L = \log p_\theta(x) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x)\big)$ can also rise by making the true posterior easier for $q$ to fit, which biases the model toward such posteriors. Maximizing the ELBO maximizes the likelihood only approximately ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), section 2.2.1).

## Which distribution is fixed? The prior \{#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior\}

The prior $p(z) = \mathcal N(0, I)$ is fixed. The posteriors are not: every image gets its own $\mu(x)$ and $\sigma^2(x)$, and the KL term only charges for how far they stray.

For one coordinate, with $q = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $p = \mathcal N(0, 1)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q(z) &= -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}, \\
\log p(z) &= -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi) - \frac{z^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Under $z \sim q$ we have $\mathbb E[(z-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb E[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p) &= \mathbb E_q[\log q - \log p] \\
&= -\tfrac12\log \sigma^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2) \\
&= \tfrac12\big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

For the diagonal encoder, both $q = \prod_j \mathcal N(\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)$ and $p = \prod_j \mathcal N(0, 1)$ factorize. The log ratio $\log q - \log p$ is then a sum over $j$ of terms that each depend on $z_j$ alone, and linearity of expectation adds the one-coordinate results ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), appendix B):

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, \mathcal N(0, I)\big) = \frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log\sigma_j^2\big) .
$$
 The value is zero only at $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$, because $\log t \le t - 1$ with equality only at $t = 1$. A full covariance $\Sigma$ gives $\tfrac12\big(\operatorname{tr}\Sigma + \mu^\top\mu - d - \log\det\Sigma\big)$ by the same steps with a trace in place of each square (folded below); correlation then has its own price, $-\tfrac12\log(1 - \rho^2)$ for two unit-variance coordinates with correlation $\rho$, which the diagonal encoder never pays because it cannot correlate at all.

<details>
<summary>The trace step for a full covariance</summary>

A scalar equals its own trace, and the trace is linear and cyclic, so for $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\big[(z - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (z - \mu)\big]
&= \operatorname{tr}\big(\Sigma^{-1}\, \mathbb E[(z - \mu)(z - \mu)^\top]\big) \\
&= \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma) = d, \\
\mathbb E[z^\top z] &= \operatorname{tr}\, \mathbb E[z z^\top] = \operatorname{tr}\Sigma + \mu^\top \mu .
\end{aligned}
$$

With $\log q(z) = -\tfrac12\log\det(2\pi\Sigma) - \tfrac12 (z - \mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(z - \mu)$ and $\log p(z) = -\tfrac d2 \log 2\pi - \tfrac12 z^\top z$, the $2\pi$ terms cancel and

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p) &= -\tfrac12\log\det\Sigma - \tfrac d2 + \tfrac12\big(\operatorname{tr}\Sigma + \mu^\top\mu\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $\mu = 0$ and $\Sigma$ with unit diagonal and off-diagonal $\rho$, $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma = 2 = d$ and $\det\Sigma = 1 - \rho^2$, which leaves $-\tfrac12\log(1 - \rho^2)$.

</details>

Read the formula as a fine:

| $\mu$ | $\sigma$ | $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ (nats) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 1 | 1 | 0.5 |
| 2 | 1 | 2 |
| 0 | 0.5 | 0.318 |
| 0 | 2 | 0.807 |
| 0 | 0.1 | 1.808 |
| 0 | 0.01 | 4.105 |

Moving the mean costs $\tfrac12\mu^2$. Shrinking the variance costs about $-\log\sigma - \tfrac12$, which grows only logarithmically, yet without bound: a point code, as in a plain autoencoder, would pay an infinite fine, since a point mass has [no finite KL](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#e-continuous-versus-discrete) against a density. Each image can therefore afford a fairly narrow, off-centre Gaussian when that helps reconstruction, but never a single point.

<KlFine />

What does the KL term ask of the codes as a whole? Take $N$ training images and their **aggregate posterior**, the mixture of all their posteriors:

$$
q(z) = \frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} q(z \mid x_n) .
$$

Treat the index $n$ as uniform on $\lbrace 1, \dots, N\rbrace$. The average KL term then splits in two ([Hoffman and Johnson, 2016](#references)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x_n) \,\|\, p(z)\big) \\
&\quad= I_q(n; z) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\|\, p(z)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the split</summary>

Write $q(z \mid x_n)/p(z)$ as $\big(q(z \mid x_n)/q(z)\big)\cdot\big(q(z)/p(z)\big)$ and average the logarithm over $n$ and $z \sim q(z \mid x_n)$. The first factor averages to the mutual information between an image's index and its code, by its definition. The second depends on $z$ alone, and averaging over $n$ then $z$ is averaging over the mixture $q(z)$, which gives the marginal KL.

</details>

The mutual information is bounded by $H(n)=\log N$. Reconstructing distinguishable observations accurately may require information about their index, but duplicates and a powerful conditional decoder can change how much is necessary. The marginal KL measures aggregate mismatch; it does not certify decoder quality. In particular, Gaussian mixtures have positive density everywhere, so “holes” here mean regions of very low encoder density relative to the prior, not literally absent support.

The prior appears only in the marginal KL, so, among unrestricted prior densities with the encoder fixed, the best prior is the aggregate posterior itself. That suggests learning the prior instead of fixing it, for instance with a [normalizing flow](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows) over $z$. The [VQ-VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae-variants#where-the-prior-went) of the next note learns its prior after the autoencoder is trained.

The DDPM construction can be read as a hierarchical latent-variable model with a fixed forward noising distribution and a learned reverse chain ([DDPM objective](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective)). [Latent diffusion](/notes/generative-models/latent-diffusion) applies diffusion in an autoencoder's lower-dimensional representation. Compression reduces the number of coordinates; a Gaussian aggregate code distribution does not follow from compression or from a finite KL penalty alone.

## Where it breaks: posterior collapse

A decoder strong enough to model the images on its own can ignore the code. The encoder then outputs the prior for every image, the KL term is zero, and the code carries nothing. This is **posterior collapse** ([Bowman et al., 2016](#references)).

The ELBO does not prevent it, and a one-dimensional Gaussian model shows why exactly. Let the data be $x \sim \mathcal N(0, 1.1)$, the prior $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, and the decoder $p(x \mid z) = \mathcal N(wz, s^2)$ with $w^2 + s^2 = 1.1$. Every such decoder gives the data its true distribution, so all of them have the same likelihood. The exact posterior is Gaussian, so the best Gaussian encoder equals it and the ELBO is tight. The rate, the average KL term, then equals the mutual information $\tfrac12\log(1 + w^2/s^2)$ (folded below):

| decoder | $w$ | $s^2$ | rate (nats) | $\log p(x = 1)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| uses the code | 1 | 0.1 | 1.199 | $-1.4211$ |
| partly | 0.5 | 0.85 | 0.129 | $-1.4211$ |
| collapsed | 0 | 1.1 | 0 | $-1.4211$ |

<details>
<summary>Where the rate comes from</summary>

With $q$ the exact posterior, the aggregate posterior is the prior: averaging $p(z \mid x)$ over the model's own $p(x)$, which here is the data distribution, returns $p(z)$. The marginal KL of the [ELBO surgery](#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior) vanishes, and the rate is the mutual information $I(x; z) = H(z) - H(z \mid x)$. By the [conditional formula](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals) with $\operatorname{Var} z = 1$, $\operatorname{Cov}(x, z) = w$ and $\operatorname{Var} x = w^2 + s^2$, the posterior variance is $1 - w^2/(w^2 + s^2) = s^2/(w^2 + s^2)$, the same for every $x$. A Gaussian of variance $v$ has entropy $\tfrac12\log(2\pi e v)$; the $2\pi e$ cancels in the difference, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(x; z) &= \tfrac12 \log 1 - \tfrac12 \log \frac{s^2}{w^2 + s^2} \\
&= \tfrac12 \log\Big(1 + \frac{w^2}{s^2}\Big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $w = 1$, $s^2 = 0.1$ this is $\tfrac12\ln 11 = 1.199$; the likelihood column is $\log \mathcal N(1;\, 0,\, 1.1)$ for all three.

</details>

In this constructed population example the bound is equally good at all three solutions, so it does not identify how much information the code should carry. A fixed-variance factorized Gaussian that ignores $z$ has only one unconditional mean; its squared-error optimum is the data mean. An expressive autoregressive decoder can model much richer data without $z$, making collapse a practical concern. **KL annealing** gradually raises the KL weight from zero to one ([Bowman et al., 2016](#references)). **Free bits** replace the KL cost for each latent group by $\max(\lambda,\overline{D}_{\mathrm{KL},j})$, with the bar an average over a minibatch in the formulation of [Kingma and Welling (2019)](#references), §2.8.1. Below the threshold, reducing KL no longer improves that term. Both modify the optimization incentives; neither guarantees useful codes.

## Conditioning on a label: the conditional VAE

The basic VAE cannot be told what to generate. The **conditional VAE** (CVAE) adds an observed condition $c$, such as a class label or a vector of binary attributes, to every part of the model ([Sohn, Yan and Lee, 2015](#references), section 4): a prior $p_\theta(z \mid c)$, often simply $\mathcal N(0, I)$ for every $c$; a decoder $p_\theta(x \mid z, c)$; and an encoder $q_\varphi(z \mid x, c)$. The [derivation of the ELBO](#where-the-elbo-comes-from) goes through with everything conditioned on $c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p_\theta(x \mid c) \ge{}& \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z \mid x, c)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z, c)\big] \\
&- D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x, c) \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid c)\big),
\end{aligned}
$$

with the gap $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x, c) \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x, c)\big)$. With the fixed prior, training uses the same reparameterization and the same closed-form KL; a learned Gaussian prior needs the [general KL between two Gaussians](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#what-the-gradients-are). In the networks, $c$ is usually concatenated to the encoder's input and to $z$ before the decoder.

Conditioning gives the decoder information it need not transmit through $z$, but does not ensure disentanglement. A transparent constructed example is $x\mid z,c\sim\mathcal N(1.4z+0.8c,0.36)$ with $c\in\{-1,1\}$. Holding $z=1$ fixed gives means $0.6$ and $2.2$, a shift of $1.6$. Here that behavior is guaranteed by the chosen formula. In a learned image model, keeping $z$ and changing $c$ need not preserve identity, pose or any other unconditioned attribute. The [conditional GAN](/notes/generative-models/gans#conditional-gan) uses an adversarial objective for a related conditioning task.

## What you should remember

- $\log p_\theta(x)=\mathcal L(x)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\varphi(z\mid x)\|p_\theta(z\mid x))$; the prior KL inside $\mathcal L$ is a different quantity from this posterior gap.
- Prior Monte Carlo can require exponentially many samples, as the independent-coordinate example demonstrates.
- Reparameterization $z=\mu+\sigma\odot\varepsilon$ provides a pathwise gradient under the differentiation conditions; diagonal-Gaussian KL is analytic.
- Fixed decoder variance $s^2=\tfrac12$ gives summed squared error plus KL; a shared learned variance depends on the overall expected MSE.
- Prior mismatch and conditional averaging are separate sample-quality problems; continuous interpolation does not guarantee meaningful intermediate objects.
- Collapse can coexist with an optimal ELBO. Annealing and free bits change the training objective, without guaranteeing informative codes.
