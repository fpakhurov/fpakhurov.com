A variational autoencoder turns an encoder–decoder into a probabilistic generative model. The decoder describes how a latent variable generates an observation; the encoder learns an approximate distribution of latent explanations for each input. Reconstruction, a prior and differentiable sampling connect these two directions.

## Why an ordinary autoencoder needs a sampling rule

The seminar starts with the familiar chain

$$
x\longrightarrow E(x)=z\longrightarrow D(z)=\hat{x}.
$$

An [autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders) learns to reconstruct training examples through a bottleneck. The scan gives a representation of roughly 10–100 numbers as an example. Reconstruction alone does not specify a convenient probability distribution from which to draw new codes.

Two generation ideas appear in the notes:

1. **Draw an arbitrary latent point and decode it.** A reconstruction-only objective need not train the decoder on that part of latent space, so the result can be poor.
2. **Start near an encoded reference.** Encode an image, perturb its code and decode nearby points. This explores a local region, but still requires a choice of perturbation distribution.

The handwritten digit sketch illustrates the second idea. A VAE instead introduces an explicit prior and trains the latent distributions in relation to it.

## Probability recap: joint, marginal and conditional

A random variable $X$ and one sampled value $x$ are different objects. For a continuous variable, $p(x)$ is a density; interval probabilities come from integrating it. Its expectation, when it exists, is

$$
\mathbb E[X]=\int x\,p(x)\,dx.
$$

For an observation $x$ and latent variable $z$, marginalization integrates out the variable we are not asking about:

$$
p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz,
\qquad
p(z)=\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dx.
$$

A conditional density fixes a value of the other variable:

$$
p_\theta(x\mid z)=\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{p(z)},
\qquad
p_\theta(z\mid x)=\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{p_\theta(x)},
$$

where the denominators are positive. Conditioning on $z=3$ means fixing a latent value; it does not mean setting the density $p(z)$ to 3.

## Prior, decoder and posterior

Choose a simple latent prior, commonly a standard Gaussian:

$$
p(z)=\mathcal N(z;0,I).
$$

The decoder defines a likelihood $p_\theta(x\mid z)$, and together they define the model:

$$
p_\theta(x,z)=p(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z).
$$

To generate, draw $z\sim p(z)$ and then $x\sim p_\theta(x\mid z)$. The prior is chosen here; decoder parameters $\theta$ are learned. The model's marginal $p_\theta(x)$ aims to explain the data distribution.

For an observed $x$, Bayes' rule gives the true model posterior:

$$
p_\theta(z\mid x)
=\frac{p(z)p_\theta(x\mid z)}
{\int p(z')p_\theta(x\mid z')\,dz'}.
$$

The denominator is the **evidence**, $p_\theta(x)$. With a neural decoder, this latent-space integral and the resulting posterior are generally intractable to compute exactly.

The encoder therefore learns $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ as an approximation to $p_\theta(z\mid x)$. The same encoder parameters $\phi$ are shared across inputs: evaluating the network produces a different approximate posterior for each $x$. This is amortized inference.

| Symbol | Role | How it is obtained |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $p(z)$ | Prior over latent variables | Chosen distribution, here $\mathcal N(0,I)$ |
| $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ | Decoder likelihood | Learned decoder |
| $p_\theta(z\mid x)$ | Exact posterior under the model | Bayes' rule; usually intractable |
| $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ | Approximate posterior | Learned encoder |

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial clarification.</strong> The scan calls the two directions “inverse.” Posterior inference and conditional generation are probability distributions, and the encoder approximates the former. They need not be inverse functions or recover one unique latent code.</aside>

## Deriving the evidence lower bound

We would like to maximize $\log p_\theta(x)$. Introduce a density $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ with suitable support and integrability. The diagonal Gaussian below has positive density throughout latent space. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p_\theta(x)
&=\log\int q_\phi(z\mid x)
\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\,dz\\
&=\log\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right]\\
&\ge\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
\left[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right]
=\mathcal L_{\theta,\phi}(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

The inequality uses the concavity of $\log$: $\log\mathbb E[R]\ge\mathbb E[\log R]$. The right-hand side is the **evidence lower bound**, or ELBO.

Factor the joint density into prior and likelihood:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\theta,\phi}(x)
&=\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)+\log p(z)-\log q_\phi(z\mid x)]\\
&=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}
[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]}_{\text{reconstruction log-likelihood}}
-\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))}_{\text{prior regularization}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The reconstruction term rewards latent explanations under which the input is likely. The KL term charges for departing from the prior. Training maximizes their difference, or minimizes its negative.

## The two KL divergences have different jobs

An exact rearrangement, for finite well-defined terms, gives

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
=\mathcal L_{\theta,\phi}(x)
+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p_\theta(z\mid x)\bigr).
$$

This second KL is the **gap to the evidence**. It compares the approximate posterior to the true model posterior, whereas the regularizer inside the ELBO compares the approximate posterior to the prior.

For a fixed decoder, improving $q_\phi$ increases the bound by reducing the posterior gap. The bound is tight when $q_\phi(z\mid x)=p_\theta(z\mid x)$ almost everywhere. During joint training, both the evidence and the gap can change; maximizing the bound is a tractable surrogate for maximizing the evidence, not a promise that every step increases the exact likelihood.

## When reconstruction becomes squared error

The scan connects reconstruction to MSE. That follows from a specific observation model. If

$$
p_\theta(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(x;f_\theta(z),\tau^2 I)
$$

with fixed variance $\tau^2>0$ and $d$ observed coordinates, then

$$
-\log p_\theta(x\mid z)
=\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\|x-f_\theta(z)\|_2^2
+\frac d2\log(2\pi\tau^2).
$$

The constant does not affect gradients when $\tau$ is fixed. The minimized negative ELBO becomes

$$
J(x)=\frac{1}{2\tau^2}
\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\|x-f_\theta(z)\|_2^2
+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))
+\text{constant}.
$$

Other likelihoods yield other reconstruction terms. For example, a Bernoulli likelihood on binary observations yields binary cross-entropy. The likelihood choice, pixel reduction and scale determine the reconstruction term's weight relative to KL.

## A Gaussian for each input

Instead of returning a single latent point, the encoder returns two vectors:

$$
\mu_\phi(x),\qquad
\ell_\phi(x)=\log\sigma_\phi^2(x).
$$

They parameterize a diagonal Gaussian:

$$
q_\phi(z\mid x)
=\mathcal N\!\left(z;\mu_\phi(x),
\operatorname{diag}(e^{\ell_\phi(x)})\right).
$$

Exponentiation is elementwise. Variance is $\sigma^2=e^\ell$; standard deviation is $\sigma=e^{\ell/2}$. This distinction resolves the scan's alternating $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma)$ and $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ notation.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Numerical qualification.</strong> Predicting log variance allows unconstrained network outputs and produces positive variance in exact arithmetic. It does not prevent overflow in $e^\ell$ or instability at extreme scales. Page 30's stability remark motivates the parameterization, rather than establishing a universal numerical guarantee.</aside>

## Reparameterization: separate noise from parameters

A naïve random draw does not provide the ordinary differentiable path through encoder parameters. For a Gaussian, move the randomness into independent standard-normal noise:

$$
\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\qquad
z=\mu_\phi(x)+e^{\ell_\phi(x)/2}\odot\epsilon.
$$

For a fixed noise draw, the expression is differentiable in $\mu$ and $\ell$. For each coordinate,

$$
\frac{\partial z_j}{\partial\mu_j}=1,
\qquad
\frac{\partial z_j}{\partial\ell_j}
=\frac12 e^{\ell_j/2}\epsilon_j.
$$

The reconstruction gradient passes through the decoder, $z$, and the encoder's output heads. We do not differentiate the act of drawing $\epsilon$. Under the usual regularity conditions, this gives a pathwise gradient estimator of the reconstruction expectation.

PyTorch's reparameterized Gaussian <code>rsample()</code> implements this kind of path. Ordinary <code>sample()</code> does not provide that path; see the [PyTorch distribution documentation](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/distributions.html#pathwise-derivative).

## Analytic Gaussian KL

For a diagonal Gaussian and the standard-normal prior, the KL has a closed form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|\mathcal N(0,I))
&=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{k}
\left(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\right)\\
&=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{k}
\left(\mu_j^2+e^{\ell_j}-1-\ell_j\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

All logarithms here are natural, so KL is in nats. It is also often coded as $-\tfrac12\sum_j(1+\ell_j-\mu_j^2-e^{\ell_j})$: the minus sign belongs to this rearrangement, and the resulting KL is nonnegative.

There is no sampling estimate needed for this term. It vanishes exactly when every $\mu_j=0$ and $\sigma_j^2=1$. Departures incur a soft cost. For finite means and positive finite variances it is finite, but it grows without bound as a variance approaches zero or becomes arbitrarily large.

## Do we really fit everything to N(0, I)?

Page 30 asks this explicitly. The prior stays fixed, while each input has its own $\mu_\phi(x)$ and $\ell_\phi(x)$. Reconstruction rewards retaining information about $x$; KL rewards staying close to the prior. The optimizer balances them.

If $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ were the same distribution for every input, the sampled code could not identify which input was supplied. If it equaled the prior for all inputs, the regularizer would be zero, but that alone would not establish useful reconstruction. A decoder may still model a marginal distribution without using input-specific latent information.

Thus “organize latent space” describes a training pressure. It does not guarantee that all encoded distributions become identical, that the aggregate encoded distribution exactly matches the prior, or that every sampled latent point gives a good observation. This clarification expands the source question.

## Generation, interpolation and conditioning

After training, generation uses the prior and decoder:

$$
z\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\qquad
x\sim p_\theta(x\mid z).
$$

Displaying $f_\theta(z)$ for the Gaussian observation model displays its conditional mean. It omits the final draw of observation noise. Reconstruction uses an input-dependent posterior code; new generation uses a prior code.

The interpolation remark on page 29 becomes

$$
z(t)=(1-t)z_a+t z_b,\qquad 0\le t\le1,
$$

followed by decoding. This traces a path between codes. It does not guarantee class separation, semantic control or that all intermediate codes are typical under a high-dimensional Gaussian.

The scan mentions blurry reconstructions and limited control over which class to generate. Squared-error prediction can favor conditional averages when several details are plausible; the model and reconstruction–KL balance also matter. Blur is a possible outcome, rather than a property of every VAE. The ordinary model above has no supplied class-conditioning input.

## Beta-VAE: notebook context

Cell 24 describes changing the weight of latent regularization. In a minimized objective, this is

$$
J_\beta(x)
=-\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]
+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z)).
$$

At $\beta=1$, this is the negative ELBO. Other weights change the objective. Increasing $\beta$ strengthens the cost of retaining input-specific latent information relative to reconstruction; it need not improve generation or yield disentangled factors. The [beta-VAE analysis by Burgess et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.03599) develops this tradeoff. The notebook's training call uses the default $\beta=1$, and contains no saved beta-sweep results.
