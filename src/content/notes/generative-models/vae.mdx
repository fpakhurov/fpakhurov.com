The [autoencoder note](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders) ended with a gap: an ordinary autoencoder compresses and reconstructs well, but it is not a generator. We cannot pick a point in its latent space and expect a sensible picture. The variational autoencoder (VAE) of [Kingma and Welling (2014)](#references), proposed independently by [Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra (2014)](#references), fixes this by **organizing the latent space**. Each input is encoded into a Gaussian rather than a single point, and these Gaussians are pulled toward one fixed prior. The VAE is also the first neural generative model in the course trained by (a bound on) maximum likelihood, the objective the [entropy note](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#why-cross-entropy-is-the-loss-we-minimize) identified as the natural one.

**Notation.** $x \in \mathbb R^D$ is a data point (an image with $D$ pixel values), $z \in \mathbb R^d$ its latent code and $\hat x$ the reconstruction. $p(z)$ is the prior over codes. $\theta$ are the decoder parameters and $\varphi$ the encoder parameters, as in the [autoencoder note](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders). $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ always means mean $\mu$ and **variance** $\sigma^2$. For vectors, $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is shorthand for $\mathcal N(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2))$, and $\mathcal N(0, I)$ is the standard normal in $d$ dimensions. $\odot$ is the element-wise product.

## From autoencoders to generation

Recall the encoder–decoder pipeline:

$$
\underset{\text{data}}{x} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{encoder}\ }\; z \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{decoder}\ }\; \underset{\text{reconstruction}}{\hat x} .
$$

The code $z$ is a **lower-dimensional representation** (512 numbers for the convolutional autoencoder of the [previous note](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders)), and the decoder's job is **reconstruction**. How could a trained autoencoder generate new images? There are two obvious options, and both fail.

1. **Sample a random $z$ and decode it.** Draw $z$ from a standard normal, say. Nothing ties the codes of a plain autoencoder to $\mathcal N(0, I)$, so such a $z$ can land far from every training code, where the decoder was never trained, and there is no reason for it to decode to a digit: the latent space is **disorganized**. Training only asks that the codes of training images decode back to those images. Nothing says what lies between the codes, or even where in $\mathbb R^d$ they are. Fitting a Gaussian per digit class to the codes, as in the [autoencoder note](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders#emergent-structure), does better, but it needs labels and per-class statistics, several of its samples are still badly distorted, and interpolated codes decode to overlays of two digits.
2. **Sample latent vectors near a reference.** Encode a real image to $z_{\text{ref}}$ and decode nearby points $z_1, z_2, z_3$. A nearby point is still not guaranteed to be a meaningful code: if it falls into a gap between the codes of training images, the decoder was never trained on it, and the decoded neighbour of a "7" need not look like a digit.

The fix is to make the distribution of codes part of the model. That requires the language of latent-variable models.

## Joint, marginal and conditional

Take two random variables, the data $x$ and a latent $z$. Their **joint** density $p(x, z)$ is a cloud in the $(x, z)$ plane. Projecting the cloud onto each axis gives the **marginal distributions** $p(x)$ and $p(z)$. **Marginalization** computes a marginal by integrating the other variable out:

$$
p(x) = \int p(x, z)\, dz .
$$

**Conditional probability** is the joint restricted to a fixed value of the condition and renormalized:

$$
p(x \mid z) = \frac{p(x, z)}{p(z)} .
$$

For example, $p(x \mid z = 3)$ is the slice of $p(x,z)$ along the line $z = 3$, divided by $p(z = 3)$ so that it integrates to one over $x$.

## The latent-variable model and its posterior

A latent-variable model *generates* data in two steps: draw a code from a **prior** $p(z)$, then draw an image from the **decoder** (likelihood) $p_\theta(x \mid z)$. The prior is chosen, not learned; the standard choice is $p(z) = \mathcal N(0, I)$. The model's distribution over images is the marginal

$$
p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz ,
$$

called the **evidence**. Training by maximum likelihood means maximizing $\log p_\theta(x)$ over the data.

The reverse direction, "which codes explain this image", is the **posterior**. By Bayes' theorem

$$
p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)}{p_\theta(x)} ,
$$

and the evidence in the denominator is an integral over the whole latent space. With a neural-network decoder there is no closed form. In high dimension quadrature is hopeless, and a naive Monte Carlo average of $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ over $z \sim p(z)$ has far too much variance to use inside every training step: almost all prior samples explain a given $x$ badly. Both the likelihood we want to maximize and the posterior we would need for encoding are therefore intractable.

## Variational approximation and the ELBO

The way out is to approximate the posterior by a distribution from a simple family. The VAE uses a diagonal Gaussian whose mean and variances are produced by an **encoder** network with parameters $\varphi$:

$$
q_\varphi(z \mid x) = \mathcal N\big(\mu_\varphi(x), \sigma_\varphi^2(x)\big) \approx p_\theta(z \mid x).
$$

Replacing an intractable posterior by the member of a simple family closest to it in $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x)\big)$ is **variational inference** ([Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe, 2017](#references)). Because one network produces the approximation for every $x$, instead of a separate optimization per data point, it is called *amortized* ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), §2.1). The quantity maximized is the **evidence lower bound (ELBO)**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x) ={}&
\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \\
&-\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regularization}} \\
\le{}& \log p_\theta(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

The inequality is derived [below](#where-the-elbo-comes-from). Its two terms pull in opposite directions.

**Reconstruction** measures how well an image survives the trip image → code → image. With a Gaussian decoder it **reduces to the $L_2$ reconstruction loss**. If $p_\theta(x \mid z) = \mathcal N\big(x;\, \hat x(z),\, s^2 I\big)$, where the decoder network outputs the mean $\hat x(z)$ and $s^2$ is a fixed variance, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\log p_\theta(x \mid z) ={}& \frac{1}{2s^2}\,\big\|x - \hat x(z)\big\|_2^2 \\
&+ \frac D2 \log(2\pi s^2),
\end{aligned}
$$

and the last term does not depend on the network. With $s^2 = \tfrac12$ the weight of the squared error is exactly 1, so a squared error **summed** over the $D$ pixels plus the KL term is the negative ELBO up to a constant. (A mean over pixels would correspond to $s^2 = D/2$.) A different fixed $s^2$ changes the balance between the two terms: multiplying the loss by $2s^2$ gives $\|x - \hat x(z)\|_2^2 + 2s^2\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}$, a KL weight $\beta = 2s^2$ (see β-VAE below).

**Regularization** measures how far each posterior $q_\varphi(z \mid x)$ is from the prior. It pulls every code distribution toward $\mathcal N(0, I)$, so all codes live in one well-known region of the latent space.

<ElboTerms />

So instead of mapping the input to a single point, the VAE maps it to a **Gaussian**. The encoder outputs $\mu$ and $\log\sigma^2$, we sample $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and the decoder reconstructs from $z$. The training loss is the negative ELBO with $s^2 = \tfrac12$, estimated with one sampled $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ per input:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\text{VAE}}(x) ={}& \underbrace{\big\|x - \hat x(z)\big\|_2^2}_{\text{reconstruction}} \\
&+ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0, I)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

The KL term now has a plus sign because we *minimize*.

## The reparameterization trick

The loss contains a sample $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and we **cannot backpropagate through sampling**: a random draw is not a differentiable function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$. The fix is to move the randomness into an input, exactly as in the [Gaussian note](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions#sampling-from-standard-noise-to-any-gaussian):

$$
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon,
\qquad
\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I).
$$

$\varepsilon$ is sampled independently of the network, then **scaled** by $\sigma$ and **shifted** by $\mu$. This gives the same distribution, $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, but now $z$ is a **differentiable function** of $\mu$ and $\sigma$: $\partial z_j / \partial \mu_j = 1$ and $\partial z_j / \partial \sigma_j = \varepsilon_j$. Gradients of the reconstruction term flow back into the encoder ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), §2.4). The resulting gradient estimate typically has much lower variance than the generic score-function (REINFORCE) estimator, whose variance Kingma and Welling (§2.2) call very high.

<Reparameterization />

The KL part of the loss needs no sampling at all: between two Gaussians it has a closed form, derived [below](#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior). Summed over the $d$ latent dimensions,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{KL}} = -\frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \big(1 + \log \sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2\big) .
$$

## What the organized latent space buys

- We can **sample** $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and decode it: the KL term pulls the codes toward the prior, so prior samples mostly land where the decoder was trained (how well this works is discussed [below](#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior)). We can also sample near the code of a reference image and get sensible variations of it.
- We can **interpolate**: decode $z_\lambda = (1-\lambda)\, z_a + \lambda\, z_b$ for $\lambda$ from 0 to 1 and move smoothly between two images or classes.

<LatentInterpolation />

**Drawbacks.**

- **Blurry images.** With a factorized Gaussian decoder we display its mean $\hat x(z)$, not a sample from $p_\theta(x \mid z)$. Where the posteriors of different images overlap, one code can be explained by several images, and the squared-error optimum $\hat x(z)$ is an average over them. One explanation offered for the blur at the level of the whole model is the mode-covering behaviour of maximum likelihood (forward KL) from [Fitting distributions with KL](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#forward-kl-covers-both-modes-reverse-kl-picks-one): a model that cannot represent the data spreads probability over everything plausible ([Theis, van den Oord and Bethge, 2016](#references)).
- **Weaker results than other likelihood-based models** for several years: [Vahdat and Kautz (2020)](#references) note that VAEs were outperformed by normalizing flows and autoregressive models. Their hierarchical NVAE reached state-of-the-art results among non-autoregressive likelihood-based models on CelebA 64 and CelebA HQ and was the first VAE applied to natural images of $256 \times 256$ pixels.
- **No conditioning** in the basic model: it cannot be told what to generate.

Extensions address these: the conditional VAE ([Sohn, Lee and Yan, 2015](#references)), β-VAE ([Higgins et al., 2017](#references)) and VQ-VAE with discrete codes ([van den Oord, Vinyals and Kavukcuoglu, 2017](#references)). β-VAE simply multiplies the KL term by a weight $\beta$. With $\beta > 1$ the codes are pulled harder toward the prior at the cost of reconstruction, and [Higgins et al. (2017)](#references) report that this encourages disentangled, more independent latent coordinates; without supervision or other inductive biases such disentanglement is not guaranteed ([Locatello et al., 2019](#references)). $\beta < 1$ favours reconstruction.

## Where the ELBO comes from

We drop $\theta$ and $\varphi$ in this section. Start from the evidence and multiply by $1 = q(z\mid x)/q(z\mid x)$ inside the integral. The rewriting needs $q(z \mid x) > 0$ wherever $p(x, z) > 0$, which holds for a Gaussian $q$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(x) &= \log \int p(x, z)\, dz \\
&= \log \int \frac{q(z \mid x)}{q(z \mid x)}\, p(x, z)\, dz \\
&= \log \mathbb E_{q(z \mid x)}\!\left[\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&\ge \mathbb E_{q(z \mid x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] = \mathcal L(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

The inequality is **Jensen's inequality**. For a convex $f$ it reads $\mathbb E[f(Y)] \ge f(\mathbb E[Y])$. The logarithm is **concave**, so the direction flips:

$$
\log \mathbb E[Y] \;\ge\; \mathbb E[\log Y].
$$

<JensenLog />

Writing $p(x, z) = p(x \mid z)\, p(z)$ splits the bound into the two familiar terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x) &= \mathbb E_{q(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log p(x \mid z) + \log \frac{p(z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb E_{q(z\mid x)}\big[\log p(x \mid z)\big] \\
&\quad - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

The evidence is never less than the ELBO, so maximizing the ELBO maximizes a guaranteed floor under $\log p(x)$.

**How loose is the bound?** Exactly by a KL divergence. Since $p(x, z) = p(z \mid x)\, p(x)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\log p(x) - \mathcal L(x) \\
&\quad= \mathbb E_{q(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log p(x) - \log\frac{p(z\mid x)\,p(x)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&\quad= \mathbb E_{q(z\mid x)}\big[\log q(z \mid x) - \log p(z \mid x)\big] \\
&\quad= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z \mid x)\big) \;\ge\; 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

In the second line $\log p(x)$ cancels, and the last step uses that KL is non-negative (see the [entropy note](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#kullbackleibler-divergence)). Written with the parameters, $\mathcal L = \log p_\theta(x) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z \mid x)\big)$. A step in $\theta$ raises this sum: usually by raising $\log p_\theta(x)$, but possibly also by making the true posterior easier for $q$ to fit, which biases the model toward such posteriors. Maximizing the ELBO therefore only approximately maximizes the likelihood ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), §2.2.1; [Hoffman and Johnson, 2016](#references), eq. 5). Over the encoder, $\log p_\theta(x)$ does not change, so the encoder can only shrink the gap: it makes $q(z \mid x)$ a better approximation of the true posterior in **reverse** KL. The bound is tight exactly when $q(z\mid x) = p(z\mid x)$.

## What is fixed: the prior, not the posterior

Is the posterior forced to be $\mathcal N(0, I)$? No. What is fixed is the **prior** $p(z) = \mathcal N(0, I)$. The posterior is not fixed: for each input the encoder outputs two vectors, $\mu(x)$ and $\log \sigma^2(x)$.

Why $\log \sigma^2$ and not $\sigma^2$?

- $\sigma^2$ is a variance and must be positive. A linear layer can output any real number, and $\sigma^2 = e^{\log \sigma^2} > 0$ always.
- It is numerically well scaled: a tiny variance such as $10^{-4}$ becomes a moderate number, $\log 10^{-4} \approx -9.2$.
- The closed-form KL between Gaussians is written directly in terms of $\log\sigma^2$.

**A constant posterior is useless.** Suppose the encoder ignored its input and output $q(z \mid x) = \mathcal N(0, I)$ for every $x$. The KL term would be zero, but $z$ would carry no information about $x$: the decoder would receive pure noise and could at best output the average image. This failure is known as **posterior collapse**, and it happens in practice when the decoder is powerful enough to model the data without the code ([Bowman et al., 2016](#references)). The input must stay recoverable from its code.

**The closed form.** For one latent dimension,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0, 1)\big) \\
&\quad= \tfrac12\big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log \sigma^2\big),
\end{aligned}
$$

and for a diagonal Gaussian the dimensions add up ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), Appendix B). With $q = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $p = \mathcal N(0,1)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q(z) &= -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}, \\
\log p(z) &= -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi) - \frac{z^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Under $z \sim q$ we have $\mathbb E[(z-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb E[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p) &= \mathbb E_q[\log q - \log p] \\
&= -\tfrac12\log \sigma^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2) \\
&= \tfrac12\big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Pulling the minus sign inside the bracket gives the $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{KL}}$ formula above. The value is zero only at $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$, because $\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2 \ge 0$ with equality only at $\sigma^2 = 1$ (from $\log t \le t - 1$ for $t > 0$, with equality only at $t = 1$).

How to read it: the model **pays a fine** if $\mu \ne 0$ or $\sigma \ne 1$, and the fine has a specific shape.

- **Moving the mean** costs $\tfrac12\mu^2$: 0.5 at $\mu = 1$ and 2 at $\mu = 2$.
- **Shrinking the variance** costs $\tfrac12\sigma^2 - \log\sigma - \tfrac12 \approx -\log\sigma - \tfrac12$ for small $\sigma$, so it grows only logarithmically: exactly 1.81 at $\sigma = 0.1$ and 4.11 at $\sigma = 0.01$. The fine grows without bound as $\sigma \to 0$, so a deterministic point code, as in a plain autoencoder, would pay an infinite fine. A point mass is not absolutely continuous with respect to a Gaussian, so its KL is infinite; see the atom-versus-density case in [Fitting distributions with KL](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#e-continuous-versus-discrete).
- **Widening** costs $\tfrac12\sigma^2 - \log\sigma - \tfrac12$, which grows like $\tfrac12\sigma^2$.

So each input can afford its own fairly narrow, off-centre Gaussian when that helps reconstruction, but never a single point.

**Which distribution should match the prior?** The KL term pulls each $q(z \mid x)$ toward the prior only as far as reconstruction allows. To see what it asks of the codes as a whole, take $N$ training images $x_1, \dots, x_N$ and define the **aggregate posterior**, the mixture of all their posteriors:

$$
q(z) = \frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} q(z \mid x_n) .
$$

Treat the index $n$ as a random variable, uniform on $\lbrace 1, \dots, N\rbrace$, so that $q(n, z) = \tfrac1N\, q(z \mid x_n)$. The average KL term of the ELBO then splits into two parts ([Hoffman and Johnson, 2016](#references), eqs. 11–12):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x_n)\,\|\,p(z)\big) \\
&\quad= I_q(n; z) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $I_q(n; z) = \mathbb E_{q(n, z)}\big[\log \frac{q(n \mid z)}{q(n)}\big]$ is the mutual information between the index of an image and its code; it lies between 0 and $\log N$. The second term is the **marginal KL** from the aggregate posterior to the prior. To check the identity, use Bayes' theorem for $q(n, z)$ to write $q(z \mid x_n)/p(z) = \big(q(z)/p(z)\big)\cdot\big(q(n \mid z)/q(n)\big)$ and take the expectation of the logarithm under $q(n, z)$: the first factor gives the marginal KL and the second the mutual information. Good reconstruction needs the code to identify the image, so this term must stay large, and that is why each individual posterior is narrow. Only the second term asks something of the prior: the mixture $q(z)$ should fill $\mathcal N(0, I)$ without leaving holes. Training pushes the mixture toward the prior, but the match is only approximate. For a VAE with 10 latent dimensions on binarized MNIST, Hoffman and Johnson measure a mutual information close to its maximum $\log N$ and a marginal KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z)\,\|\,p(z))$ of 8.35 nats (§4, Table 1). Where $q(z)$ leaves holes, prior samples land in regions the decoder rarely saw and decode poorly.

## Summary

- The likelihood $p_\theta(x)$ and the posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ of a latent-variable model are intractable, because both require integrating over the latent space.
- An encoder produces an approximate posterior $q_\varphi(z \mid x)$, a diagonal Gaussian.
- Training maximizes the ELBO, reconstruction minus the KL to the prior, which is a lower bound on $\log p_\theta(x)$ with gap $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x))$.
- The reparameterization trick makes the sampled reconstruction term differentiable; the KL term is available in closed form.
- The fixed prior organizes the latent space, so decoding $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ generates new images. The match between the aggregate posterior and the prior is only approximate, and holes in it produce poor samples.

## Notebook

The implementation follows the [VAE notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/42dd64b535f5bedf1d2b3db28f2f21ce96c9f09f/seminars/06_Variational-Autoencoders/VAE.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* repository (pinned commit; cell numbers count every cell from 1). The notebook has no saved outputs, so it documents the procedure rather than results.

- **Cells 4–5**: CelebA faces resized to $64 \times 64$ RGB and normalized to $[-1, 1]$, batch size 128, `drop_last=True`.
- **Cells 6–7**: why plain autoencoders fail to generate, and the Bayes-theorem argument for an approximate posterior.
- **Cells 10 and 12**: the encoder has four $4 \times 4$ stride-2 convolutions, each followed by LeakyReLU and then BatchNorm (none after the last), and two linear heads, `mean_layer` and `logvar_layer`. The decoder is not a literal mirror: a linear layer with ReLU, then four blocks of $\times 2$ upsampling and a $3 \times 3$ convolution, with a Tanh output in $[-1, 1]$.
- **Cell 15**: the `VAE` class with `latent_dim = 100`. `reparametrize` computes `std_dev = exp(0.5 * logvar)`, draws `epsilon = randn_like(std_dev)` and returns `mean + epsilon * std_dev`; `sample(n)` decodes $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$.
- **Cells 16–17**: `loss_function`: squared error summed over pixels and averaged over the batch, plus `kld_loss = -0.5 * sum(1 + logvar - mean² - exp(logvar))` averaged over the batch and multiplied by `beta` (default 1). This is the one-sample estimate of the negative ELBO with $s^2 = \tfrac12$ from the [reconstruction paragraph](#variational-approximation-and-the-elbo), up to a constant. The `exp(logvar)` inside the KL is clamped from below at $10^{-8}$. The clamp has no stabilizing effect: it changes the loss only when $\sigma^2 < 10^{-8}$, and the $\log\sigma^2$ term itself is not clamped. The markdown of cell 16 rewrites $\sum_i \log\sigma_i^2$ as $\lVert\log\boldsymbol\sigma\rVert_1$, which is not correct: $\sum_i \log\sigma_i^2 = 2\sum_i \log\sigma_i$, and the logs can be negative, so neither the factor 2 nor the sign survives in a norm. The code in cell 17 uses the correct sum.
- **Cells 18–21**: training with Adam at learning rate $10^{-3}$ for 10 epochs with a cosine learning-rate schedule (`CosineAnnealingLR`, `T_max = 10`), logging the two loss terms separately and decoding 64 prior samples after every epoch.
- **Cell 23**: generation from the prior. **Cell 24** (markdown only) defines the β-VAE loss and disentanglement; no β-VAE is trained, because `train_vae` calls `loss_function` with the default $\beta = 1$.
- **Implementation notes.** The logged and printed losses are divided by the batch size a second time (`mse_loss.item() / len(data)`, and epoch sums of per-image losses divided by `len(dataset)`), so they are about 128 times smaller than the per-image loss. The model is never switched to `eval()`: sampling in cells 18 and 23 runs the decoder's BatchNorm on the statistics of the generated batch and updates its running averages.
