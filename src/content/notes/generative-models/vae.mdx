Pick a random point in the latent space of the [autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders) and decode it. You get a smudge. The codes of training images decode well, but nothing told the network where those codes lie or what fills the space between them. To generate, we need codes that follow a distribution we can sample from, and a decoder trained on everything that distribution produces.

The **variational autoencoder** (VAE) of [Kingma and Welling (2014)](#references), proposed at the same time by [Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra (2014)](#references), builds that in. Each image is encoded to a Gaussian instead of a point, and all these Gaussians are pulled toward one fixed distribution. It is also the first neural model of the course trained by a bound on maximum likelihood, the objective the [entropy note](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#why-cross-entropy-is-the-loss-we-minimize) singled out.

## From autoencoders to generation

The autoencoder maps an image $x$ to a code $z$ and back to a reconstruction $\hat x$. Two ways of generating with it suggest themselves, and both fail.

Drawing $z$ from a standard normal and decoding it fails because nothing ties the codes to $\mathcal N(0, I)$. A random $z$ can land far from every training code, where the decoder was never trained. Fitting a Gaussian per class to the codes, as the [autoencoder note](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders#emergent-structure) did, needs labels and still gives distorted digits.

Decoding points near the code of a real image fails for the same reason on a smaller scale. A nearby point can fall into a gap between training codes, and the decoded neighbour of a 7 need not look like a digit.

<LatentGarbage />

The fix is to make the distribution of the codes part of the model.

## The latent-variable model and its posterior

Start from the simplest model of this kind. **Probabilistic PCA** draws a code $z \in \mathbb R^d$ from a **prior** and places the image $x \in \mathbb R^D$, with $d < D$, near a linear function of it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &\sim \mathcal N(0, I), \\
x \mid z &\sim \mathcal N(Wz + \mu, \sigma^2 I) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Everything is Gaussian, so both the evidence and the posterior have closed forms. The image $x = Wz + \mu + \varepsilon$, with noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 I)$, is an affine map of the Gaussian pair $(z, \varepsilon)$, so $x \sim \mathcal N(\mu, WW^\top + \sigma^2 I)$ by the [affine-map rule](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps). Completing the square in $z$ (folded below) gives the posterior

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(z \mid x) &= \mathcal N\big(M^{-1} W^\top (x - \mu),\ \sigma^2 M^{-1}\big), \\
M &= W^\top W + \sigma^2 I .
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the posterior</summary>

Bayes' rule keeps only the factors that depend on $z$; the rest is a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(z \mid x)
&= \log p(x \mid z) + \log p(z) + \text{const} \\
&= -\frac{\lVert x - \mu - Wz\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{z^\top z}{2} + \text{const} \\
&= -\tfrac12\, z^\top \frac{M}{\sigma^2}\, z + z^\top \frac{W^\top (x - \mu)}{\sigma^2} + \text{const} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The third line expands the square and collects $z^\top z + z^\top W^\top W z/\sigma^2 = z^\top (M/\sigma^2)\, z$. A Gaussian $\mathcal N(m, P^{-1})$ has the log density $-\tfrac12 z^\top P z + z^\top P m + \text{const}$. Matching the quadratic term gives $P = M/\sigma^2$, and matching the linear term, $Pm = W^\top (x - \mu)/\sigma^2$, gives $m = M^{-1} W^\top (x - \mu)$.

</details>

Maximum likelihood sets $\mu$ to the data mean, where the gradient $\sum_n C^{-1}(x_n - \mu)$ of a Gaussian log-likelihood vanishes for any covariance $C$. EM fits $W$ and $\sigma^2$ by alternating two exact steps: set $q(z)$ to this posterior, then maximize the expected log joint ([EM as coordinate ascent](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca#em-as-coordinate-ascent-on-the-evidence-lower-bound)). Probabilistic PCA is thus a VAE with a linear decoder and an exact Gaussian encoder ([probabilistic PCA](/notes/bayesian-methods/em-and-bayesian-pca)), diagonal when the columns of $W$ are orthogonal and $M$ is diagonal.

Images do not lie near a linear subspace. A random point in the space of $100 \times 100$ images is noise, so real images fill a small, curved part of it. The VAE keeps the two-step model and replaces $Wz + \mu$ by a neural network: the **decoder** $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ is a Gaussian whose mean, and possibly variance, are outputs of a network with parameters $\theta$ ([«Байесовские методы в анализе данных», 2026](#references), lecture 6). The model's distribution of images is the marginal of the joint $p_\theta(x, z) = p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)$ ([joint and marginal densities](/notes/probability/joint-distributions)). The marginal is called the **evidence**:

$$
p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz .
$$

Maximum likelihood maximizes $\log p_\theta(x)$ over the training images. The reverse question, which codes explain a given image, is answered by the **posterior**:

$$
p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)}{p_\theta(x)} .
$$

Both need the integral over the whole latent space, and a neural decoder gives it no closed form. EM breaks at its first step, which needs the exact posterior.

<DataLatentMaps />

Why not estimate the integral by sampling? Draw $z_1, \dots, z_n$ from the prior and average $p_\theta(x \mid z_k)$. The $n$ terms are independent with mean $p(x)$, so the average is unbiased, with variance $\big(\mathbb E_{p(z)}[p(x \mid z)^2] - p(x)^2\big)/n$. Relative to $p(x)^2$ it is

$$
\frac{\operatorname{Var}\big[\hat p(x)\big]}{p(x)^2} = \frac1n \left(\frac{\mathbb E_{p(z)}\big[p(x \mid z)^2\big]}{p(x)^2} - 1\right) .
$$

Almost every prior sample explains a given $x$ badly, and the rare good ones dominate the average. A toy makes this concrete: let each of $d$ coordinates be independent, with $z_j \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $x_j \mid z_j \sim \mathcal N(z_j, 0.1)$. At $x = 0$ every coordinate multiplies the ratio inside the brackets by $11/\sqrt{21} = 2.40$, and $n = 100\,(\text{ratio} - 1)$ samples bring the relative error, the square root of the relative variance, to 10%:

| $d$ | ratio $2.40^d$ | samples for a 10% relative error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2.4 | 140 |
| 10 | 6350 | 635,000 |
| 100 | $1.1 \cdot 10^{38}$ | $1.1 \cdot 10^{40}$ |

<details>
<summary>Where 2.40 comes from</summary>

For one coordinate with noise variance $v = 0.1$, $p(x = 0 \mid z) = (2\pi v)^{-1/2} e^{-z^2/(2v)}$ and, by the affine-map rule, $p(x = 0) = \big(2\pi(1 + v)\big)^{-1/2}$. The square of the likelihood is again a Gaussian shape in $z$, and the Gaussian integral $\int e^{-a z^2/2} dz = \sqrt{2\pi/a}$ with $a = 2/v + 1$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{p(z)}\big[p(0 \mid z)^2\big]
&= \frac{1}{2\pi v} \int \frac{e^{-z^2/v - z^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, dz \\
&= \frac{1}{2\pi v}\, \Big(1 + \frac 2v\Big)^{-1/2}, \\
\frac{\mathbb E_{p(z)}\big[p(0 \mid z)^2\big]}{p(0)^2}
&= \frac{1 + v}{\sqrt{v^2 + 2v}} = \frac{1.1}{\sqrt{0.21}} = 2.40 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The coordinates are independent, so with $d$ of them both numerator and denominator are products over coordinates, and the ratio is $2.40^d$.

</details>

The cost grows exponentially with the latent dimension ([Isachenko, 2025](#references), lecture 3). Samples drawn from the posterior instead would all land where $x$ is explained; we cannot draw them, but we can learn something close.

## Variational approximation and the ELBO

The way out keeps EM's lower bound and gives up the exact E-step: $q$ is restricted to a simple family and optimized together with $\theta$. Replacing an intractable posterior by the closest member of a simple family is **variational inference**, the subject of the [variational inference note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference) of the Bayesian Methods course.

The plainest choice gives each training image $x_i$ its own diagonal Gaussian $q(z_i) = \mathcal N(m_i, S_i)$. That works, but the variational parameters grow with the data, and every new image needs its own optimization. The VAE replaces the table of $(m_i, S_i)$ by an **encoder** network with parameters $\varphi$:

$$
q_\varphi(z \mid x) = \mathcal N\big(\mu_\varphi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma_\varphi^2(x)\big) .
$$

One network that outputs $q$ for any $x$ is **amortized** inference: the cost of inference is paid once, in training the encoder ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), section 2.1; [amortization in numbers](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference#amortized-inference-and-the-vae)).

Encoder and decoder are trained together by maximizing the **evidence lower bound** (ELBO), derived [below](#where-the-elbo-comes-from):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x) ={}& \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z \mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big] \\
&- D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)\big) \\
\le{}& \log p_\theta(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term is the **reconstruction**: how well an image survives the trip image → code → image. It averages $\log p_\theta(x \mid z)$ over $q$, so it would be largest if $q$ put all its mass on the single best code, since an average never exceeds its maximum; on its own it would shrink every $q_\varphi(z \mid x)$ to a point and give back the plain autoencoder. The second term, the **regularization**, charges for that collapse to a point and for every departure from the prior ([«Байесовские методы в анализе данных», 2026](#references), lecture 6).

<ElboTerms />

With a Gaussian decoder of fixed variance $s^2$, whose network outputs the mean $\hat x(z)$, the reconstruction term is a squared error:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x \mid z) = \frac{\lVert x - \hat x(z)\rVert^2}{2s^2} + \frac D2 \log(2\pi s^2) .
$$

The last term does not depend on the networks. With $s^2 = \tfrac12$ the squared error, **summed** over the $D$ pixels, plus the KL term is the negative ELBO up to a constant; a mean over pixels corresponds to $s^2 = D/2$. Any other $s^2$ reweights the two terms: multiplying by $2s^2$ gives the squared error plus $2s^2$ times the KL, the weight $\beta$ of the [β-VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae-variants#β-vae-a-weight-on-the-kl-term).

A decoder that also learns its variance keeps the last term, because it now depends on the network. For one shared $s^2$ the loss becomes the log of the MSE (folded below), so MSE alone is not the loss; a per-pixel variance can shrink to 0 on a pixel reconstructed exactly, where $\tfrac12\log s^2 \to -\infty$, so it needs a floor. Binary pixels call for a Bernoulli decoder and binary cross-entropy ([the two readings](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders#reconstruction-loss)).

<details>
<summary>The loss with a learned shared variance</summary>

The derivative of $\lVert x - \hat x\rVert^2/(2s^2) + \tfrac D2 \log s^2$ in $s^2$ is $-\lVert x - \hat x\rVert^2/(2s^4) + D/(2s^2)$, which vanishes at $s^2 = \lVert x - \hat x\rVert^2/D$. Substituting back, the first term becomes $D/2$ and the loss is $\tfrac D2 \log\big(\lVert x - \hat x\rVert^2/D\big)$ plus a constant.

</details>

The training loss is the negative ELBO with one sampled code per image:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\text{VAE}}(x) ={}& \big\lVert x - \hat x(z)\big\rVert^2 \\
&+ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2) \,\|\, \mathcal N(0, I)\big),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, where $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ are the encoder's outputs for $x$ and the variance is applied coordinate-wise. Neither term contains the posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ or the evidence: the loss needs only the encoder, the prior and the decoder, which we can all evaluate.

The weights $\theta$ and $\varphi$ are point estimates, fitted by stochastic gradient ascent on the ELBO like the parameters of an M-step ([the VAE as variational EM](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference#amortized-inference-and-the-vae)). Only the code is integrated over and sampled, as are the noisy images of the later diffusion models. A distribution over the weights too would make a [Bayesian neural network](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-deep-learning#bayes-by-backprop).

## The reparameterization trick

The ELBO of a training set is a sum over its $n$ images, so a stochastic gradient takes one random image, or a minibatch, and scales its term by $n$. For the decoder the rest is easy: $q_\varphi$ does not depend on $\theta$, the gradient moves inside the expectation, and one sampled code estimates it. The encoder's parameters shape the distribution the expectation is taken over. Differentiating that density and multiplying by $q_\varphi / q_\varphi$ turns the gradient back into an expectation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\varphi\, \mathbb E_{q_\varphi}\big[f(z)\big]
&= \int f(z)\, \nabla_\varphi q_\varphi(z \mid x)\, dz \\
&= \mathbb E_{q_\varphi}\big[f(z)\, \nabla_\varphi \log q_\varphi(z \mid x)\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

This **score-function** or REINFORCE estimator works for any $q$, but its variance is high ([«Байесовские методы в анализе данных», 2026](#references), lecture 6): it multiplies $f(z)$ by a score whose mean is zero and never uses how $f$ changes with $z$. For the gradient in $\mu$ of $\mathbb E_q[z^2]$ at $\mu = \sigma = 1$, one score-function sample has variance 30 and one reparameterized sample, $2(1 + \varepsilon)$, variance 4 ([worked example](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference#the-score-function-gradient)); for a logistic regression with 50 weights the factor reaches thousands ([in numbers](/notes/bayesian-methods/black-box-variational-inference#the-variance-in-numbers)).

A Gaussian $q$ allows better. The fix moves the randomness into an input, as in [sampling any Gaussian from standard noise](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions#sampling-from-standard-noise-to-any-gaussian):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon &\sim \mathcal N(0, I), \\
z &= \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\odot$ is the element-wise product. The distribution of $z$ is unchanged, but now $\partial z_j / \partial \mu_j = 1$ and $\partial z_j / \partial \sigma_j = \varepsilon_j$, so the reconstruction gradient flows back into the encoder ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), section 2.4). Expectations over $z$ become expectations over $\varepsilon$, which does not depend on $\varphi$:

$$
\nabla_\varphi\, \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z \mid x)}\big[f(z)\big] = \mathbb E_{\varepsilon}\big[\nabla_\varphi f(\mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon)\big] .
$$

<Reparameterization />

The KL term needs no sampling. Between two Gaussians it has a closed form, derived [below](#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior):

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}} = -\frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \big(1 + \log \sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2\big) .
$$

The encoder outputs $\log \sigma^2$ rather than $\sigma^2$. A linear layer can output any real number and $e^{\log\sigma^2}$ is always positive, a tiny variance such as $10^{-4}$ becomes a moderate $-9.2$, and the KL is written directly in $\log \sigma^2$.

## What the organized latent space buys

Because the KL term pulls every code distribution toward $\mathcal N(0, I)$, a code drawn from the prior mostly lands where the decoder was trained. Generation is two lines: draw $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, then $x \sim p_\theta(x \mid z) = \mathcal N\big(\hat x(z), s^2 I\big)$. The encoder is not needed. Figures show the mean $\hat x(z)$ instead of a draw, a display convention ([«Байесовские методы в анализе данных», 2026](#references), lecture 6): with $s^2 = \tfrac12$ on pixels in $[-1, 1]$, a draw would add independent noise of standard deviation $0.71$ to every pixel.

Interpolation works better too. Decoding $z_\lambda = (1-\lambda)\, z_a + \lambda\, z_b$ for $\lambda$ from 0 to 1 moves smoothly between two images, as long as the segment stays where codes are dense. In high dimension a straight segment can still leave that region: two independent codes from $\mathcal N(0, I_d)$ have lengths near $\sqrt d$, while their midpoint, distributed as $\mathcal N(0, \tfrac12 I_d)$, has length near $0.71\sqrt d$, inside the [shell](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#high-dimensions-the-shell) where prior samples almost never fall. Directions can carry meaning as well: the difference between the average codes of images with and without an attribute, such as a smile, is an attribute vector, and adding it to another image's code adds the attribute ([Carter and Nielsen, 2017](#references), with a VAE of fonts and a "bolding" vector).

<LatentInterpolation />

The price is blur. Generation assumes that the posteriors of the training images, together, cover the prior. Each is an ellipse with axes along the coordinates, and they rarely overlap where they are dense, since a code in an overlap would have to stand for two images at once. A prior sample often lands between them, where the decoder saw little training, and decodes to a vague image ([«Байесовские методы в анализе данных», 2026](#references), lecture 6). Where posteriors do overlap, one code can come from several images, and the squared-error optimum $\hat x(z)$, the mean we display, is their average, because the $c$ that minimizes $\mathbb E\lVert x - c\rVert^2$ is $\mathbb E[x]$.

At the level of the whole model, maximizing the average ELBO minimizes a forward KL from the data, paired with the encoder, to the model's joint over $(x, z)$ (folded below). A model that cannot fit the data exactly then spreads mass over everything plausible ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), section 2.8.2; [forward KL covers modes](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#forward-kl-covers-both-modes-reverse-kl-picks-one)).

<details>
<summary>The average ELBO as a forward KL</summary>

Pair the data distribution $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ with the encoder into the joint $q_\varphi(x, z) = p_{\text{data}}(x)\, q_\varphi(z \mid x)$. By the definition of KL and of the ELBO,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(x, z) \,\|\, p_\theta(x, z)\big) \\
&\quad= \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(x, z)}\!\left[\log p_{\text{data}}(x) + \log \frac{q_\varphi(z \mid x)}{p_\theta(x, z)}\right] \\
&\quad= -H(p_{\text{data}}) - \mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}(x)}\big[\mathcal L(x)\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The entropy of the data does not depend on $\theta$ or $\varphi$, so raising the average ELBO lowers this KL by the same amount. The data side comes first, the direction that [covers modes](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#forward-kl-covers-both-modes-reverse-kl-picks-one).

</details>

The adversarial models of a later section fail the other way: sharp samples that may cover only some modes of the data. Which failure is tolerable depends on the task. For pictures, realism often matters more than variety; for generating candidate molecules, a model that misses modes misses the new compounds it was built to find ([«Байесовские методы в анализе данных», 2026](#references), lecture 6). The hierarchical NVAE ([Vahdat and Kautz, 2020](#references)) was the first VAE applied to natural images of $256 \times 256$ pixels.

## Where the ELBO comes from

Drop $\theta$ and $\varphi$ for the derivation. Multiply the integrand of the evidence by $q(z \mid x)/q(z \mid x)$, which needs $q > 0$ wherever $p(x, z) > 0$, true for a Gaussian $q$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(x) &= \log \int p(x, z)\, dz \\
&= \log \mathbb E_{q(z \mid x)}\!\left[\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&\ge \mathbb E_{q(z \mid x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] = \mathcal L(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

The inequality is Jensen's for the concave logarithm, $\log \mathbb E[Y] \ge \mathbb E[\log Y]$.

<JensenLog />

Writing $p(x, z) = p(x \mid z)\, p(z)$ splits the bound into the two terms used above:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x) &= \mathbb E_{q}\!\left[\log p(x \mid z) + \log \frac{p(z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb E_{q}\big[\log p(x \mid z)\big] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Jensen shows that the bound holds; it does not say how loose it is. Writing $p(x, z) = p(z \mid x)\, p(x)$ instead gives the exact gap:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(x) - \mathcal L(x)
&= \mathbb E_{q}\!\left[\log p(x) - \log\frac{p(z\mid x)\,p(x)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb E_{q}\big[\log q(z \mid x) - \log p(z \mid x)\big] \\
&= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z \mid x)\big) \ge 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The bound is tight exactly when $q$ is the true posterior. The [variational inference note](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference#the-evidence-lower-bound-and-the-reverse-kl) derives the same decomposition for any latent-variable model and explains why only the reverse KL can be minimized this way.

The gap shows what each network does. Over the encoder, $\log p_\theta(x)$ is fixed, so raising $\mathcal L$ only brings $q_\varphi$ closer to the posterior in reverse KL. Over the decoder, $\mathcal L = \log p_\theta(x) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x)\big)$ can also rise by making the true posterior easier for $q$ to fit, which biases the model toward such posteriors. Maximizing the ELBO maximizes the likelihood only approximately ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), section 2.2.1).

## Which distribution is fixed? The prior \{#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior\}

The prior $p(z) = \mathcal N(0, I)$ is fixed. The posteriors are not: every image gets its own $\mu(x)$ and $\sigma^2(x)$, and the KL term only charges for how far they stray.

For one coordinate, with $q = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $p = \mathcal N(0, 1)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q(z) &= -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}, \\
\log p(z) &= -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi) - \frac{z^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Under $z \sim q$ we have $\mathbb E[(z-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb E[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p) &= \mathbb E_q[\log q - \log p] \\
&= -\tfrac12\log \sigma^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2) \\
&= \tfrac12\big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

For the diagonal encoder, both $q = \prod_j \mathcal N(\mu_j, \sigma_j^2)$ and $p = \prod_j \mathcal N(0, 1)$ factorize. The log ratio $\log q - \log p$ is then a sum over $j$ of terms that each depend on $z_j$ alone, and linearity of expectation adds the one-coordinate results ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), appendix B):

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\|\, \mathcal N(0, I)\big) = \frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log\sigma_j^2\big) .
$$
 The value is zero only at $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$, because $\log t \le t - 1$ with equality only at $t = 1$. A full covariance $\Sigma$ gives $\tfrac12\big(\operatorname{tr}\Sigma + \mu^\top\mu - d - \log\det\Sigma\big)$ by the same steps with a trace in place of each square (folded below); correlation then has its own price, $-\tfrac12\log(1 - \rho^2)$ for two unit-variance coordinates with correlation $\rho$, which the diagonal encoder never pays because it cannot correlate at all.

<details>
<summary>The trace step for a full covariance</summary>

A scalar equals its own trace, and the trace is linear and cyclic, so for $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\big[(z - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (z - \mu)\big]
&= \operatorname{tr}\big(\Sigma^{-1}\, \mathbb E[(z - \mu)(z - \mu)^\top]\big) \\
&= \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma) = d, \\
\mathbb E[z^\top z] &= \operatorname{tr}\, \mathbb E[z z^\top] = \operatorname{tr}\Sigma + \mu^\top \mu .
\end{aligned}
$$

With $\log q(z) = -\tfrac12\log\det(2\pi\Sigma) - \tfrac12 (z - \mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(z - \mu)$ and $\log p(z) = -\tfrac d2 \log 2\pi - \tfrac12 z^\top z$, the $2\pi$ terms cancel and

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p) &= -\tfrac12\log\det\Sigma - \tfrac d2 + \tfrac12\big(\operatorname{tr}\Sigma + \mu^\top\mu\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $\mu = 0$ and $\Sigma$ with unit diagonal and off-diagonal $\rho$, $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma = 2 = d$ and $\det\Sigma = 1 - \rho^2$, which leaves $-\tfrac12\log(1 - \rho^2)$.

</details>

Read the formula as a fine:

| $\mu$ | $\sigma$ | $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ (nats) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 1 | 1 | 0.5 |
| 2 | 1 | 2 |
| 0 | 0.5 | 0.318 |
| 0 | 2 | 0.807 |
| 0 | 0.1 | 1.808 |
| 0 | 0.01 | 4.105 |

Moving the mean costs $\tfrac12\mu^2$. Shrinking the variance costs about $-\log\sigma - \tfrac12$, which grows only logarithmically, yet without bound: a point code, as in a plain autoencoder, would pay an infinite fine, since a point mass has [no finite KL](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#e-continuous-versus-discrete) against a density. Each image can therefore afford a fairly narrow, off-centre Gaussian when that helps reconstruction, but never a single point.

<KlFine />

What does the KL term ask of the codes as a whole? Take $N$ training images and their **aggregate posterior**, the mixture of all their posteriors:

$$
q(z) = \frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} q(z \mid x_n) .
$$

Treat the index $n$ as uniform on $\lbrace 1, \dots, N\rbrace$. The average KL term then splits in two ([Hoffman and Johnson, 2016](#references)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x_n) \,\|\, p(z)\big) \\
&\quad= I_q(n; z) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\|\, p(z)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the split</summary>

Write $q(z \mid x_n)/p(z)$ as $\big(q(z \mid x_n)/q(z)\big)\cdot\big(q(z)/p(z)\big)$ and average the logarithm over $n$ and $z \sim q(z \mid x_n)$. The first factor averages to the mutual information between an image's index and its code, by its definition. The second depends on $z$ alone, and averaging over $n$ then $z$ is averaging over the mixture $q(z)$, which gives the marginal KL.

</details>

The first part, the mutual information, is at most $H(n) = \log N$. Good reconstruction needs the code to identify the image, so the mutual information must stay large, and each posterior stays narrow. Only the second part, the **marginal KL**, concerns sampling: the mixture $q(z)$ should fill $\mathcal N(0, I)$ without holes. Training pushes it there only approximately, and prior samples that land in holes decode poorly; this mismatch is often named as a main cause of poor VAE samples ([Isachenko, 2025](#references), lecture 4).

The prior appears only in the marginal KL, so the best prior for a trained encoder is the aggregate posterior itself. That suggests learning the prior instead of fixing it, for instance with a [normalizing flow](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows) over $z$. The [VQ-VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae-variants#where-the-prior-went) of the next note learns its prior after the autoencoder is trained.

Fixing the encoder instead gives a model of a later section. A diffusion model is a VAE whose encoder is a fixed noising process and whose decoder undoes it in many steps ([the DDPM objective](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective)), and [latent diffusion](/notes/generative-models/latent-diffusion) runs such a model in the latent space of an autoencoder, which is smaller and closer to a Gaussian than the space of pixels ([«Байесовские методы в анализе данных», 2026](#references), lecture 6).

## Where it breaks: posterior collapse

A decoder strong enough to model the images on its own can ignore the code. The encoder then outputs the prior for every image, the KL term is zero, and the code carries nothing. This is **posterior collapse** ([Bowman et al., 2016](#references)).

The ELBO does not prevent it, and a one-dimensional Gaussian model shows why exactly. Let the data be $x \sim \mathcal N(0, 1.1)$, the prior $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, and the decoder $p(x \mid z) = \mathcal N(wz, s^2)$ with $w^2 + s^2 = 1.1$. Every such decoder gives the data its true distribution, so all of them have the same likelihood. The exact posterior is Gaussian, so the best Gaussian encoder equals it and the ELBO is tight. The rate, the average KL term, then equals the mutual information $\tfrac12\log(1 + w^2/s^2)$ (folded below):

| decoder | $w$ | $s^2$ | rate (nats) | $\log p(x = 1)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| uses the code | 1 | 0.1 | 1.199 | $-1.4211$ |
| partly | 0.5 | 0.85 | 0.129 | $-1.4211$ |
| collapsed | 0 | 1.1 | 0 | $-1.4211$ |

<details>
<summary>Where the rate comes from</summary>

With $q$ the exact posterior, the aggregate posterior is the prior: averaging $p(z \mid x)$ over the model's own $p(x)$, which here is the data distribution, returns $p(z)$. The marginal KL of the [ELBO surgery](#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior) vanishes, and the rate is the mutual information $I(x; z) = H(z) - H(z \mid x)$. By the [conditional formula](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals) with $\operatorname{Var} z = 1$, $\operatorname{Cov}(x, z) = w$ and $\operatorname{Var} x = w^2 + s^2$, the posterior variance is $1 - w^2/(w^2 + s^2) = s^2/(w^2 + s^2)$, the same for every $x$. A Gaussian of variance $v$ has entropy $\tfrac12\log(2\pi e v)$; the $2\pi e$ cancels in the difference, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
I(x; z) &= \tfrac12 \log 1 - \tfrac12 \log \frac{s^2}{w^2 + s^2} \\
&= \tfrac12 \log\Big(1 + \frac{w^2}{s^2}\Big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $w = 1$, $s^2 = 0.1$ this is $\tfrac12\ln 11 = 1.199$; the likelihood column is $\log \mathcal N(1;\, 0,\, 1.1)$ for all three.

</details>

The bound is equally good at all three points, so it gives no reason to use the code. Our decoder, a factorized Gaussian with fixed variance, pays heavily for collapse on real images: without the code it can only output the average image. An autoregressive decoder can, and early in training, when $\log p(x \mid z)$ is still weak, $q \approx p(z)$ is an attractive state that is hard to leave ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), section 2.8.1). Two standard remedies change the KL term: **KL annealing** raises its weight from 0 to 1 over training ([Bowman et al., 2016](#references)), and **free bits** penalize the KL of each group of latent coordinates only above a floor of $\lambda$ nats, so the code may carry that much information at no cost ([Kingma and Welling, 2019](#references), section 2.8.1).

## Conditioning on a label: the conditional VAE

The basic VAE cannot be told what to generate. The **conditional VAE** (CVAE) adds an observed condition $c$, such as a class label or a vector of binary attributes, to every part of the model ([Sohn, Lee and Yan, 2015](#references), section 4): a prior $p_\theta(z \mid c)$, often simply $\mathcal N(0, I)$ for every $c$; a decoder $p_\theta(x \mid z, c)$; and an encoder $q_\varphi(z \mid x, c)$. The [derivation of the ELBO](#where-the-elbo-comes-from) goes through with everything conditioned on $c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p_\theta(x \mid c) \ge{}& \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z \mid x, c)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z, c)\big] \\
&- D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x, c) \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid c)\big),
\end{aligned}
$$

with the gap $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x, c) \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x, c)\big)$. With the fixed prior, training uses the same reparameterization and the same closed-form KL; a learned Gaussian prior needs the [general KL between two Gaussians](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#what-the-gradients-are). In the networks, $c$ is usually concatenated to the encoder's input and to $z$ before the decoder.

The decoder receives $c$ for free, while every nat in the code costs KL. The objective therefore rewards leaving out of $z$ whatever $c$ already says, and the code tends to keep what the label does not describe, such as pose or lighting. To generate, fix $c$, draw $z$ from the prior and decode. Keeping $z$ and changing $c$, for example one of the 40 binary attributes of CelebA faces, asks for the same image with a different attribute; how cleanly that works depends on how much of the attribute leaked into $z$. The [conditional GAN](/notes/generative-models/gans#conditional-gan) is the adversarial counterpart.

## What you should remember

- $\log p_\theta(x) = \mathcal L(x) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x) \,\|\, p_\theta(z \mid x)\big)$, and $\mathcal L$ is reconstruction minus the KL to the prior.
- Naive Monte Carlo over the prior needs exponentially many samples in $d$; an encoder learns where to look.
- $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon$ makes the sampled term differentiable; the KL is $\tfrac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log\sigma_j^2)$.
- A Gaussian decoder with $s^2 = \tfrac12$ turns the reconstruction term into a summed squared error; other $s^2$ act as a KL weight.
- Sampling quality depends on the aggregate posterior filling the prior; blur comes from averaging over overlapping posteriors.
- The ELBO cannot tell a used code from a collapsed one; annealing and free bits push the code into use.

## Notebook

The implementation follows the [VAE notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/spring-2025-long/seminars/06_Variational-Autoencoders/VAE.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* repository. It has no saved outputs, so it documents the procedure only.

- Data: CelebA faces resized to $64 \times 64$ and mapped to $[-1, 1]$, batches of 128. `Resize((64, 64))` squeezes the $178 \times 218$ faces to a square; `Resize(64)` followed by `CenterCrop(64)` would keep the aspect ratio. The dataset class returns the 40 attributes, which this notebook ignores.
- Model: four $4 \times 4$ stride-2 convolutions ($32 \to 256$ channels) and two linear heads for $\mu$ and $\log\sigma^2$ with a 100-dimensional code; the decoder is a linear layer and four blocks of nearest-neighbour upsampling and $3 \times 3$ convolution with a $\tanh$ output.
- Loss: the squared error summed over pixels plus the closed-form KL, both averaged over the batch, which is the one-sample negative ELBO with $s^2 = \tfrac12$. A `beta` argument weights the KL but is left at 1, so no β-VAE is trained. The markdown rewrites $\sum_i \log\sigma_i^2$ as $\lVert\log\sigma\rVert_1$, which is wrong in both the factor 2 and the sign; the code uses the correct sum.
- Training: Adam at $10^{-3}$ with a cosine schedule for 10 epochs, logging the two terms separately and decoding 64 prior samples each epoch. The logged losses are divided by the batch size a second time, and sampling runs without `eval()`, so batch norm normalizes each generated batch by its own statistics.
