The [autoencoder note](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders) ended with a gap: an ordinary autoencoder compresses and reconstructs well, but it is not a generator. We cannot pick a point in its latent space and expect a sensible picture. The variational autoencoder (VAE) of [Kingma and Welling (2014)](#references), proposed independently by [Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra (2014)](#references), fixes this by **organizing the latent space**. Each input is encoded into a Gaussian rather than a single point, and these Gaussians are pulled toward one fixed prior. The VAE is also the first neural generative model in the course trained by (a bound on) maximum likelihood, the objective the [entropy note](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#why-cross-entropy-is-the-loss-we-minimize) identified as the natural one.

**Notation.** $x$ is a data point (an image), $z \in \mathbb R^d$ its latent code and $x'$ the reconstruction. $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ always means mean $\mu$ and **variance** $\sigma^2$. For vectors, $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is shorthand for $\mathcal N(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2))$, and $\mathcal N(0, I)$ is the standard normal in $d$ dimensions. $\odot$ is the element-wise product.

## From autoencoders to generation

Recall the encoder–decoder pipeline:

$$
\underset{\text{data}}{x} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{encoder}\ }\; z \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{decoder}\ }\; \underset{\text{reconstruction}}{x'} .
$$

The code $z$ is a **low-dimensional representation**, typically 10–100 numbers, and the decoder's job is **reconstruction**. How could a trained autoencoder generate new images? There are two obvious options, and both fail.

1. **Sample a random $z$ and decode it.** The result is garbage, because the latent space is **disorganized**. Training only asks that the codes of training images decode back to those images. Nothing says what lies between the codes, or even where in $\mathbb R^d$ they are.
2. **Sample latent vectors near a reference.** Encode a real image to $z_{\text{ref}}$ and decode nearby points $z_1, z_2, z_3$. A nearby point is still not guaranteed to be a meaningful code, so the decoded neighbour of a "7" can be a scribble.

<LatentGarbage />

The fix is to make the distribution of codes part of the model. That requires the language of latent-variable models.

## Joint, marginal and conditional

Take two random variables, the data $x$ and a latent $z$. Their **joint** density $p(x, z)$ is a cloud in the $(x, z)$ plane. Projecting the cloud onto each axis gives the **marginal distributions** $p(x)$ and $p(z)$. **Marginalization** computes a marginal by integrating the other variable out:

$$
p(x) = \int p(x, z)\, dz .
$$

**Conditional probability** is the joint restricted to a fixed value of the condition and renormalized:

$$
p(x \mid z) = \frac{p(x, z)}{p(z)} .
$$

For example, $p(x \mid z = 3)$ is the slice of $p(x,z)$ along the line $z = 3$, divided by $p(z = 3)$ so that it integrates to one over $x$.

## The latent-variable model and its posterior

A latent-variable model *generates* data in two steps: draw a code from a **prior** $p(z)$, then draw an image from the **decoder** (likelihood) $p_\theta(x \mid z)$. The prior is chosen, not learned; the standard choice is $p(z) = \mathcal N(0, I)$. The model's distribution over images is the marginal

$$
p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz ,
$$

called the **evidence**. Training by maximum likelihood means maximizing $\log p_\theta(x)$ over the data.

The reverse direction, "which codes explain this image", is the **posterior**. By Bayes' theorem

$$
p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)}{p_\theta(x)} ,
$$

and the evidence in the denominator is an integral over the whole latent space. With a neural-network decoder there is no closed form, and in high dimension the integral cannot be computed numerically either. Both the likelihood we want to maximize and the posterior we would need for encoding are therefore intractable.

## Variational approximation and the ELBO

The way out is to approximate the posterior by a distribution from a simple family. The VAE uses a diagonal Gaussian whose mean and variances are produced by an **encoder** network with parameters $\varphi$:

$$
q_\varphi(z \mid x) = \mathcal N\big(\mu_\varphi(x), \sigma_\varphi^2(x)\big) \approx p_\theta(z \mid x).
$$

Replacing an intractable posterior by the best member of a simple family is **variational inference** ([Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe, 2017](#references)). Because one network produces the approximation for every $x$, it is called *amortized*. The quantity maximized is the **evidence lower bound (ELBO)**:

$$
\mathcal L(x) =
\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}}
\;-\;
\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regularization}} \;\le\; \log p_\theta(x).
$$

The inequality is derived [below](#where-the-elbo-comes-from). Its two terms pull in opposite directions.

**Reconstruction** measures how well an image survives the trip image → code → image. With a Gaussian decoder it **reduces to the $L_2$ reconstruction loss**. If $p_\theta(x \mid z) = \mathcal N\big(x;\, x'(z),\, s^2 I\big)$ with $x \in \mathbb R^D$, then

$$
-\log p_\theta(x \mid z) = \frac{1}{2s^2}\,\big\|x - x'(z)\big\|_2^2 + \frac D2 \log(2\pi s^2),
$$

and the last term does not depend on the network. With $s^2 = \tfrac12$ the weight of the squared error is exactly 1, so a summed squared error plus the KL term is the negative ELBO up to a constant. A different fixed $s^2$ changes the balance between the two terms, which is the same as weighting the KL term (see β-VAE below).

**Regularization** measures how far each posterior $q_\varphi(z \mid x)$ is from the prior. It pulls every code distribution toward $\mathcal N(0, I)$, so all codes live in one well-known region of the latent space.

<ElboTerms />

So instead of mapping the input to a single point, the VAE maps it to a **Gaussian**. The encoder outputs $\mu$ and $\log\sigma^2$, we sample $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and the decoder reconstructs from $z$. The training loss is the negative ELBO, estimated with one sampled $z$ per input:

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{VAE}}(x) = \underbrace{\big\|x - x'(z)\big\|_2^2}_{\text{reconstruction}} + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0, I)\big), \qquad z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2) .
$$

The KL term now has a plus sign because we *minimize*.

## The reparameterization trick

The loss contains a sample $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and we **cannot backpropagate through sampling**: a random draw is not a differentiable function of $\mu$ and $\sigma$. The fix is to move the randomness into an input, exactly as in the [Gaussian note](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions#sampling-from-standard-noise-to-any-gaussian):

$$
z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon,
\qquad
\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I).
$$

$\varepsilon$ is sampled independently of the network, then **scaled** by $\sigma$ and **shifted** by $\mu$. This gives the same distribution, $z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, but now $z$ is a **differentiable function** of $\mu$ and $\sigma$: $\partial z_j / \partial \mu_j = 1$ and $\partial z_j / \partial \sigma_j = \varepsilon_j$. Gradients of the reconstruction term flow back into the encoder, and the resulting gradient estimate has much lower variance than generic alternatives such as the score-function (REINFORCE) estimator ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), §2.2–2.4).

<Reparameterization />

The KL part of the loss needs no sampling at all: between two Gaussians it has a closed form, derived [below](#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior). Summed over the $d$ latent dimensions,

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{KL}} = -\frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{d} \big(1 + \log \sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2\big) .
$$

## What the organized latent space buys

- We can **sample** $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and decode it: the prior is exactly where the codes were pulled during training. We can also sample near the code of a reference image and get sensible variations of it.
- We can **interpolate**: decode $z_\lambda = (1-\lambda)\, z_a + \lambda\, z_b$ for $\lambda$ from 0 to 1 and move smoothly between two images or classes.

<LatentInterpolation />

**Drawbacks.**

- **Blurry images.** The posteriors of different images overlap, so one code can be explained by several images, and a decoder trained with squared error outputs their average. This is the mode-covering behaviour of the forward KL from [Fitting distributions with KL](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#forward-kl-covers-both-modes-reverse-kl-picks-one): maximum likelihood spreads probability over everything plausible.
- **Weaker sample quality than GANs** in the years after their introduction. Hierarchical VAEs later closed much of the gap ([Vahdat and Kautz, 2020](#references)).
- **No conditioning** in the basic model: it cannot be told what to generate.

Extensions address these: the conditional VAE ([Sohn, Lee and Yan, 2015](#references)), β-VAE ([Higgins et al., 2017](#references)) and VQ-VAE with discrete codes ([van den Oord, Vinyals and Kavukcuoglu, 2017](#references)). β-VAE simply multiplies the KL term by a weight $\beta$: $\beta > 1$ pulls harder toward the prior and encourages disentangled, more independent latent coordinates, at the cost of reconstruction; $\beta < 1$ favours reconstruction.

## Where the ELBO comes from

Start from the evidence and multiply by $1 = q(z\mid x)/q(z\mid x)$ inside the integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(x) &= \log \int p(x, z)\, dz
= \log \int \frac{q(z \mid x)}{q(z \mid x)}\, p(x, z)\, dz \\
&= \log \mathbb E_{q(z \mid x)}\!\left[\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&\ge \mathbb E_{q(z \mid x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] = \mathcal L(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

The inequality is **Jensen's inequality**. For a convex $f$ it reads $\mathbb E[f(Y)] \ge f(\mathbb E[Y])$. The logarithm is **concave**, so the direction flips:

$$
\log \mathbb E[Y] \;\ge\; \mathbb E[\log Y].
$$

<JensenLog />

Writing $p(x, z) = p(x \mid z)\, p(z)$ splits the bound into the two familiar terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x) &= \mathbb E_{q(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log p(x \mid z) + \log \frac{p(z)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&= \mathbb E_{q(z\mid x)}\big[\log p(x \mid z)\big] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

The evidence is never less than the ELBO, so maximizing the ELBO maximizes a guaranteed floor under $\log p(x)$.

**How loose is the bound?** Exactly by a KL divergence. Since $p(x, z) = p(z \mid x)\, p(x)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(x) - \mathcal L(x)
&= \mathbb E_{q(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log p(x) - \log\frac{p(z\mid x)\,p(x)}{q(z \mid x)}\right] \\
&= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z \mid x)\big) \;\ge\; 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Maximizing the ELBO over the decoder tends to raise $\log p(x)$ itself. Over the encoder, $\log p(x)$ does not change, so the encoder can only shrink the gap: it makes $q(z \mid x)$ a better approximation of the true posterior in **reverse** KL. The bound is tight exactly when $q(z\mid x) = p(z\mid x)$.

## What is fixed: the prior, not the posterior

What is fixed is the **prior** $p(z) = \mathcal N(0, I)$. The posterior is not fixed: for each input the encoder outputs two vectors, $\mu(x)$ and $\log \sigma^2(x)$.

Why $\log \sigma^2$ and not $\sigma^2$?

- $\sigma^2$ is a variance and must be positive. A linear layer can output any real number, and $\sigma^2 = e^{\log \sigma^2} > 0$ always.
- It is numerically well scaled: a tiny variance such as $10^{-4}$ becomes a moderate number, $\log 10^{-4} \approx -9.2$.
- The closed-form KL between Gaussians is written directly in terms of $\log\sigma^2$.

**A constant posterior is useless.** Suppose the encoder ignored its input and output $q(z \mid x) = \mathcal N(0, I)$ for every $x$. The KL term would be zero, but $z$ would carry no information about $x$: the decoder would receive pure noise and could at best output the average image. This failure is known as **posterior collapse**, and it happens in practice when the decoder is powerful enough to model the data without the code ([Bowman et al., 2016](#references)). The input must stay recoverable from its code.

**The closed form.** For one latent dimension,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0, 1)\big) = \frac12\big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log \sigma^2\big),
$$

and for a diagonal Gaussian the dimensions add up ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references), Appendix B). With $q = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $p = \mathcal N(0,1)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q(z) &= -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}, \\
\log p(z) &= -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi) - \frac{z^2}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Under $z \sim q$ we have $\mathbb E[(z-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb E[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p) &= \mathbb E_q[\log q - \log p]
= -\tfrac12\log \sigma^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2) \\
&= \tfrac12\big(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Pulling the minus sign inside the bracket gives the $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{KL}}$ formula above. The value is zero only at $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$, because $\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2 \ge 0$ with equality only at $\sigma^2 = 1$.

How to read it: the model **pays a fine** if $\mu \ne 0$ or $\sigma \ne 1$, and the fine has a specific shape.

- **Moving the mean** costs $\tfrac12\mu^2$: 0.5 at $\mu = 1$ and 2 at $\mu = 2$.
- **Shrinking the variance** costs only logarithmically, about $-\log\sigma - \tfrac12$: 1.81 at $\sigma = 0.1$ and 4.11 at $\sigma = 0.01$. The fine grows without bound as $\sigma \to 0$, so a deterministic point code, as in a plain autoencoder, would pay an infinite fine. Compare the infinite KL between distributions with disjoint supports in [Fitting distributions with KL](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#a-common-support).
- **Widening** costs $\tfrac12\sigma^2 - \log\sigma - \tfrac12$, which grows like $\tfrac12\sigma^2$.

So each input can afford its own fairly narrow, off-centre Gaussian when that helps reconstruction, but never a single point.

| $\mu$ | $\sigma$ | $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ (nats) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 1 | 1 | 0.5 |
| 2 | 1 | 2 |
| 0 | 0.5 | 0.318 |
| 0 | 2 | 0.807 |
| 0 | 0.1 | 1.808 |
| 0 | 0.01 | 4.105 |

The KL compares **densities**, not parameter vectors, and it pulls each $q(z \mid x)$ toward the prior only as far as reconstruction allows. What should actually match the prior is the **aggregate posterior**

$$
q(z) = \mathbb E_{x \sim \text{data}}\big[q(z \mid x)\big] \approx p(z) :
$$

the posteriors of all training inputs, taken together, should fill $\mathcal N(0, I)$ without leaving holes ([Hoffman and Johnson, 2016](#references)). Each individual posterior is narrow and off-centre; their mixture covers the prior. That is why sampling $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and decoding works.

## Summary

- The likelihood $p_\theta(x)$ and the posterior $p_\theta(z \mid x)$ of a latent-variable model are intractable, because both require integrating over the latent space.
- An encoder produces an approximate posterior $q_\varphi(z \mid x)$, a diagonal Gaussian.
- Training maximizes the ELBO, reconstruction minus the KL to the prior, which is a lower bound on $\log p_\theta(x)$ with gap $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x))$.
- The reparameterization trick makes the sampled reconstruction term differentiable; the KL term is available in closed form.
- The fixed prior organizes the latent space, so decoding $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ generates new images.
