You have trained two [VAE variants](/notes/generative-models/vae-variants) and drawn a grid of samples from each. One grid looks sharper, the other more varied. Which model is better, and by how much?

For a VAE the first answer is the likelihood: compare the ELBO of the two models on held-out images. That answer has two problems. The generative adversarial networks later in the course define no likelihood at all; they only produce samples. And even where a likelihood exists, it can disagree with what the samples look like ([Theis, van den Oord and Bethge, 2016](#references)).

## Why the likelihood is not enough

Take an excellent model $q_0$ and a useless one $q_1$ that outputs white noise, and mix them:

$$
q_2(x) = 0.01\, q_0(x) + 0.99\, q_1(x) .
$$

Ninety-nine samples in a hundred from $q_2$ are noise. Its log-likelihood barely moves, because dropping the second term can only lower the logarithm:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q_2(x) &\ge \log\big(0.01\, q_0(x)\big) \\
&= \log q_0(x) - 4.61 .
\end{aligned}
$$

For a $32 \times 32$ colour image, with $D = 3072$ values, the log-likelihood is a sum over thousands of dimensions, and 4.61 nats is $0.0015$ nats per dimension. The model got far worse to look at; its likelihood is almost the same.

The opposite also happens. A mixture of narrow Gaussians centred on the training images, $q(x) = \frac1N \sum_n \mathcal N(x \mid x_n, \epsilon^2 I)$, produces perfect-looking samples, because they are the training images plus invisible noise. Its likelihood on test images is terrible, because the test images lie far from every centre ([Murphy, 2023](#references), Section 20.4.1).

To see how terrible, let $\delta > 0$ be the distance from a test image $x$ to its nearest training image. Every term of the mixture is a Gaussian density whose exponent is at most $-\delta^2/(2\epsilon^2)$, and an average is at most its largest term, so

$$
\log q(x) \le -\frac{\delta^2}{2\epsilon^2} - \frac D2 \log(2\pi\epsilon^2) .
$$

As $\epsilon \to 0$ the first term falls like $1/\epsilon^2$ and the second grows only like $\log(1/\epsilon)$, so the test log-likelihood tends to $-\infty$ while the samples get ever sharper.

So sample quality has to be measured on samples. People can do it: [Salimans et al. (2016)](#references) asked crowd workers to tell generated images from real ones, and found the answers depended on the interface and on whether workers saw their mistakes. The [last note of this section](/notes/generative-models/human-evaluation) returns to people. The Inception Score was proposed in the same paper as an automatic stand-in for them.

## What the Inception Score asks for

A good set of generated images should have two properties. Each image should clearly show *something*: a classifier looking at it should be confident. And the set should contain many different things: across the images, the classifier's answers should be spread over many classes.

Neither property is enough alone. A generator that draws the same sharp beagle every time passes the first test and fails the second. A generator that draws shapeless blobs might spread the classifier's guesses evenly, yet no single image is recognizable.

## The two entropies

The score uses a pretrained classifier, by convention **Inception v3** trained on the 1000 ImageNet classes ([Szegedy et al., 2016](#references)). For a generated image $x$ it outputs a softmax distribution $p(y \mid x)$ over the classes $y \in \{1, \dots, n\}$.

<IsPipeline />

Confidence on one image is low entropy of its output:

$$
H\big(p(y \mid x)\big) = -\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log p(y \mid x) .
$$

A one-hot output has entropy 0; a flat output has the maximum $\log n$. Logarithms are natural throughout.

Variety across images is high entropy of the **marginal** class distribution, the classifier's output averaged over generated images. Let $X$ be a random generated image and $Y$ the class the classifier assigns to it. By the law of total probability,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(y) = P(Y = y)
&= \mathbb E_X\big[P(Y = y \mid X)\big] \\
&= \mathbb E_x\big[p(y \mid x)\big] \\
&\approx \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N p(y \mid x_i) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $x_1, \dots, x_N$ are samples from the generator. Only samples are needed, never a density of the generator, which is what makes the score usable for models without a likelihood.

## From two entropies to one KL divergence

We want $H(p(y))$ large and the average $H(p(y \mid x))$ small, so we look at their difference. Expand both entropies, write the weight $p(y)$ of the first sum as $\mathbb E_x[p(y \mid x)]$, and use linearity of expectation to pull both sums under one $\mathbb E_x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&H\big(p(y)\big) - \mathbb E_x\Big[H\big(p(y \mid x)\big)\Big] \\
&\quad= -\sum_y p(y) \log p(y) \\
&\qquad + \mathbb E_x\Big[\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log p(y \mid x)\Big] \\
&\quad= \mathbb E_x\Big[\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log p(y \mid x)\Big] \\
&\qquad - \mathbb E_x\Big[\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log p(y)\Big] \\
&\quad= \mathbb E_x\Big[\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)}\Big] \\
&\quad= \mathbb E_x\Big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y)\big)\Big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

Only the second equality needs care. The factor $\log p(y)$ does not depend on $x$, and the sum over classes is finite, so the expectation can move outside:

$$
\sum_y \mathbb E_x\big[p(y \mid x)\big] \log p(y) = \mathbb E_x\Big[\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log p(y)\Big] .
$$

<IsIdentity />

The KL form says what the score compares: each image's class distribution against the average one. An image scores high when its prediction is sharp and sits on a class the average does not concentrate on, so it differs from the other images.

The left-hand side is also a familiar quantity. The entropy of $Y$ minus its conditional entropy given $X$ is the **mutual information** $I(X; Y)$ between a generated image and its predicted class: how much seeing the image tells us about the label ([Barratt and Sharma, 2018](#references)).

## The score

The Inception Score exponentiates the average divergence:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{IS} &= \exp\Big(\mathbb E_x\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y)\big)\big]\Big) \\
&\approx \exp\Big(\frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(y \mid x_i) \,\|\, p(y)\big)\Big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Higher is better. Splitting the exponent into its two entropies gives a reading in numbers of classes:

$$
\mathrm{IS} = \frac{e^{H(p(y))}}{e^{\,\mathbb E_x H(p(y \mid x))}} .
$$

The exponential of an entropy is an effective number of classes: $e^{\log 3} = 3$ for a uniform distribution over three. The numerator counts how many classes the generator covers; the denominator, how many classes a typical single image looks like. Stanford's CS236 writes the same product as diversity times sharpness ([Ermon, 2023](#references)).

The divergence is always finite. A KL divergence is finite when $p(y) > 0$ wherever $p(y \mid x) > 0$, and $p(y)$ is an average that includes $p(y \mid x)$ itself. In floating point a softmax output can underflow to exactly 0, so the code must use $0 \log 0 = 0$.

### The range

Three facts from [Entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences) bound the exponent. A KL divergence is never negative, and it is 0 only when its two arguments are equal ([the KL section](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#kullbackleibler-divergence)). A discrete entropy is never negative, because every term $-p \log p$ with $0 \le p \le 1$ is non-negative, so by the identity the exponent is at most $H(p(y))$. And the entropy of a distribution over $n$ classes is at most $\log n$, with equality only for the uniform distribution ([Gibbs' inequality](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#cross-entropy-surprise-under-a-wrong-belief)). Together,

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &\le \mathbb E_x\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big] \le H\big(p(y)\big) \le \log n , \\
1 &\le \mathrm{IS} \le n .
\end{aligned}
$$

The equality cases follow from the same facts. $\mathrm{IS} = 1$ means the average divergence is 0. An average of non-negative numbers is 0 only when each of them is 0, and each KL is 0 only when $p(y \mid x) = p(y)$. So $\mathrm{IS} = 1$ exactly when every image gets the same class distribution, peaked (mode collapse) or flat (nothing recognizable).

$\mathrm{IS} = n$ needs both inequalities on the right to be equalities: the marginal must be uniform, and every $H(p(y \mid x))$ must be 0, which means one-hot outputs. A softmax is never exactly one-hot, so the score only approaches $n = 1000$ for Inception v3.

The base of the logarithm does not matter as long as the exponential matches it. An exponent of $a$ nats is $a / \ln 2$ bits, and $2^{a / \ln 2} = e^{a}$ gives the same score.

## A worked example with three classes

Take a classifier with $n = 3$ classes (beagle, corgi, husky) and three generated images per generator. Entropies and divergences are in nats.

| Generator | Outputs $p(y \mid x_i)$ | Marginal $p(y)$ | $H(p(y))$ | mean $H(p(y \mid x))$ | IS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A: confident, diverse | $(0.9, 0.05, 0.05),$ $(0.05, 0.9, 0.05),$ $(0.05, 0.05, 0.9)$ | $(\frac13, \frac13, \frac13)$ | $1.099$ | $0.394$ | $2.02$ |
| B: mode collapse | $(0.9, 0.05, 0.05)$ three times | $(0.9, 0.05, 0.05)$ | $0.394$ | $0.394$ | $1.00$ |
| C: not confident | $(\frac13, \frac13, \frac13)$ three times | $(\frac13, \frac13, \frac13)$ | $1.099$ | $1.099$ | $1.00$ |
| D: partial collapse | $(0.9, 0.05, 0.05)$ twice, $(0.05, 0.9, 0.05)$ | $(0.617, 0.333, 0.05)$ | $0.814$ | $0.394$ | $1.52$ |

Each confident output has the same entropy:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H &= -\big(0.9 \ln 0.9 + 2 \cdot 0.05 \ln 0.05\big) \\
&= 0.0948 + 0.2996 \\
&= 0.394 .
\end{aligned}
$$

For A the marginal is uniform, $H(p(y)) = \ln 3 = 1.099$, and the score is $e^{1.099 - 0.394} = e^{0.704} = 2.02$. In the effective-number reading, A covers 3 classes and each image looks like $e^{0.394} = 1.48$ classes: $3 / 1.48 = 2.02$.

B and C both get the minimum 1, for opposite reasons. B is confident but always draws a beagle, so its marginal is as peaked as each output. C spreads its guesses but no image looks like anything. D never draws a husky and lands between. Its three divergences are $0.245$, $0.245$ and $0.768$; only their mean, $0.420$, equals $0.814 - 0.394$.

<IsWorkedExample />

The estimator is a few lines; SciPy's `rel_entr` computes $a \log(a / b)$ with the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import rel_entr

def inception_score(probs):              # probs[i] = p(y | x_i), shape (N, n)
    p_y = probs.mean(axis=0)              # the marginal p(y)
    kl = rel_entr(probs, p_y).sum(axis=1) # KL(p(y | x_i) || p(y)) for every i
    return np.exp(kl.mean())

a, b, c = [0.9, 0.05, 0.05], [0.05, 0.9, 0.05], [0.05, 0.05, 0.9]
print(round(inception_score(np.array([a, b, c])), 2))   # generator A
print(round(inception_score(np.array([a, a, b])), 2))   # generator D
```

```console
2.02
1.52
```

## Where it breaks \{#limitations\}

The score never looks at real data, and the worked example already shows what that costs. Replace generator A by one that outputs exactly three fixed images, one perfect beagle, one perfect corgi and one perfect husky, each with the outputs of A. Its marginal is uniform and its score is $2.02$, the same as A's. It has no variety within any class, and its three images could be copied straight from the training set. A generator that memorizes training images scores as well as the data itself ([Barratt and Sharma, 2018](#references)). Within-class variety, faithfulness to the training distribution and memorization are all invisible to IS.

Other failures follow from the classifier.

It can be gamed. Salimans et al. themselves caution that the score is a rough guide for models trained by some other criterion: optimizing it directly produces adversarial examples. Barratt and Sharma show this concretely: small perturbations of generated images push the score to 900.15, close to its maximum of 1000, without making them look more like natural images (their Section 4.2.2 and Figure 1).

It depends on the network and its implementation. The Keras and the PyTorch ports of Inception v3 have almost the same ImageNet accuracy, yet on the same 50,000 images their mean scores differ by 3.5% for ImageNet validation images and by 11.5% for CIFAR-10 images ([Barratt and Sharma, 2018](#references), Section 4.1.1, Table 1). For data far from ImageNet, faces, digits or medical images, the 1000 classes do not describe what varies in the data at all.

It depends on the protocol. Diversity is measured through the marginal, which needs many samples: Salimans et al. evaluate on 50,000 images. The common protocol splits them into 10 groups of 5,000, estimates the marginal in each group and reports the mean and standard deviation of the 10 scores. Scores computed with other sample sizes or splits are not comparable.

## What you should remember

- A likelihood can be fine while samples are bad, and some models have none: sample quality is measured on samples.
- $p(y) = \frac1N \sum_i p(y \mid x_i)$; $\log \mathrm{IS} = H(p(y)) - \mathbb E_x H(p(y \mid x)) = \mathbb E_x D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y))$.
- The exponent is the mutual information $I(X; Y)$; the score is classes covered divided by classes per image.
- $1 \le \mathrm{IS} \le n$; 1 means every image gets the same class distribution.
- IS never sees real data: one perfect image per class, or copied training images, score as well as a good generator.
