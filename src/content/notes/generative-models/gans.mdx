The [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) trains its generator through a bound on the likelihood and pays for it with blurry samples. A generative adversarial network (GAN) avoids likelihoods altogether ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#references)). The generator is a network that turns noise into samples, and it is trained only through a second network, the discriminator, which tries to tell generated samples from real ones. The model is *implicit*: we can sample from it, but we cannot evaluate its density.

Two facts organize this note; the rest shows where they come from.

- **If the discriminator is optimal**, the generator's objective becomes the [Jensen–Shannon divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#jensenshannon-divergence) in disguise: $2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g) - 2\ln 2$. Training the generator then means minimizing $\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g)$.
- **If the supports of the two distributions do not intersect** and the discriminator is (near-)optimal, the generator receives no useful gradient: JS is stuck at its maximum $\ln 2$ wherever $p_g$ sits, so the objective is flat.

**Notation.** $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ is the data density, $p_z(z)$ the noise prior, $G$ the generator with parameters $\theta_g$, $p_g(x)$ the density of the generated samples $G(z)$, and $D$ the discriminator with parameters $\theta_d$; $D(x) \in (0, 1)$ is the probability that $x$ is real. Logarithms are natural.

## The setup

Sample noise $z \sim p_z(z)$, for example a standard Gaussian, and pass it through the generator. The output $G(z)$ is a generated sample, and its distribution is $p_g(x)$. The discriminator receives either a generated sample, labelled $y = 0$ (fake), or a data point $x \sim p_{\text{data}}(x)$, labelled $y = 1$ (real), and outputs the probability that its input is real.

<GanSchematic />

The generator never sees real data directly. It learns only through the discriminator's output, and the discriminator learns to separate the two streams.

## A minimax game

The two networks play a **minimax** game: the discriminator **maximizes** its success, and the generator **minimizes** the discriminator's success. Success is measured by the **value function**

$$
\min_G \max_D V(G, D) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\ln D(x)\big] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}\big[\ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big].
$$

The discriminator wants $D(x)$ close to $1$ on real data and $D(G(z))$ close to $0$ on fakes; both make $V$ larger. The generator wants $D(G(z))$ close to $1$, which makes the second term very negative.

### Where $V$ comes from: binary cross-entropy

The discriminator is a binary classifier, so start from the **binary cross-entropy** between a label $y$ and a prediction $\hat y$:

$$
H(y, \hat y) = -\big[\,y\ln\hat y + (1 - y)\ln(1 - \hat y)\,\big].
$$

- For a real sample, $y = 1$, $\hat y = D(x)$ and $H = -\ln D(x)$.
- For a fake sample, $y = 0$, $\hat y = D(G(z))$ and $H = -\ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)$.

For one real and one fake sample the total is $H = -\big[\ln D(x) + \ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big]$. Averaging over many samples turns the sums into expectations:

$$
\mathbb{E}[-H] = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\ln D(x)\big] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}\big[\ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big],
$$

which for continuous distributions reads

$$
\int p_{\text{data}}(x)\ln D(x)\,dx + \int p_z(z)\ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\,dz .
$$

This is exactly $V(G, D)$. So **$V$ is the negative binary cross-entropy** of the discriminator: maximizing $V$ over $D$ is the same as training $D$ as an ordinary classifier with a BCE loss.

## How to optimize: alternate the players

The two players take turns, each with a minibatch of size $m$ ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#references), Algorithm 1).

1. **Fix $G$.** Take $m$ real samples $x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(m)}$ and $m$ noise samples $z^{(1)},\dots,z^{(m)}$, and update $\theta_d$ by gradient **ascent** along
$$
\nabla_{\theta_d}\,\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[\ln D\big(x^{(i)}\big) + \ln\Big(1 - D\big(G(z^{(i)})\big)\Big)\Big].
$$
2. **Fix $D$.** Take $m$ fresh noise samples and update $\theta_g$ by gradient **descent** along
$$
\nabla_{\theta_g}\,\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ln\Big(1 - D\big(G(z^{(i)})\big)\Big).
$$

The generator step uses only the second term because **the first term does not depend on $G$**: $\ln D(x)$ involves real data and $\theta_d$ only, so its gradient with respect to $\theta_g$ is zero. Step 1 may be repeated $k$ times per generator step; the original paper used $k = 1$.

## Global optimality: $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$

The game is won when the generator's distribution **equals the data distribution**. The proof has two steps ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#references), Proposition 1 and Theorem 1).

### Step 1: the optimal discriminator for a fixed $G$

Rewrite the second expectation over generated samples: $x = G(z)$ has density $p_g$, so $\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}\big[\ln(1 - D(G(z)))\big] = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_g}\big[\ln(1 - D(x))\big]$. Then, for fixed $G$,

$$
V(G, D) = \int_x \Big[\,p_{\text{data}}(x)\ln D(x) + p_g(x)\ln\big(1 - D(x)\big)\Big]\,dx .
$$

$D$ can be chosen freely at every $x$, so the integrand can be maximized pointwise. Write $a = p_{\text{data}}(x)$, $b = p_g(x)$ and $y = D(x)$. The function $f(y) = a\ln y + b\ln(1 - y)$ on $(0, 1)$ has

$$
f'(y) = \frac{a}{y} - \frac{b}{1 - y} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; y^* = \frac{a}{a + b},
\qquad
f''(y) = -\frac{a}{y^2} - \frac{b}{(1 - y)^2} < 0,
$$

so $y^*$ is the unique maximum (for $a + b > 0$; where both densities vanish, $D$ does not matter).

<PointwiseMaximum />

Hence, for a fixed generator, the best discriminator is

$$
D^*_G(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} .
$$

<OptimalDiscriminator />

### Step 2: substitute $D^*$ and recognize JS

Plug $D^*_G$ back into $V$. Since $1 - D^*_G = \dfrac{p_g}{p_{\text{data}} + p_g}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
C(G) :={}& \max_D V(G, D) = V(G, D^*_G) \\
={}& \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\ln\frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} \\
&+ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_g}\ln\frac{p_g(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The Jensen–Shannon divergence compares each distribution with the mixture $\tfrac12(p_1 + p_2)$:

$$
\mathrm{JS}(p_1\,\|\,p_2) = \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_1}\ln\frac{p_1(x)}{\frac{p_1(x) + p_2(x)}{2}} + \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_2}\ln\frac{p_2(x)}{\frac{p_1(x) + p_2(x)}{2}} .
$$

To make the denominators match, divide each by $2$ inside the logarithm and pay $\ln 2$ for each expectation, because $\ln\frac{p}{p+q} = \ln\frac{p}{(p+q)/2} - \ln 2$:

$$
C(G) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\ln\frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{\frac{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)}{2}}
+ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_g}\ln\frac{p_g(x)}{\frac{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)}{2}} - 2\ln 2 ,
$$

that is,

$$
C(G) = 2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g) - 2\ln 2 .
$$

Since $\mathrm{JS} \ge 0$, with equality only when $p_{\text{data}} = p_g$, the minimum over $G$ is

$$
\min_G C(G) = -2\ln 2 \approx -1.386, \quad\text{reached only at } p_g = p_{\text{data}} ,
$$

where $D^* = \tfrac12$ everywhere. For the two densities in the optimal-discriminator figure, $\mathrm{JS} \approx 0.430$ nats and $C(G) \approx -0.526$, computed by numerical integration both from the definition of $V$ and from the JS formula.

This also explains the warning about **non-overlapping supports**. If $p_{\text{data}}$ and $p_g$ live on disjoint sets, then $D^* = 1$ on the data and $0$ on the fakes, $\mathrm{JS} = \ln 2$ and $C(G) = 0$, no matter where exactly $p_g$ sits. A small move of the generator does not change this, so the gradient that reaches the generator is zero. This is not an exotic case: natural images and generated images concentrate near low-dimensional manifolds, which almost never intersect early in training ([Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017](#references)). It is the same saturation of JS that the [fitting note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#js-does-not-cover-the-modes-either) observed in one dimension. The [WGAN note](/notes/generative-models/wgan) replaces JS with the Wasserstein-1 distance to fix it.

## Training as a picture

<TrainingEvolution />

1. **At the beginning**, $p_g$ is far from $p_{\text{data}}$ and the discriminator is arbitrary.
2. **After updating $\theta_d$**, $D$ approaches $D^*$: high over the data, low over the generated samples.
3. **After updating $\theta_g$**, the gradient of $D$ guides $G(z)$ towards regions that $D$ classifies as data, so $p_g$ moves towards $p_{\text{data}}$.
4. **Finally**, $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$ and the discriminator cannot tell them apart: $D(x) = \tfrac12$.

The picture is idealized. The theory assumes that $D$ reaches its optimum after every generator step and that both players can represent any function. In practice both are networks trained a few steps at a time, and the alternating updates can oscillate or collapse onto a few modes of the data (**mode collapse**) instead of converging.

## Which generator loss is used in practice?

The minimax loss for the generator, $\mathbb{E}_z\ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)$, is rarely minimized directly. Already the original paper recommends training the generator to **maximize** $\ln D(G(z))$ instead, that is, to minimize

$$
\mathcal L_G = -\mathbb{E}_z\ln D\big(G(z)\big),
$$

the **non-saturating** loss. In code this is the BCE of the discriminator's output on generated samples with the "real" label.

**The problem with the minimax loss.** When $D$ is good, it rejects fakes confidently: $d = D(G(z)) \approx 0$. Then $\ln(1 - d) \approx \ln 1 = 0$. A small value does not by itself mean a small gradient; what matters is the slope of the loss with respect to $d$, and further back through the network:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial d}\ln(1 - d) = -\frac{1}{1 - d} \approx -1,
\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial d}\big(-\ln d\big) = -\frac1d \to -\infty \quad (d \to 0).
$$

At $d = 0.05$ the slopes are $-1.05$ and $-20$. The discriminator ends in a sigmoid, $d = \sigma(\ell)$, and $\partial d/\partial\ell = d(1 - d)$. Per logit the gradients are therefore $-d \to 0$ for the minimax loss and $-(1 - d) \to -1$ for the non-saturating one. The minimax loss **saturates** at the logit, which is where the gradient enters the network: its gradient vanishes exactly when the generator is worst, while the non-saturating gradient is largest there.

<SaturatingLoss />

Both losses decrease as $D(G(z))$ grows, so they push the generator in the same direction, and for the optimal discriminator they share the fixed point $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$. The non-saturating version is no longer the minimax game exactly. It fixes the weak gradients early in training, but not the disjoint-support problem of the previous section: with a near-optimal discriminator its updates become unstable rather than vanishing ([Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017](#references)).

A common stabilizer is **one-sided label smoothing**: train the discriminator against the target $0.9$ instead of $1$ on real samples, so that it never becomes fully confident ([Salimans et al., 2016](#references)).

## Extensions

### Conditional GAN

A conditional GAN ([Mirza and Osindero, 2014](#references)) gives both networks a label $c$, such as a class: the generator produces $G(z, c)$ and the discriminator judges pairs $D(x, c)$. The value function becomes

$$
V_{\text{cond}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,c)\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\ln D(x,c)\big] + \mathbb{E}_{c\sim p_c,\; z\sim p_z}\big[\ln\big(1-D(G(z,c),c)\big)\big].
$$

The discriminator now also penalizes images that are realistic but do not match their label, so the generator learns to follow the condition. This is the simplest answer to the "no conditioning" drawback listed for the VAE.

### f-GAN

The original GAN minimizes JS. An f-GAN ([Nowozin, Cseke and Tomioka, 2016](#references)) generalizes the game to any **f-divergence**

$$
D_f(P\,\|\,Q) = \int q(x)\, f\!\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) dx ,
$$

for a convex $f$ with $f(1) = 0$; $f(u) = u\ln u$ gives the KL divergence. The key tool is the **convex conjugate** $f^*(t) = \sup_u \{ut - f(u)\}$. Rearranged, its definition says $f(u) \ge tu - f^*(t)$ for every $t$: each line with slope $t$ and intercept $-f^*(t)$ lies below the graph of $f$, and the line with $t = f'(u)$ touches it at $u$. For example, $f(u) = \tfrac12 u^2$ gives $f^*(t) = \tfrac12 t^2$.

<FenchelConjugate />

Replacing $f$ by its best supporting line at every point gives a variational lower bound ([Nguyen, Wainwright and Jordan, 2010](#references)):

$$
D_f(P\,\|\,Q) \;\ge\; \sup_T \Big\{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\big[T(x)\big] - \mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\big[f^*(T(x))\big]\Big\}.
$$

With $P = p_{\text{data}}$, $Q = p_g$ and $T$ a neural network, the right-hand side is a two-player game: $T$ plays the discriminator's role and maximizes, and the generator minimizes. Both expectations need only samples, never densities, which is what makes the bound usable for an implicit model. One particular choice of $f$ recovers the original GAN objective, up to constants.

The [GANs notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/42dd64b535f5bedf1d2b3db28f2f21ce96c9f09f/seminars/02_GAN/GANs.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* repository (pinned commit; cell numbers count every cell from 1) implements all three models.

- **Cells 5–15: unconditional GAN on MNIST zeros**, resized to $32\times32$ and scaled to $[-1, 1]$. The generator upsamples bilinearly and convolves; the discriminator returns logits. **Cell 10** sets the hyperparameters: Adam with learning rate $10^{-4}$ for both networks, one discriminator step per generator step (`update_ratio = 1`), gradient-norm clipping at $1$ and one-sided label smoothing $0.9$. **Cell 13** is the training loop with `binary_cross_entropy_with_logits`. The discriminator loss is the mean of BCE on real images (target 0.9) and on detached fakes (target 0), which is $-V$ up to the factor $\tfrac12$ and the smoothing. The generator loss is the non-saturating BCE towards the real label. Here the smoothed target $0.9$ is also used for the generator, so the exact loss is $-\mathbb{E}_z\big[0.9\ln D(G(z)) + 0.1\ln(1 - D(G(z)))\big]$, minimized at $D(G(z)) = 0.9$ rather than $1$. **Cells 14–16** plot the losses, sample a grid and list common tricks (label smoothing and flipping, update ratios).
- **Cells 17–26: conditional GAN on CIFAR-100**. The label is embedded and concatenated as an extra channel, at the generator's latent grid and at the discriminator's input.
- **Cells 27–33: f-GAN.** `Activation_g` maps the network output into the domain of $f^*$ for each divergence (`"GAN"`, `"KLD"`, `"JSD"`, `"RKL"`, `"SQH"`, `"CHI"`); cell 31 selects JS. In cell 32 the generator maximizes $T$ on generated samples rather than minimizing the fake term of the bound, a non-saturating surrogate analogous to the one above; the direct form is left as a commented alternative.
- **Cells 34–36**: practical notes on latent size and a pros-and-cons summary: no intractable integrals or Markov chains, but no clear stopping criterion and unstable training.
