Take a network that turns Gaussian noise into $64 \times 64$ images. You can draw samples and [score](/notes/generative-models/fid) them, but not compute the probability of a training image: that is an integral over all noise vectors that map near it, with no closed form. How do you train it without maximum likelihood?

A **two-sample test** decides from two sets of samples whether they come from the same distribution. In high dimension no fixed statistic is sharp enough, so learn one: a classifier that tells real images from generated ones. Where it beats chance shows where the generator is wrong; if the generator matches the data, no classifier beats a coin. A **generative adversarial network** (GAN) trains the sampler against such a classifier ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#references)).

Likelihood and sample quality can disagree anyway. A model that puts weight $0.01$ on the true density and $0.99$ on noise loses at most $\ln 100 \approx 4.6$ nats per image: the logarithm is increasing, so $\ln(0.01\,p + 0.99\,q) \ge \ln(0.01\,p) = \ln p - \ln 100$. The log-likelihood of an image grows with its number of pixels, so in high dimension this loss is negligible, yet 99 samples in 100 are noise ([CS236, lecture 9](#references)).

## The setup

Sample noise $z$ from a simple prior $p_z$, such as a standard Gaussian, and pass it through the **generator** $G$, a network with parameters $\theta_g$. The outputs $G(z)$ have some distribution $p_g$ on image space. The **discriminator** $D$, with parameters $\theta_d$, receives either a generated sample, labelled $y = 0$, or a data point $x \sim p_{\text{data}}$, labelled $y = 1$, and outputs $D(x) \in (0, 1)$, the probability that its input is real.

<GanSchematic />

The generator never sees a training image; its only signal is the gradient of the discriminator's output. The model is **implicit**: it defines $p_g$ by a sampling procedure and never writes down a density.

## A minimax game

The discriminator wants $D(x)$ near 1 on real data and $D(G(z))$ near 0 on fakes. The generator wants the opposite on its fakes. Both goals are measured by one **value function**

$$
\begin{aligned}
V(G, D) ={}& \mathbb E_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\ln D(x)\big] \\
&+ \mathbb E_{z\sim p_z}\big[\ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big],
\end{aligned}
$$

which the discriminator maximizes and the generator minimizes: the game is $\min_G \max_D V(G, D)$.

### Where the value function comes from: binary cross-entropy

The discriminator is a binary classifier, so its natural loss is the **binary cross-entropy** between a label $y$ and a prediction $\hat y$:

$$
H(y, \hat y) = -\big[y\ln\hat y + (1 - y)\ln(1 - \hat y)\big] .
$$

A real sample has $y = 1$ and costs $-\ln D(x)$. A fake has $y = 0$ and costs $-\ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)$. In a stream with as many real samples as fakes, each kind has weight $\tfrac12$, so by linearity of expectation the mean loss is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar H &= \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_x\big[-\ln D(x)\big] \\
&\quad + \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_z\big[-\ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big] \\
&= -\tfrac12\,V(G, D) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The factor $\tfrac12$ does not move the maximizer, so maximizing $V$ over $D$ is ordinary classifier training with class prior $\tfrac12$.

## How to optimize: alternate the players

The two networks take turns, each step on a minibatch of size $m$ ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#references), Algorithm 1).

1. Fix $G$. Draw real samples $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(m)}$ and noise $z^{(1)}, \dots, z^{(m)}$, and take a gradient **ascent** step on $\theta_d$ along
$$
\nabla_{\theta_d}\,\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[\ln D\big(x^{(i)}\big) + \ln\Big(1 - D\big(G(z^{(i)})\big)\Big)\Big].
$$
2. Fix $D$. Draw fresh noise and take a gradient **descent** step on $\theta_g$ along
$$
\nabla_{\theta_g}\,\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ln\Big(1 - D\big(G(z^{(i)})\big)\Big).
$$

The generator's step drops the first term because $\ln D(x)$ on real data does not depend on $\theta_g$. Step 1 can be repeated $k$ times per generator step; the original paper used $k = 1$ (see the [balance section](#balancing-the-two-players)).

## Global optimality: the generator matches the data

Why should this game end with $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$? The argument has two steps. It assumes that both distributions have densities on the same space and that $D$ can be any function ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](#references), Section 4).

### Step 1: the optimal discriminator for a fixed generator

The generated samples $x = G(z)$ have density $p_g$, so the second expectation can be taken over $x \sim p_g$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V(G, D) = \int \Big[&p_{\text{data}}(x)\ln D(x) \\
&+ p_g(x)\ln\big(1 - D(x)\big)\Big]\,dx .
\end{aligned}
$$

$D$ can take any value at every $x$, so we maximize the integrand pointwise. Write $a = p_{\text{data}}(x)$, $b = p_g(x)$ and $s = D(x)$. For $a, b > 0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(s) &= a\ln s + b\ln(1 - s), \\
f'(s) &= \frac{a}{s} - \frac{b}{1 - s} = 0 , \\
s^* &= \frac{a}{a + b} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second derivative, $-a/s^2 - b/(1 - s)^2$, is negative, so $s^*$ is the unique maximum. Where only one density is positive, $f$ is monotone and the same formula gives the endpoint, $s^* = 1$ or $0$; where both vanish, $D$ does not matter.

<PointwiseMaximum />

For a fixed generator the best discriminator is therefore

$$
D^*_G(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} .
$$

It is the posterior probability of "real" when real and fake samples are equally likely a priori.

<OptimalDiscriminator />

### Step 2: substitute the optimal discriminator and recognize JS

Put $D^*_G$ back into $V$, using $1 - D^*_G = p_g / (p_{\text{data}} + p_g)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C(G) &= \max_D V(G, D) \\
&= \mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}}\ln\frac{p_{\text{data}}}{p_{\text{data}} + p_g} \\
&\quad + \mathbb E_{p_g}\ln\frac{p_g}{p_{\text{data}} + p_g} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The [Jensen–Shannon divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#jensenshannon-divergence) compares each distribution with the mixture $m = \tfrac12(p_{\text{data}} + p_g)$. To bring it in, write each denominator as $2m$ and pull out the 2, which costs $\ln 2$ per expectation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C(G) &= \mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}}\ln\frac{p_{\text{data}}}{m} + \mathbb E_{p_g}\ln\frac{p_g}{m} - 2\ln 2 \\
&= D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, m) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_g \,\|\, m) - 2\ln 2 \\
&= 2\,D_{\mathrm{JS}}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_g) - 2\ln 2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line is the definition of KL, and the third the definition of JS, $D_{\mathrm{JS}}(p \,\|\, q) = \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, m) + \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, m)$, multiplied by 2.

Against an optimal discriminator, the generator minimizes the Jensen–Shannon divergence. $D_{\mathrm{JS}} \ge 0$, with equality only when the two densities coincide, so the unique minimum is

$$
\min_G C(G) = -2\ln 2 \approx -1.386 \quad \text{at } p_g = p_{\text{data}},
$$

where $D^* = \tfrac12$ everywhere. For the two densities of the figure, $D_{\mathrm{JS}} \approx 0.430$ nats and $C(G) \approx -0.526$.

### Where it breaks: supports that do not overlap

Let $p_{\text{data}}$ and $p_g$ live on disjoint sets. Then $D^* = 1$ on the data and $0$ on the fakes, $D_{\mathrm{JS}} = \ln 2$ and $C(G) = 0$, wherever $p_g$ sits. Moving the generator closer to the data, without overlap, does not change $C$ at all.

The generator's gradient is $\nabla_x D$ at its samples, sent back through $G$. Disjoint compact supports lie a distance $\delta > 0$ apart. A smooth function equal to 1 within $\delta/3$ of the data and 0 within $\delta/3$ of the fakes makes both logarithms in $V$ zero, their maximum, so it is an optimal discriminator; it is constant around every fake, so its gradient there is zero, and so is the generator's ([Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017](#references), Theorem 2.1; Theorem 2.2 extends it to manifolds that do not align).

This is the usual case. A generator built from affine maps and ReLU-type or smooth increasing activations maps its noise into a countable union of manifolds of dimension at most $\dim z$, which has zero volume when $\dim z$ is below the number of pixels ([Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017](#references), Lemma 1). Natural images are believed to concentrate near a low-dimensional set too. After an arbitrarily small random shift, two such sets meet, if at all, in a lower-dimensional set that carries no mass (Lemmas 2 and 3), as two curves in three-dimensional space miss each other. The [fitting note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#js-does-not-cover-the-modes-either) saw the same saturation of JS in one dimension; the [Wasserstein GAN](/notes/generative-models/wgan) replaces JS with a distance that keeps a slope.

## Which generator loss is used in practice?

The minimax generator loss $\mathbb E_z\ln\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)$ is rarely used. Already the original paper trains the generator to maximize $\ln D(G(z))$ instead, that is, to minimize the **non-saturating** loss

$$
\mathcal L_G = -\mathbb E_z\ln D\big(G(z)\big) .
$$

Why change it? Early in training $D$ rejects fakes confidently, $d = D(G(z)) \approx 0$. Compare the slopes of the two losses in $d$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial d}\ln(1 - d) &= -\frac{1}{1 - d} \approx -1, \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial d}\big(-\ln d\big) &= -\frac1d \to -\infty .
\end{aligned}
$$

At $d = 0.05$ the slopes are $-1.05$ and $-20$. Through the logit $\ell$, with $d = \sigma(\ell)$ and $\partial d/\partial\ell = d(1 - d)$, the minimax loss has slope $-d$, which vanishes as $D$ grows confident, and the non-saturating loss $-(1 - d)$, largest there: the minimax loss gives the weakest signal to the worst generator.

<SaturatingLoss />

Both losses share the fixed point $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$, but the non-saturating loss is no longer the minimax game. With $D = D^*$ computed for the current generator and held fixed, its expected gradient is the gradient of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_g \,\|\, p_{\text{data}}) - 2\,D_{\mathrm{JS}}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_g)$ ([Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017](#references), Theorem 2.5), derived below. The first term, a reverse KL, punishes fakes where the data have little mass and barely punishes missed data modes, one reason for dropped modes.

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

Let $p_\theta$ be the generator's density, $\theta_0$ the current parameters and $D^* = p_{\text{data}}/(p_{\text{data}} + p_{\theta_0})$, frozen. Assume $p_\theta$ is differentiable in $\theta$, that $\nabla_\theta$ and $\int$ can be swapped, and that $p_{\text{data}} > 0$ wherever $p_\theta > 0$. Two facts do the work. For a fixed function $h$, $\mathbb E_z h(G_\theta(z)) = \int p_\theta h$, so its gradient is $\int \nabla p_\theta\, h$. And $\int \nabla p_\theta = \nabla 1 = 0$, so constants added to $h$ drop out.

Each divergence is $\int \phi(p_\theta(x))\,dx$ for a function $\phi$ of the value $p = p_\theta(x)$, so its gradient is $\int \phi'(p_\theta)\,\nabla p_\theta\,dx$ by the chain rule. With $a = p_{\text{data}}(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi_{\mathrm{KL}}(p) &= p\ln\frac{p}{a}, \\
\phi_{\mathrm{KL}}'(p) &= \ln\frac{p}{a} + 1, \\
\phi_{\mathrm{JS}}(p) &= a\ln\frac{2a}{a + p} + p\ln\frac{2p}{a + p}, \\
\phi_{\mathrm{JS}}'(p) &= -\frac{a}{a + p} + \ln\frac{2p}{a + p} + 1 - \frac{p}{a + p} \\
&= \ln 2 + \ln\frac{p}{a + p} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\phi_{\mathrm{JS}}$ is the integrand of $2D_{\mathrm{JS}}$. At $\theta_0$, $p/a = (1 - D^*)/D^*$ and $p/(a + p) = 1 - D^*$. Dropping the constants 1 and $\ln 2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla D_{\mathrm{KL}} &= \int \nabla p_\theta\,\big[\ln(1 - D^*) - \ln D^*\big] , \\
\nabla\, 2D_{\mathrm{JS}} &= \int \nabla p_\theta\,\ln(1 - D^*) , \\
\nabla\big(D_{\mathrm{KL}} - 2D_{\mathrm{JS}}\big) &= \int \nabla p_\theta\,\big(-\ln D^*\big) \\
&= \nabla_\theta\,\mathbb E_z\big[-\ln D^*(G_\theta(z))\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line is the first fact read backwards. The second line also shows that the minimax loss $\mathbb E_z\ln(1 - D^*(G_\theta(z)))$ has the gradient of $2D_{\mathrm{JS}}$, as Step 2 says.

</details>

The non-saturating loss also leaves the disjoint-support problem in place. Near a perfect discriminator $D(G(z)) \approx 0$, and the gradient $-\nabla_x D / D$ divides one small error by another. If the errors of $D$ and of $\nabla_x D$ are independent zero-mean Gaussian noise, the ratio has a Cauchy distribution, which has no mean and no variance ([Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017](#references), Theorem 2.6). The updates become noisy instead of vanishing.

### Computing the losses from logits

Both logarithms are functions of the logit that never need $\sigma(\ell)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln D &= \ln\sigma(\ell) = -\operatorname{softplus}(-\ell), \\
\ln(1 - D) &= \ln\sigma(-\ell) = -\operatorname{softplus}(\ell),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\operatorname{softplus}(t) = \ln(1 + e^{t})$ is evaluated stably as $\max(t, 0) + \ln(1 + e^{-\lvert t\rvert})$, the two-term [log-sum-exp](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability#log-sum-exp-and-the-stable-softmax). The discriminator then minimizes $\mathbb E_x\operatorname{softplus}(-\ell(x)) + \mathbb E_z\operatorname{softplus}(\ell(G(z)))$ and the generator $\mathbb E_z\operatorname{softplus}(-\ell(G(z)))$.

Computing $\sigma$ first fails where the discriminator is confident: in [32-bit floating point](/notes/acos/floating-point#rounding-ulp-and-machine-epsilon) $\sigma(\ell)$ rounds to 1 once $\ell$ exceeds about 17, so $\ln(1 - \sigma(\ell)) = -\infty$ where softplus gives about $-17$. PyTorch provides the stable form as `binary_cross_entropy_with_logits`.

## Training GANs in practice

Real training depends on empirical rules; none is a theorem, so each comes with the reason it helps.

### Architecture: the DCGAN guidelines

The first convolutional GANs that trained reliably on images followed five rules ([Radford, Metz and Chintala, 2016](#references)):

1. strided convolutions instead of pooling, transposed convolutions to upsample;
2. batch normalization in both networks, except at the generator's output and the discriminator's input;
3. no fully connected hidden layers: the noise is reshaped into a $4 \times 4$ map with 1024 channels;
4. ReLU in the generator, Tanh at its output, images scaled to $[-1, 1]$;
5. LeakyReLU with slope $0.2$ in the discriminator.

Training used Adam with learning rate $2\cdot10^{-4}$ and $\beta_1 = 0.5$ (the usual $0.9$ oscillated), minibatches of 128 and weights from $\mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)$.

The rules keep the gradient signal dense: max-pooling passes gradient to one input per window and ReLU none for negative inputs, while strided convolutions and LeakyReLU pass some everywhere. [Batch normalization](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#batch-normalization-in-training-and-in-evaluation) let deep generators start learning without collapsing all samples to one point. Later models upsample by resizing and a convolution, which avoids [checkerboard artifacts](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders#transposed-convolution-or-upsample--conv).

Sampling $z \sim \mathcal N(0, 0.7^2 I)$ instead of $\mathcal N(0, I)$ trades [recall for precision](/notes/generative-models/precision-recall#where-the-trade-off-appears); an FID of such samples must report the scale. [BigGAN's truncation trick](/notes/generative-models/gan-scaling#the-truncation-trick) is the principled version.

### Balancing the two players

The discriminator is the generator's only source of gradient, so the two must improve at comparable speeds, tuned by $k$, the learning rates and the capacities. A discriminator that rejects every fake leaves the generator vanishing or noisy gradients: weaken it. One that lags points the generator in poor directions: update it more often. The [WGAN](/notes/generative-models/wgan) makes a strong critic help instead.

The losses say little about sample quality: track samples from fixed noise vectors and an [FID](/notes/generative-models/fid), where a collapse shows first. [Gradient-norm clipping](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#gradient-clipping) guards against explosive updates.

### Label smoothing and label flipping

**One-sided label smoothing** trains the discriminator toward $0.9$ instead of 1 on real samples ([Salimans et al., 2016](#references)). Why only the real side? With targets $\alpha$ for real and $\beta$ for fake samples, the pointwise integrand of [Step 1](#step-1-the-optimal-discriminator-for-a-fixed-generator) becomes $A\ln s + B\ln(1 - s)$ with $A = \alpha p_{\text{data}} + \beta p_g$ and $B = (1 - \alpha)p_{\text{data}} + (1 - \beta)p_g$, maximized at $A/(A + B)$:

$$
D^*(x) = \frac{\alpha\,p_{\text{data}}(x) + \beta\,p_g(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} .
$$

Look at a region where the generator puts much more mass than the data, with $r = p_{\text{data}}/p_g$ small. To first order in $r$, $D^* \approx \beta + (\alpha - \beta)r$, and the non-saturating loss is

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\ln D^* &\approx -\ln\alpha - \ln r && \beta = 0, \\
-\ln D^* &\approx -\ln\beta - \frac{\alpha - \beta}{\beta}\,r && \beta > 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

With $\beta = 0$ the loss follows the log density ratio, and its gradient does not fade as $r$ shrinks. With $\beta > 0$ the gradient carries the factor $r$, so fakes far from the data barely move. Hence only the real label is smoothed.

**Random label flipping** of a fraction $\varepsilon$ of the labels is, in expectation, two-sided smoothing with $\alpha = 1 - \varepsilon$, $\beta = \varepsilon$ (cross-entropy is linear in the target), so it is used sparingly.

### Mode collapse and how to fight it

In **mode collapse** the generator maps most of its noise to a few outputs the current discriminator accepts. The discriminator judges each sample on its own, so nothing tells the outputs to differ ([Salimans et al., 2016](#references)); when it rejects the collapsed mode, the generator moves to another. The reverse-KL term makes dropping a mode cheap.

Take data from eight Gaussians of standard deviation $0.02$ on a circle. A standard GAN puts almost all its mass on one mode at a time and rotates from mode to mode, never covering all eight ([Metz et al., 2017](#references)). Nearly every sample lies on a real mode while one mode in eight is covered: [precision](/notes/generative-models/precision-recall) near 1, recall near $1/8$ (toy values). FID penalizes the missing modes; each sample alone looks perfect.

**Minibatch discrimination** lets the discriminator compare samples within a batch ([Salimans et al., 2016](#references)). A learned tensor maps a feature vector $f(x_i)$ to a matrix $M_i$ with rows $M_{i,b}$, giving the features

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_b(x_i, x_j) &= \exp\big(-\lVert M_{i,b} - M_{j,b}\rVert_1\big), \\
o(x_i)_b &= \sum_{j} c_b(x_i, x_j), \quad b = 1, \dots, B .
\end{aligned}
$$

The vector $o(x_i)$ is appended to $f(x_i)$; a collapsed batch has large $o$, which the discriminator learns to reject.

**Unrolled GANs** let the generator anticipate the discriminator's reply ([Metz et al., 2017](#references)). Starting from the current $\theta_D$, take $K$ ascent steps of the discriminator and score the generator against the result:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta_D^{0} &= \theta_D, \\
\theta_D^{k+1} &= \theta_D^{k} + \eta\,\nabla_{\theta_D} V\big(\theta_G, \theta_D^{k}\big), \\
V_K(\theta_G, \theta_D) &= V\big(\theta_G, \theta_D^{K}(\theta_G, \theta_D)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The generator descends $V_K$ through the $K$ steps; the discriminator is updated with $V$. By the chain rule,

$$
\frac{dV_K}{d\theta_G} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial \theta_G} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial \theta_D^{K}}\,\frac{d\theta_D^{K}}{d\theta_G} .
$$

The first term alone favours putting all mass on the point $D$ likes most; the second sees that the discriminator would reject such a collapse. $K = 0$ is the standard GAN; with $K = 10$ the eight-Gaussian generator covers all modes. A buffer of earlier fakes, cheaper, keeps an abandoned mode punished.

## Conditional GAN

A **conditional GAN** ([Mirza and Osindero, 2014](#references)) gives both networks a label $c$, such as a class. The generator produces $G(z, c)$ and the discriminator judges pairs $D(x, c)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\text{cond}} ={}& \mathbb E_{(x, c)\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\ln D(x, c)\big] \\
&+ \mathbb E_{c \sim p_c,\, z \sim p_z}\big[\ln\big(1 - D(G(z, c), c)\big)\big],
\end{aligned}
$$

where $p_c$ is the label distribution of the data. Generated pairs have the joint $p_c(c)\,p_g(x \mid c)$, and the optimality argument applied to pairs gives $p_g(x, c) = p_{\text{data}}(x, c)$: a realistic image with the wrong label can be rejected, so the generator follows the label, as in the [conditional VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae#conditioning-on-a-label-the-conditional-vae).

## f-GAN

*Optional depth: the game for any f-divergence. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

The GAN minimizes JS. An **f-GAN** ([Nowozin, Cseke and Tomioka, 2016](#references)) plays the same game for any **f-divergence**

$$
D_f(P \,\|\, Q) = \int q(x)\, f\!\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) dx ,
$$

with $f$ convex, lower semicontinuous and $f(1) = 0$; $f(u) = u\ln u$ gives $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \,\|\, Q)$. The tool is the **convex conjugate** $f^*(t) = \sup_u \{ut - f(u)\}$. By definition $f(u) \ge tu - f^*(t)$ for every $t$: each line of slope $t$ and intercept $-f^*(t)$ lies below $f$. For a convex, lower semicontinuous $f$ these lines recover it, $f(u) = \sup_t\{tu - f^*(t)\}$, the **Fenchel–Moreau theorem** ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), Section 3.3.2 and Exercise 3.39). For the differentiable $f$ of the table below, two lines prove it. The inequality gives $\sup_t\{tu - f^*(t)\} \le f(u)$. The [tangent](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-first-order-condition-the-tangent-lies-below) at $u$, of slope $t_u = f'(u)$, lies below $f$ and touches it at $u$, so the supremum defining $f^*(t_u)$ is attained at $u$: $f^*(t_u) = t_u u - f(u)$, and the slope $t_u$ reaches $f(u)$. For example, $f(u) = \tfrac12(u - 1)^2$ has its supremum at $u = 1 + t$, so $f^*(t) = t + \tfrac12 t^2$.

<FenchelConjugate />

Substitute this at every point, with $u = p(x)/q(x)$ and a slope $T(x)$ that may depend on $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_f(P \,\|\, Q) &= \int q(x) \sup_t \big\{t\,u(x) - f^*(t)\big\}\,dx \\
&= \sup_T \int \big[p(x)\,T(x) - q(x)\,f^*(T(x))\big]\,dx \\
&\ge \sup_{T \in \mathcal T} \Big\{\mathbb E_{P}\big[T(x)\big] - \mathbb E_{Q}\big[f^*(T(x))\big]\Big\} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line chooses the slope at each point; nothing is lost, because $T(x) = f'(u(x))$ attains every pointwise supremum at once ([Nguyen, Wainwright and Jordan, 2010](#references)). The inequality comes from restricting $T$ to a network family $\mathcal T$. With $P = p_{\text{data}}$ and $Q = p_g$, the last line is a two-player game that needs only samples.

A fixed increasing **output activation** $g_f$ maps the network's unconstrained output $v(x)$ into the domain of $f^*$, $T(x) = g_f(v(x))$. The usual choices, with $P$ the data:

| Divergence | $f(u)$ | $g_f(v)$ | $f^*(t)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)$ | $u\ln u$ | $v$ | $e^{t-1}$ |
| $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\Vert P)$ | $-\ln u$ | $-e^{-v}$ | $-1-\ln(-t)$, $t<0$ |
| Pearson $\chi^2$ | $(u-1)^2$ | $v$ | $t + t^2/4$ |
| squared Hellinger | $(\sqrt u - 1)^2$ | $1 - e^{-v}$ | $t/(1-t)$, $t<1$ |
| $2D_{\mathrm{JS}}$ | $u\ln u - (u+1)\ln\frac{u+1}{2}$ | $\ln 2 - \ln(1 + e^{-v})$ | $-\ln(2 - e^{t})$, $t<\ln 2$ |
| GAN | $u\ln u - (u+1)\ln(u+1)$ | $-\ln(1 + e^{-v})$ | $-\ln(1 - e^{t})$, $t<0$ |

The GAN row has $f(1) = -\ln 4$, so its divergence is $2D_{\mathrm{JS}} - \ln 4$; with $T = \ln D$ the bound is the value function $V(G, D)$. The fifth row differs from it by a constant and trains the same way.

Evaluate the composition $f^*(g_f(v))$ in one piece, as with softplus above. Row by row it is $e^{v-1}$, $v - 1$, $v + v^2/4$, $e^{v} - 1$, $\operatorname{softplus}(v) - \ln 2$ and $\operatorname{softplus}(v)$.

## Least-squares GAN

*Optional depth: a squared-error discriminator and the divergence it minimizes. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

The **least-squares GAN** (LSGAN) also repairs the gradient for fakes the discriminator already accepts that are still far from the data ([Mao et al., 2017](#references)). Its discriminator regresses toward $b$ on real samples and $a$ on fakes, and the generator asks for the score $c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_D\;& \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}}\big[(D(x) - b)^2\big] + \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{z}\big[(D(G(z)) - a)^2\big], \\
\min_G\;& \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{z}\big[(D(G(z)) - c)^2\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The common coding is $a = 0$, $b = c = 1$.

With a sigmoid cross-entropy, a fake far on the real side of the boundary has almost no gradient: the slope of $-\ln\sigma(\ell)$ is $-(1 - \sigma(\ell)) \to 0$. The squared loss is flat only at $D = c$, so a fake scored beyond $c$ is pulled back toward the level set $D = c$, which helps if that set runs through the data.

<LeastSquaresPull />

**The optimal discriminator.** For a fixed generator the integrand $\tfrac12 p_{\text{data}}(D - b)^2 + \tfrac12 p_g(D - a)^2$ is a parabola in $D$, minimized at

$$
D^*(x) = \frac{b\,p_{\text{data}}(x) + a\,p_g(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} .
$$

Add $\tfrac12\mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}}[(D(x) - c)^2]$ to the generator's loss; it does not depend on $G$. At $D = D^*$ the extended loss is

$$
\begin{aligned}
2C(G) &= \int (p_{\text{data}} + p_g)(D^* - c)^2\,dx \\
&= \int \frac{\big((b - c)p_{\text{data}} + (a - c)p_g\big)^2}{p_{\text{data}} + p_g}\,dx .
\end{aligned}
$$

Choose $b - c = 1$ and $b - a = 2$, for example $a = -1$, $b = 1$, $c = 0$. The numerator becomes $(p_{\text{data}} - p_g)^2 = 4(m - p_g)^2$ and the denominator $2m$, with $m = \tfrac12(p_{\text{data}} + p_g)$:

$$
C(G) = \int \frac{\big(p_g(x) - m(x)\big)^2}{m(x)}\,dx = \chi^2_{\text{Pearson}}(p_g \,\|\, m) .
$$

Like the [JS identity](#step-2-substitute-the-optimal-discriminator-and-recognize-js), it compares the generator with the mixture, here through $\chi^2$. The 0–1 coding misses these conditions, yet its $2C(G) = \int \varphi(p_g(x))\,dx$, with $\varphi(q) = q^2/(p_{\text{data}}(x) + q)$, is still minimized at $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$ among densities. The constraint $\int p_g = 1$ is needed: without it $p_g = 0$ would give 0. At each $x$, $\varphi''(q) = 2p_{\text{data}}^2/(p_{\text{data}} + q)^3 > 0$, so $\varphi$ is convex, and $\varphi'(p_{\text{data}}) = \tfrac34$ at every $x$. The tangent bound then gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int \varphi(p_g)\,dx &\ge \int \Big[\varphi(p_{\text{data}}) + \tfrac34\,(p_g - p_{\text{data}})\Big]dx \\
&= \int \varphi(p_{\text{data}})\,dx ,
\end{aligned}
$$

because both densities integrate to 1.

## What the game gives up

A GAN samples with one forward pass, and nothing in its loss averages plausible images into a blur. The price is what the game never computes: without a density, models are compared on samples, with [FID](/notes/generative-models/fid) and [precision and recall](/notes/generative-models/precision-recall); without an encoder, the code of an image needs an optimization over $z$ or a second network, as in the [autoencoding GANs](/notes/generative-models/alpha-gan).

For images, realism often counts for more than variety. For molecules in drug discovery, variety is the point, and a likelihood-trained model such as the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae), whose loss charges every underweighted mode, is the safer choice ([BMML lecture notes, 2026](#references)), though not a guarantee, as the next section shows.

### Strengths and weaknesses

| | VAE | GAN |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Objective | maximize the [ELBO](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from), a lower bound on $\ln p_\theta(x)$ | a minimax game; against $D^*$, $2D_{\mathrm{JS}} - 2\ln 2$ |
| Encoder | $q_\phi(z \mid x)$, trained with the decoder | none; inversion needs an optimization or a second network |
| Density | a lower bound on $\ln p_\theta(x)$ for any image | none, only samples |
| Typical failures | blurry samples, [posterior collapse](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-it-breaks-posterior-collapse) | mode collapse, unstable training |
| Sampling cost | one decoder pass: $z \sim p(z)$, then $x \sim p_\theta(x \mid z)$ | one generator pass: $z \sim p_z$, $x = G(z)$ |

The failures row is typical behaviour, not theorems. Blur comes from a fixed-variance Gaussian decoder: its squared error is minimized by the average of the images compatible with $z$. Mode collapse is a failure of the optimization: at the global optimum of the game, $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$ covers every mode.

Nor does a VAE guarantee coverage: its forward KL charges an underweighted mode only in proportion to the mode's mass. Let the data put mass $0.05$ on one mode and $0.95$ on the rest, on disjoint regions, and the model fit each region exactly but give the rare mode $0.005$. The density ratio is constant on each region, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}} &= 0.05\ln\frac{0.05}{0.005} + 0.95\ln\frac{0.95}{0.995} \\
&\approx 0.115 - 0.044 = 0.071 \text{ nats}
\end{aligned}
$$

per sample, invisible next to a log-likelihood of thousands of nats. The ELBO is also only a bound, so training maximizes the bound, not the likelihood.

## What you should remember

- Against $D^* = p_{\text{data}}/(p_{\text{data}} + p_g)$ the game value is $2D_{\mathrm{JS}} - 2\ln 2$, minimized only at $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$.
- Disjoint supports: $D_{\mathrm{JS}} = \ln 2$ and no gradient.
- Train $G$ with $-\ln D(G(z))$, from logits via softplus.
- Smooth only the real label; mode collapse is high precision, low recall.
- f-GAN: any f-divergence via $f^*$; LSGAN: Pearson $\chi^2$ to the mixture.
