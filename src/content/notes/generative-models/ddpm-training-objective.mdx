The [forward process](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process) gave us a diffusion process: a fixed chain that turns an image into Gaussian noise. To generate images we have to **invert** it, and that is the job of a deep neural network. This note derives what the network is trained to do. The route is the same as for the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae): the likelihood is intractable, so we minimise an upper bound on the negative log-likelihood instead. The bound then simplifies, step by step, into a plain mean squared error on noise.

**Notation.** The forward step adds noise with variance $\beta_t \in (0, 1)$ at step $t = 1, \dots, T$. We write

$$
\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t, \qquad \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s, \qquad \bar\alpha_0 = 1,
$$

so the forward step and its closed-form shortcut from the previous note are

$$
\begin{gathered}
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\; \beta_t I\big), \\
q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\; (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

The second argument of $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is always the **variance**. $q$ denotes the fixed forward process and $q(x_0)$ the data distribution where it starts, $p_\theta$ the learned reverse process, $\theta$ the parameters of the neural network, and $p(x_T) = \mathcal N(0, I)$ the noise distribution where generation starts.

## The reverse process

The image $x_0$ sits at one end of the chain and pure noise $x_T$ at the other. Going forward, the step $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ is known. Going backward we need $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$: the probability density that we have to learn in order to invert the process.

<InverseProcessChain />

What should $\theta$ optimise? The usual answer for a generative model: make real images likely. We minimise the **negative log-likelihood**, averaged over the data,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim q(x_0)}\big[-\log p_\theta(x_0)\big] \;\to\; \min_\theta ,
$$

which is the same as maximising the likelihood, the density the model assigns to real images. The derivation below works with one image $x_0$; the average over images is added back at the end.

## Complete forward and reverse processes

The **complete forward process** is the joint distribution of all noisy versions of an image. By the chain rule of probability,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& q(x_1, \dots, x_T \mid x_0) \\
&\quad = q(x_1 \mid x_0)\, q(x_2 \mid x_1, x_0) \\
&\qquad \cdots q(x_T \mid x_{T-1}, \dots, x_0).
\end{aligned}
$$

The forward process is a **Markov chain**: each next state depends only on the previous one, so every factor keeps only its last condition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& q(x_1, \dots, x_T \mid x_0) \\
&\quad = q(x_1 \mid x_0)\, q(x_2 \mid x_1) \cdots q(x_T \mid x_{T-1}).
\end{aligned}
$$

With the shorthand $x_{s:T} = (x_s, \dots, x_T)$, so that $x_{1:T}$ are the noisy states and $x_{0:T}$ the whole chain, the complete forward and reverse processes are

$$
\begin{gathered}
\underbrace{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})}_{\text{complete forward process}} , \\[4pt]
\underbrace{p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}_{\text{complete reverse process}} .
\end{gathered}
$$

The reverse process starts from $p(x_T) = \mathcal N(0, I)$. This factor has no parameters and needs no knowledge about the data: it is the fixed standard normal, the same for every dataset. All learning sits in the reverse steps $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$.

## The exact log-likelihood is intractable

To get the exact log-likelihood of an image, take the joint reverse distribution and integrate out all intermediate states. This is **marginalisation**, extracting a marginal from the joint distribution:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x_0) = -\log \int p_\theta(x_{0:T})\, dx_{1:T} .
$$

Can we use it? In DDPM each reverse step is a Gaussian whose mean is computed from $x_t$ by a neural network (made precise below). The network is nonlinear in $x_t$, so, unlike the forward chain of linear Gaussian steps, the composition of reverse steps is not Gaussian and the integral has no closed form. This is the same obstacle as for the [VAE decoder](/notes/generative-models/vae#the-latent-variable-model-and-its-posterior). What remains is numerical integration, so look at what the integral means. Every value of $x_{1:T}$ is one **path** from a noise sample $x_T$ to the image $x_0$, and the integral adds up the probabilities of all such paths. For images, each $x_t$ has as many dimensions as there are pixels, and there are $T$ of them (hundreds or thousands of steps), so the integral runs over $T \times (\text{number of pixels})$ dimensions. We cannot really compute all of those paths.

## An upper bound via Jensen's inequality

The trick is the one from the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from). Multiply and divide by the complete forward process $q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)$, which we know and can sample from (it is a product of Gaussians, positive everywhere, so the division is safe):

$$
\begin{aligned}
& -\log p_\theta(x_0) \\
&\quad = -\log \int q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\, \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\, dx_{1:T} \\
&\quad = -\log \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\!\left[ \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The logarithm is concave, so Jensen's inequality, $\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log Y]$, moves it inside the expectation and turns the equality into a bound:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\log p_\theta(x_0) &\le -\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\!\left[ \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The right-hand side is an **upper bound on the negative log-likelihood**. It is the negative **ELBO**: equivalently, the ELBO is a lower bound on the log-likelihood. The bound only involves quantities we can sample and evaluate. Minimising it caps the negative log-likelihood from above, but the two are not the same. The gap is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\big)$, exactly as in the VAE ([how loose is the bound](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from)), and it also depends on $\theta$. Lowering the bound therefore lowers the negative log-likelihood unless the gap grows.

## Splitting the bound into KL terms

The algebra is in Appendix A of the DDPM paper ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 5) and is sketched in the callout below. The bound becomes a sum of three kinds of terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[\, \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_T \mid x_0) \,\|\, p(x_T)\big)}_{\text{does not depend on }\theta} \\
& + \sum_{t > 1} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\big) \\
& \underbrace{-\, \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)}_{\text{reconstruction}}
\,\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\mathbb{E}_q$ averages over the noisy states $x_{1:T} \sim q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)$ of one image. Each term needs only some of them: the $t$-th KL depends only on $x_t$, so its average is over $x_t \sim q(x_t \mid x_0)$, and the reconstruction term depends only on $x_1$. In training we also average over images $x_0 \sim q(x_0)$.

<aside class="callout">

<strong>Where the KL terms come from.</strong> Write the ratio inside the logarithm with the two products and peel off the $t = 1$ factor $p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)/q(x_1 \mid x_0)$. For $t \ge 2$, because the chain is Markov, $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0)$, and Bayes' theorem turns each forward step into a <em>backward</em> one: $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0) = q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\, q(x_t \mid x_0) / q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$. The ratios $q(x_t \mid x_0)/q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$ telescope to $q(x_T \mid x_0)/q(x_1 \mid x_0)$. The $q(x_1 \mid x_0)$ left over cancels the one in the peeled-off factor, and the bound becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[ & -\log \frac{p(x_T)}{q(x_T \mid x_0)} \\
& - \sum_{t>1} \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)} \\
& - \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

Averaging the $t$-th log-ratio over $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ gives the KL divergence, and the outer expectation is over $x_t \sim q(x_t \mid x_0)$; the first term is a KL in the same way, averaged over $q(x_T \mid x_0)$. [Luo (2022)](#references) writes out every step.

</aside>

Read the three terms one by one.

- **First term.** $q(x_T \mid x_0)$ is the fixed forward process and $p(x_T)$ is the fixed standard normal. Nothing here depends on $\theta$, so for training it is a constant. (It is also tiny for a well-chosen schedule: DDPM's linear schedule reaches $\bar\alpha_{1000} \approx 4 \times 10^{-5}$, so $q(x_T \mid x_0) \approx \mathcal N(0, I)$. The course notebook's toy schedule stops at $\bar\alpha_{100} \approx 0.605$ and does not get there.)
- **Last term.** $-\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)$ measures how likely the model is to recreate the image from the slightly noisier $x_1$. At $x_1$ barely any noise is left, so for the derivation we set this term aside. This is a simplification: DDPM keeps the term as a discretized decoder for 8-bit pixel values ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §3.3, Eq. 13), and in its simplified loss the $t = 1$ noise-regression term approximates it (§3.4).
- **Middle terms.** These do the work. Each compares the "**true posterior**" $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ with the model's **reverse diffusion step** $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$.

Simplified, the objective is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[ \sum_{t > 1} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\Big( & \underbrace{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)}_{\text{true posterior}} \\
& \Big\|\, \underbrace{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}_{\text{reverse step}} \Big) \Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

## The true posterior

The three states and the three conditional distributions fit into one small graph. $x_{t-1}$ is noisy, $x_t$ is more noisy, and $x_0$ is the original image.

<PosteriorGraph />

The forward step $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ goes right. The model's step $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ goes left, using only $x_t$. The true posterior $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ also goes left, but it looks at both $x_t$ and the original image $x_0$.

The true posterior has a **closed-form expression** (derived below). So why do we need a neural network at all? Because the true posterior requires knowing $x_0$. At generation time we have no image; we have to produce one starting from pure noise alone. The network $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ must therefore imitate the true posterior without seeing $x_0$.

So we **train the model's reverse step to match the true posterior**:

$$
\begin{gathered}
p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal N\!\big(\mu_\theta(x_t, t),\; \sigma_\theta^2(x_t, t)\, I\big) \\
\big\updownarrow \\
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal N\!\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0),\; \tilde\beta_t I\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

Why Gaussians?

1. **Small steps keep the reverse step Gaussian.** For a diffusion with small steps, the reverse of a Gaussian Markov chain is also (approximately) Gaussian: as $\beta_t \to 0$, the reverse step $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ has the same Gaussian functional form as the forward step ([Feller, 1949](#references); used by [Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015](#references), §2.2). For finite steps $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ is *not* exactly Gaussian, because it mixes over all images $x_0$ in the data. The posterior conditioned on $x_0$, $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$, is exactly Gaussian for any $\beta_t$ when $t \ge 2$. At $t = 1$ it collapses to the point mass at $x_0$ ($\tilde\beta_1 = 0$), which is why that step is handled by the reconstruction term.
2. **Everything is just simpler.** KL divergences between Gaussians have closed forms, and sampling from a Gaussian is trivial.

**The closed form.** Let $t \ge 2$. By Bayes' theorem, $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \propto q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\, q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$. Both factors are Gaussian in $x_{t-1}$. The first has the exponent $-(x_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1})^2/(2\beta_t) = -\alpha_t\,(x_{t-1} - x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t})^2/(2\beta_t)$ (per coordinate), so as a function of $x_{t-1}$ it has mean $x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$ and precision $\alpha_t/\beta_t$. The second is $\mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0,\; 1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}\big)$. Completing the square in the product, the precisions add and the mean is the precision-weighted average of the two means:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\tilde\beta_t} &= \frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}
= \frac{\alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t}{\beta_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})} \\
&= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\beta_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}, \\
\tilde\mu_t &= \tilde\beta_t \left( \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t}\, x_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0 \right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The numerator simplifies by $\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t = \alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t + \beta_t = 1 - \bar\alpha_t$, using $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}$ and $\alpha_t + \beta_t = 1$. Inverting the precision and multiplying out the mean gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) &= \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 \\
&\quad + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_t, \\
\tilde\beta_t &= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \beta_t .
\end{aligned}
$$

The true posterior mean is a weighted mix of the image and the current noisy state.

## Matching two Gaussians: KL becomes L2

We would still have to predict both $\mu_\theta$ and $\sigma_\theta$. DDPM simplifies further: **fix $\sigma_\theta$ to a schedule $\sigma_t$**, so it is not learned, and only learn the mean. Ho et al. use $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ or $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t$ and report similar results ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §3.2); [Nichol and Dhariwal (2021)](#references), §3.1, later learn a variance that interpolates between the two.

With fixed variances, the KL divergence between the two Gaussians depends on $\theta$ only through the distance between the means. For $q = \mathcal N(\tilde\mu_t, \tilde\beta_t I)$ and $p_\theta = \mathcal N(\mu_\theta, \sigma_t^2 I)$ in $d$ dimensions, the [KL divergence between two 1-D Gaussians](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#what-the-gradients-are), summed over the $d$ coordinates, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p_\theta)
&= \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\big\|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\big\|^2 \\
&\quad + \underbrace{\frac d2\left(\frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2} - 1 - \log\frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2}\right)}_{\text{const; }0\text{ if }\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t} .
\end{aligned}
$$

So in fact the KL term is an **L2 loss**, $\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\|^2$, plus a constant. For one pair $(x_t, x_0)$ it would be zero with $\mu_\theta$ on $\tilde\mu_t$. But $\tilde\mu_t$ depends on $x_0$, which the network does not see, and many images are compatible with the same $x_t$. Since $\mu_\theta$ sees only $x_t$, the best it can do is the average of $\tilde\mu_t$ over all images $x_0$ compatible with $x_t$, because the conditional mean minimises the expected squared error:

$$
\mu_\theta^*(x_t, t) = \mathbb{E}_q\big[\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) \mid x_t\big],
$$

the average over $x_0 \sim q(x_0 \mid x_t)$. By the tower property this is also $\mathbb{E}_q[x_{t-1} \mid x_t]$, the mean of the true reverse step $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$. At the optimum the KL term is therefore not zero: what remains reflects how much $x_t$ leaves the image undetermined.

<EqualVarianceKL />

So:

$$
\mathbb{E}_q\!\left[ \sum_{t > 1} \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\, \big\| \tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 \right] \;\to\; \min_\theta .
$$

## From means to noise

The true posterior mean still contains $x_0$. Remove it with the **reparameterisation** of the forward shortcut, as in the VAE:

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I) \\
\Longrightarrow\quad
x_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\big(x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

Substitute into $\tilde\mu_t$. The key identity is $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}$, so $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$. The coefficient of $x_t$ collects to

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)} + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \\
&\quad= \frac{\beta_t + \alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}
= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}},
\end{aligned}
$$

using $\beta_t + \alpha_t = 1$. The coefficient of $\varepsilon$ is $-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}$. Hence

$$
\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon \right).
$$

The denoiser has access to $x_t$ and $t$, but not to $\varepsilon$. So give the model's mean exactly the same form and let the network predict only the missing piece, the noise ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 11):

$$
\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \right).
$$

Now both $\tilde\mu_t$ and $\mu_\theta(x_t, t)$ are expressed using only $x_t$ and a noise. In the difference $x_t$ cancels, leaving $\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta = \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\big(\varepsilon_\theta - \varepsilon\big)$, and the loss becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
L = \mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[ & \sum_{t > 1} \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\, \alpha_t\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)} \\
& \cdot \big\| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 \Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

This is Eq. 12 of [Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020)](#references), summed over $t$. Here $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ is the **network's prediction of the noise**. As for the means, it cannot recover the particular $\varepsilon$ that produced $x_t$, because it does not see $x_0$. The optimum is $\varepsilon_\theta^*(x_t, t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t]$, the average noise consistent with $x_t$; this is the same optimum as $\mu_\theta^*$, since $\tilde\mu_t$ is an affine function of $\varepsilon$ for fixed $x_t$. The denoiser is a noise predictor.

## Final simplification: a random timestep per sample

Summing over all $T - 1$ terms for every training image is too expensive: it costs $T - 1$ network evaluations per image. Instead, pick a **random timestep for each sample**. Since $\sum_{t=2}^{T} f(t) = (T - 1)\, \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{2, \dots, T\}}\big[f(t)\big]$, one uniformly drawn $t$ gives an unbiased estimate of the sum divided by $T - 1$, and a constant factor does not change the minimiser. Averaging also over images $x_0 \sim q(x_0)$ and noises $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, with $t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{2, \dots, T\}$, the final loss is

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbb{E}_{x_0,\, t,\, \varepsilon}\!\left[ \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\, \alpha_t\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\, \big\| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 \right], \\
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon .
\end{gathered}
$$

**The training step.** One training step needs no chain at all: draw an image, a timestep and a noise, jump straight to $x_t$ with the shortcut, and regress the noise.

<aside class="callout">

<strong>Ho et al. drop the weight.</strong> The bound-derived objective keeps the factor $\beta_t^2 / (2\sigma_t^2 \alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_t))$. DDPM's best sample quality (FID 3.17 on CIFAR-10; [Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Table 1) comes from the unweighted <em>simplified</em> loss (Eq. 14)

$$
L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\big[\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2\big]
$$

with $t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{1, \dots, T\}$ and $x_t$ as above, where the $t = 1$ term stands in for the reconstruction term. The course notebook trains exactly this unweighted loss.

</aside>

Dropping the weight is a deliberate reweighting, not a cosmetic change. With the linear schedule of the paper ($T = 1000$, $\beta_t$ from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$) and $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$, the weight simplifies to $\beta_t / (2\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_t))$. It is $0.273$ at $t = 2$, falls to a minimum of $0.0050$ at $t = 349$, and climbs back to $0.0102$ at $t = T$. The true bound puts about $55$ times more weight at $t = 2$ than at the minimum. $L_{\text{simple}}$ weights every step equally, which shifts effort towards the harder, noisier steps. Ho et al. found that this gives better samples at the price of a slightly worse likelihood (§3.4 and Table 1: FID 3.17 against 13.51 for the true bound, but $3.75$ against $3.70$ bits per dimension).

## Sampling: running the chain backwards

Training never runs the chain, but generation does. Start from pure noise $x_T \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and apply the learned reverse step $T$ times. Each step samples from $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_t, t), \sigma_t^2 I)$, using the noise form of the mean derived above, with fresh noise $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ for $t > 1$ and $z = 0$ for $t = 1$ ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Algorithm 2):

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{t-1} = {} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \right) \\
& + \sigma_t z .
\end{aligned}
$$

Each step removes the network's estimate of the noise, rescales, and adds a little fresh noise; the last step returns the mean without extra noise. Generation therefore costs $T$ network evaluations, one per step, which is the main practical drawback of DDPM. Later samplers such as DDIM ([Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021](#references)) reuse the same trained $\varepsilon_\theta$ with far fewer steps.

## Notebook

The [diffusion notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/42dd64b535f5bedf1d2b3db28f2f21ce96c9f09f/seminars/10_Diffusion-Models/02_DPM_Models.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* repository (pinned commit; cell numbers count every cell from 1; no saved outputs) trains two versions of the reverse process on a 2-D Swiss roll with $T = 100$ and a sigmoid schedule between $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-2}$ ($\beta_1 \approx 3.5 \times 10^{-5}$, $\beta_{100} \approx 1.0 \times 10^{-2}$). Code indices are 0-based, so code step `t = 0` is $t = 1$ in the formulas.

- **Cells 17–18** (restated in cell 37): the true posterior. `q_posterior_mean_variance` uses the coefficients $\beta_t\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/(1-\bar\alpha_t)$ and $(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})\sqrt{\alpha_t}/(1-\bar\alpha_t)$ and the variance $\tilde\beta_t$, as derived above. At code step `t = 0` the variance is $\tilde\beta_1 = 0$, the degenerate case of [the closed form](#the-true-posterior), and the "clipped" log-variance still takes $\log 0 = -\infty$. In `loss_likelihood_bound` (cell 31) this makes the KL infinite whenever a batch contains code step `t = 0`, so the loss printed in cell 33 can be `inf`; the gradients stay finite, because the infinite part does not depend on $\theta$. Using $\tilde\beta_2$ for the first entry avoids this.
- **Cells 19–33: the original diffusion model** of Sohl-Dickstein et al. A small timestep-conditioned network predicts a mean and a log-variance; `p_sample` and `p_sample_loop` run the reverse chain; `normal_kl` and `loss_likelihood_bound` (cells 28–31) train the KL bound at random timesteps. The full ELBO written in cell 27 is not the standard bound (it includes a Gaussian KL at the degenerate endpoint); the decomposition used in this note is the one restated in cell 38.
- **Cells 34–36: DDPM.** `ConditionalModelDDPM` predicts the noise, and `p_sample_ddpm` implements the sampling step above with $\sigma_t = \sqrt{\beta_t}$. It adds fresh noise at every step, including the last one, where Algorithm 2 adds none. The text of cell 34 says that $\tilde\beta_t$ is usually preferred for its better results; Ho et al. found the two choices similar (§3.2).
- **Cells 38–41**: the variational loss with a discretized Gaussian decoder at the first step, the way DDPM handles $-\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)$. It is shown for illustration and is never trained; as written, its helper expects a mean and a log-variance, while the DDPM model returns only a noise estimate.
- **Cells 42–43**: `noise_estimation_loss`, the unweighted $L_{\text{simple}}$: `(noise - predicted_noise).square().mean()`. Half of the batch's timesteps are drawn with `torch.randint(0, n_steps, ...)` and the other half are their antithetic partners `n_steps - t - 1`, a variance-reduction trick borrowed from the DDIM code. The text of cell 42 calls $L_{\text{simple}}$ a direct minimisation of the KL terms; as shown above, it reweights them.
- **Cells 44–48**: an exponential moving average of the weights (`EMA`, decay `mu=0.9` in the training loop) and the training loop with `noise_estimation_loss`. The EMA weights are updated but never loaded into the model used for sampling. The plotting cells (33 and 48) show `x_seq[10*j]`, the reverse chain after $10j$ steps (from $x_{90}$ down to the final sample), but title the panels $q(x_{100j})$: wrong scale, wrong direction, and $q$ instead of $p_\theta$.

## The course in one paragraph

The four families of the course answer the same question, how to train a generator when $p(x)$ is unknown, in four ways. [VAEs](/notes/generative-models/vae) bound the likelihood with an encoder. [GANs](/notes/generative-models/gans) avoid the likelihood and train against a discriminator. [Normalizing flows](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows) compute the likelihood exactly with invertible layers. Diffusion models bound the likelihood again, but with a fixed encoder, the noising chain, so that only the decoder is learned, and the bound collapses into a simple regression on noise. All of them are judged by the same [metrics](/notes/generative-models/inception-score), and all of them rest on the [KL divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences) and its relatives.
