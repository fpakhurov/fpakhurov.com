The [forward process](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process) gave us a diffusion process: a fixed chain that turns an image into Gaussian noise. To generate images we have to **invert** it, and that is the job of a deep neural network. This note derives what the network is trained to do. The route is the same as for the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae): the likelihood is intractable, so we minimise an upper bound on the negative log-likelihood instead. The bound then simplifies, step by step, into a plain mean squared error on noise.

**Notation.** The forward step adds noise with variance $\beta_t \in (0, 1)$ at step $t = 1, \dots, T$. We write

$$
\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t, \qquad \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s, \qquad \bar\alpha_0 = 1,
$$

so the forward step and its closed-form shortcut from the previous note are

$$
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\; \beta_t I\big),
\qquad
q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\; (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\big).
$$

The second argument of $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is always the **variance**. $q$ denotes the fixed forward process, $p_\theta$ the learned reverse process, $\theta$ the parameters of the neural network, and $p(x_T) = \mathcal N(0, I)$ the noise distribution where generation starts.

## The inverse process

The image $x_0$ sits at one end of the chain and pure noise $x_T$ at the other. Going forward, the step $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ is known. Going backward we need $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$: the probability density that we have to learn in order to invert the process.

<InverseProcessChain />

What should $\theta$ optimise? The usual answer for a generative model: make real images likely. We minimise the **negative log-likelihood**

$$
-\log p_\theta(x_0) \;\to\; \min_\theta ,
$$

which is the same as maximising the probability (likelihood) that the model produces the real image.

## Complete forward and reverse processes

The **complete forward process** is the joint distribution of all noisy versions of an image. By the chain rule of probability,

$$
q(x_1, \dots, x_T \mid x_0) = q(x_1 \mid x_0)\, q(x_2 \mid x_1, x_0) \cdots q(x_T \mid x_{T-1}, \dots, x_0).
$$

The forward process is a **Markov chain**: each next state depends only on the previous one, so every factor keeps only its last condition:

$$
q(x_1, \dots, x_T \mid x_0) = q(x_1 \mid x_0)\, q(x_2 \mid x_1) \cdots q(x_T \mid x_{T-1}).
$$

With the shorthand $x_{1:T} = (x_1, \dots, x_T)$, the complete forward and reverse processes are

$$
\underbrace{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})}_{\text{complete forward process}}
\qquad\qquad
\underbrace{p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}_{\text{complete reverse process}} .
$$

The reverse process starts from $p(x_T) = \mathcal N(0, I)$. This factor has no parameters and needs no knowledge about the data: it is the fixed standard normal, the same for every dataset. All learning sits in the reverse steps $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$.

## The exact log-likelihood is intractable

To get the exact log-likelihood of an image, take the joint reverse distribution and integrate out all intermediate states. This is **marginalisation**, extracting a marginal from the joint distribution:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x_0) = -\log \int p_\theta(x_{0:T})\, dx_{1:T} .
$$

Can we use it? Look at what the integral means. Every value of $x_{1:T}$ is one **path** from a noise sample $x_T$ to the image $x_0$, and the integral adds up the probabilities of all such paths. For images, each $x_t$ has as many dimensions as there are pixels, and there are $T$ of them (hundreds or thousands of steps). We cannot really compute all of those paths.

## An upper bound via Jensen's inequality

The trick is the one from the VAE. Multiply and divide by the complete forward process $q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)$, which we know and can sample from:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x_0) = -\log \int q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\, \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\, dx_{1:T}
= -\log \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\!\left[ \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \right].
$$

The logarithm is concave, so Jensen's inequality, $\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log Y]$, moves it inside the expectation and turns the equality into a bound:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x_0) \;\le\; -\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\!\left[ \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \right].
$$

The right-hand side is an **upper bound on the negative log-likelihood**. It is the negative **ELBO**: equivalently, the ELBO is a lower bound on the log-likelihood. Pushing the bound down pushes the negative log-likelihood down with it, and the bound only involves quantities we can sample and evaluate.

## Splitting the bound into KL terms

The algebra is in Appendix A of the DDPM paper ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references)) and is sketched in the callout below. The result is the somewhat scary expression

$$
\mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[
\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_T \mid x_0) \,\|\, p(x_T)\big)}_{\text{does not depend on }\theta}
+ \sum_{t > 1} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\big)
\underbrace{-\, \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)}_{\text{reconstruction}}
\Bigg].
$$

<aside class="callout">

<strong>Where the KL terms come from.</strong> Write the ratio inside the logarithm with the two products and peel off the $t = 1$ factor. Because the chain is Markov, $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0)$, and Bayes' theorem turns each forward step into a <em>backward</em> one: $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0) = q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\, q(x_t \mid x_0) / q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$. The ratios $q(x_t \mid x_0)/q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$ telescope to $q(x_T \mid x_0)/q(x_1 \mid x_0)$, and the bound becomes

$$
\mathbb{E}_q\!\left[-\log \frac{p(x_T)}{q(x_T \mid x_0)} - \sum_{t>1} \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)} - \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)\right].
$$

Each log-ratio averaged over its $q$ is a KL divergence.

</aside>

Read the three terms one by one.

- **First term.** $q(x_T \mid x_0)$ is the fixed forward process and $p(x_T)$ is the fixed standard normal. Nothing here depends on $\theta$, so for training it is a constant. (It is also tiny for a well-chosen schedule: DDPM's linear schedule reaches $\bar\alpha_{1000} \approx 4 \times 10^{-5}$, so $q(x_T \mid x_0) \approx \mathcal N(0, I)$. The course notebook's toy schedule stops at $\bar\alpha_{100} \approx 0.605$ and does not get there.)
- **Last term.** $-\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)$ measures how likely the model is to recreate the image from the slightly noisier $x_1$. At $x_1$ barely any noise is left, so for the derivation we set this term aside. This is a simplification: DDPM keeps the term as a discretized decoder for 8-bit pixel values, and in the final simplified loss it reappears as the $t = 1$ noise-regression term.
- **Middle terms.** These do the work. Each compares the "**true posterior**" $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ with the model's **reverse diffusion step** $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$.

Simplified, the objective is

$$
\mathbb{E}_q\!\left[ \sum_{t > 1} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\underbrace{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)}_{\text{"true posterior"}} \,\big\|\, \underbrace{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}_{\text{reverse diffusion step}}\big) \right].
$$

## The true posterior

The three states and the three conditional distributions fit into one small graph. $x_{t-1}$ is noisy, $x_t$ is more noisy, and $x_0$ is the original image.

<PosteriorGraph />

The forward step $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ goes right. The model's step $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ goes left, using only $x_t$. The true posterior $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ also goes left, but it looks at both $x_t$ and the original image $x_0$.

The true posterior has a **closed-form expression** (derived below). So why do we need a neural network at all? Because the true posterior requires knowing $x_0$. At generation time we have no image; we have to produce one starting from pure noise alone. The network $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ must therefore imitate the true posterior without seeing $x_0$.

So we try to **match the true posterior and our approximate posterior**:

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal N\!\big(\mu_\theta(x_t, t),\; \sigma_\theta^2(x_t, t)\, I\big)
\quad \longleftrightarrow \quad
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal N\!\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0),\; \tilde\beta_t I\big).
$$

Why Gaussians?

1. **A theorem**, which needs a qualification. For a diffusion with small steps, the reverse of a Gaussian Markov chain is also (approximately) Gaussian: as $\beta_t \to 0$, the reverse step $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ has the same Gaussian functional form as the forward step ([Feller, 1949](#references); used by [Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015](#references)). For finite steps $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ is *not* exactly Gaussian, because it mixes over all images $x_0$ in the data. The posterior conditioned on $x_0$, $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$, is exactly Gaussian for any $\beta_t$.
2. **Everything is just simpler.** KL divergences between Gaussians have closed forms, and sampling from a Gaussian is trivial.

**The closed form.** By Bayes' theorem, $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \propto q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\, q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$. Both factors are Gaussian in $x_{t-1}$, so their product is Gaussian. Completing the square adds the precisions, $1/\tilde\beta_t = \alpha_t/\beta_t + 1/(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})$, and gives

$$
\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_t,
\qquad
\tilde\beta_t = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \beta_t .
$$

The true posterior mean is a weighted mix of the image and the current noisy state.

## Matching two Gaussians: KL becomes L2

We would still have to predict both $\mu_\theta$ and $\sigma_\theta$. DDPM simplifies further: **fix $\sigma_\theta$ to a schedule $\sigma_t$**, so it is not learned, and only learn the mean. Ho et al. use $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ or $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t$ and report similar results.

With fixed variances, the KL divergence between the two Gaussians depends on $\theta$ only through the distance between the means. For $q = \mathcal N(\tilde\mu_t, \tilde\beta_t I)$ and $p_\theta = \mathcal N(\mu_\theta, \sigma_t^2 I)$ in $d$ dimensions,

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p_\theta)
= \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\big\|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\big\|^2
+ \underbrace{\frac d2\left(\frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2} - 1 - \log\frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2}\right)}_{\text{const; }0\text{ if }\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t} .
$$

So in fact the KL term is an **L2 loss**, $\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\|^2$, plus a constant. Training slides the model's Gaussian along until its mean $\mu_\theta$ sits on the true posterior mean $\tilde\mu_t$.

<EqualVarianceKL />

So:

$$
\mathbb{E}_q\!\left[ \sum_{t > 1} \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\, \big\| \tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 \right] \;\to\; \min_\theta .
$$

## From means to noise

The true posterior mean still contains $x_0$. Remove it with the **reparameterisation** of the forward shortcut, as in the VAE:

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
x_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\big(x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon\big).
$$

Substitute into $\tilde\mu_t$. The key identity is $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}$, so $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$. The coefficient of $x_t$ collects to

$$
\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)} + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}
= \frac{\beta_t + \alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}
= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}},
$$

using $\beta_t + \alpha_t = 1$. The coefficient of $\varepsilon$ is $-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}$. Hence

$$
\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon \right).
$$

The denoiser has access to $x_t$ and $t$, but not to $\varepsilon$. So give the model's mean exactly the same form and let the network predict only the missing piece, the noise:

$$
\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \right).
$$

Now both $\tilde\mu_t$ and $\mu_\theta(x_t, t)$ are expressed using only $x_t$ and a noise. In the difference $x_t$ cancels, leaving $\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta = \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\big(\varepsilon_\theta - \varepsilon\big)$, and the loss becomes

$$
L = \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[ \sum_{t > 1} \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\, \alpha_t\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\, \big\| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 \right].
$$

Here $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ is the **network's estimate of the noise** that was added to $x_0$ to produce $x_t$. The denoiser is literally a noise detector.

## Final simplification: a random timestep per sample

Summing over thousands of steps for every training image is too tedious. Instead, pick a **random timestep for each sample**. Since $\sum_{t=2}^{T} f(t) = (T - 1)\, \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{2, \dots, T\}}\big[f(t)\big]$, one random $t$ gives an unbiased estimate of the sum, up to the constant factor $T-1$, which does not change the minimiser. Over a large number of samples, the average converges to the original objective divided by $T - 1$, which has the same minimiser. The final loss is

$$
\mathbb{E}_{q,\,t}\!\left[ \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\, \alpha_t\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\, \big\| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 \right],
\qquad x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon .
$$

**The end.** One training step needs no chain at all: draw an image, a timestep and a noise, jump straight to $x_t$ with the shortcut, and regress the noise.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Ho et al. drop the weight.</strong> The bound-derived objective keeps the factor $\beta_t^2 / (2\sigma_t^2 \alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_t))$. DDPM's best results come from the unweighted <em>simplified</em> loss $L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\big[\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\, t)\|^2\big]$ with $t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{1, \dots, T\}$, where the $t = 1$ term stands in for the reconstruction term. The course notebook trains exactly this unweighted loss.</aside>

Dropping the weight is a deliberate reweighting, not a cosmetic change. With the linear schedule of the paper ($T = 1000$, $\beta_t$ from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$) and $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$, the weight simplifies to $\beta_t / (2\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_t))$. It is $0.273$ at $t = 2$, falls to a minimum of $0.0050$ at $t = 349$, and climbs back to $0.0102$ at $t = T$. The true bound puts about $55$ times more weight on the nearly clean steps than on the middle of the chain. $L_{\text{simple}}$ weights every step equally, which shifts effort towards the harder, noisier steps; Ho et al. found that this gives better samples, at the price of a slightly worse likelihood.

## Sampling: running the chain backwards

Training never runs the chain, but generation does. Start from pure noise $x_T \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and apply the learned reverse step $T$ times. Each step samples from $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_t, t), \sigma_t^2 I)$, using the noise form of the mean derived above ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Algorithm 2):

$$
x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \right) + \sigma_t z,
\qquad z \sim \mathcal N(0, I) \text{ for } t > 1,\quad z = 0 \text{ for } t = 1 .
$$

Each step removes the network's estimate of the noise, rescales, and adds a little fresh noise; the last step returns the mean without extra noise. Generation therefore costs $T$ network evaluations, one per step, which is the main practical drawback of DDPM. Later samplers such as DDIM ([Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021](#references)) reuse the same trained $\varepsilon_\theta$ with far fewer steps.

## The course so far

The four families of the course answer the same question, how to train a generator when $p(x)$ is unknown, in four ways. [VAEs](/notes/generative-models/vae) bound the likelihood with an encoder. [GANs](/notes/generative-models/gans) avoid the likelihood and train against a discriminator. [Normalizing flows](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows) compute the likelihood exactly with invertible layers. Diffusion models bound the likelihood again, but with a fixed encoder, the noising chain, so that only the decoder is learned, and the bound collapses into a simple regression on noise. All of them are judged by the same [metrics](/notes/generative-models/inception-score), and all of them rest on the [KL divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences) and its relatives.
