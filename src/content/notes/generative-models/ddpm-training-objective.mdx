The [forward process](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process) gave us a diffusion process: a fixed chain that turns an image into Gaussian noise. To generate images we have to **invert** it, and that is the job of a deep neural network. This note derives what the network is trained to do. The route is the same as for the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae): the likelihood is intractable, so we minimise an upper bound on the negative log-likelihood instead. The bound then simplifies, step by step, into a plain mean squared error on noise.

**Notation.** The forward step adds noise with variance $\beta_t \in (0, 1)$ at step $t = 1, \dots, T$. We write

$$
\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t, \qquad \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s, \qquad \bar\alpha_0 = 1,
$$

so the forward step and its closed-form shortcut from the previous note are

$$
\begin{gathered}
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\; \beta_t I\big), \\
q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\; (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

The second argument of $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is always the **variance**. $q$ denotes the fixed forward process and $q(x_0)$ the data distribution where it starts, $p_\theta$ the learned reverse process, $\theta$ the parameters of the neural network, and $p(x_T) = \mathcal N(0, I)$ the noise distribution where generation starts.

## The reverse process

The image $x_0$ sits at one end of the chain and pure noise $x_T$ at the other. Going forward, the step $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ is known. Going backward we need $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$: the probability density that we have to learn in order to invert the process.

<InverseProcessChain />

What should $\theta$ optimise? The usual answer for a generative model: make real images likely. We minimise the **negative log-likelihood**, averaged over the data,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x_0 \sim q(x_0)}\big[-\log p_\theta(x_0)\big] \;\to\; \min_\theta ,
$$

which is the same as maximising the likelihood, the density the model assigns to real images. The derivation below works with one image $x_0$; the average over images is added back at the end.

## Complete forward and reverse processes

The **complete forward process** is the joint distribution of all noisy versions of an image. By the chain rule of probability,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& q(x_1, \dots, x_T \mid x_0) \\
&\quad = q(x_1 \mid x_0)\, q(x_2 \mid x_1, x_0) \\
&\qquad \cdots q(x_T \mid x_{T-1}, \dots, x_0).
\end{aligned}
$$

The forward process is a **Markov chain**: each next state depends only on the previous one, so every factor keeps only its last condition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& q(x_1, \dots, x_T \mid x_0) \\
&\quad = q(x_1 \mid x_0)\, q(x_2 \mid x_1) \cdots q(x_T \mid x_{T-1}).
\end{aligned}
$$

With the shorthand $x_{s:T} = (x_s, \dots, x_T)$, so that $x_{1:T}$ are the noisy states and $x_{0:T}$ the whole chain, the complete forward and reverse processes are

$$
\begin{gathered}
\underbrace{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})}_{\text{complete forward process}} , \\[4pt]
\underbrace{p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}_{\text{complete reverse process}} .
\end{gathered}
$$

The reverse process starts from $p(x_T) = \mathcal N(0, I)$. This factor has no parameters and needs no knowledge about the data: it is the fixed standard normal, the same for every dataset. All learning sits in the reverse steps $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$.

## The exact log-likelihood is intractable

To get the exact log-likelihood of an image, take the joint reverse distribution and integrate out all intermediate states. This is **marginalisation**, extracting a marginal from the joint distribution:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x_0) = -\log \int p_\theta(x_{0:T})\, dx_{1:T} .
$$

Can we use it? In DDPM each reverse step is a Gaussian whose mean is computed from $x_t$ by a neural network (made precise below). The network is nonlinear in $x_t$, so, unlike the forward chain of linear Gaussian steps, the composition of reverse steps is not Gaussian and the integral has no closed form. This is the same obstacle as for the [VAE decoder](/notes/generative-models/vae#the-latent-variable-model-and-its-posterior). What remains is numerical integration, so look at what the integral means. Every value of $x_{1:T}$ is one **path** from a noise sample $x_T$ to the image $x_0$, and the integral adds up the probabilities of all such paths. For images, each $x_t$ has as many dimensions as there are pixels, and there are $T$ of them (hundreds or thousands of steps), so the integral runs over $T \times (\text{number of pixels})$ dimensions. We cannot really compute all of those paths.

## An upper bound via Jensen's inequality

The trick is the one from the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from). Multiply and divide by the complete forward process $q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)$, which we know and can sample from (it is a product of Gaussians, positive everywhere, so the division is safe):

$$
\begin{aligned}
& -\log p_\theta(x_0) \\
&\quad = -\log \int q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\, \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\, dx_{1:T} \\
&\quad = -\log \mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\!\left[ \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The logarithm is concave, so Jensen's inequality, $\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log Y]$, moves it inside the expectation and turns the equality into a bound:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\log p_\theta(x_0) &\le -\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\!\left[ \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The right-hand side is an **upper bound on the negative log-likelihood**. It is the negative **ELBO**: equivalently, the ELBO is a lower bound on the log-likelihood. The bound only involves quantities we can sample and evaluate. Minimising it caps the negative log-likelihood from above, but the two are not the same. The gap is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\big)$, exactly as in the VAE ([how loose is the bound](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from)), and it also depends on $\theta$. Lowering the bound therefore lowers the negative log-likelihood unless the gap grows.

## Splitting the bound into KL terms

The algebra is in Appendix A of the DDPM paper ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 5) and is sketched in the callout below. The bound becomes a sum of three kinds of terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[\, \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_T \mid x_0) \,\|\, p(x_T)\big)}_{\text{does not depend on }\theta} \\
& + \sum_{t > 1} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\big) \\
& \underbrace{-\, \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)}_{\text{reconstruction}}
\,\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\mathbb{E}_q$ averages over the noisy states $x_{1:T} \sim q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)$ of one image. Each term needs only some of them: the $t$-th KL depends only on $x_t$, so its average is over $x_t \sim q(x_t \mid x_0)$, and the reconstruction term depends only on $x_1$. In training we also average over images $x_0 \sim q(x_0)$.

<aside class="callout">

<strong>Where the KL terms come from.</strong> Write the ratio inside the logarithm with the two products. The bound becomes a sum of per-step log-ratios, each comparing a reverse step with the forward step it should undo ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 3):

$$
\mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[ -\log p(x_T) - \sum_{t \ge 1} \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})} \Bigg].
$$

The two densities in each ratio are densities of different variables, $x_{t-1}$ and $x_t$, so a ratio is not yet a KL divergence. Peel off the $t = 1$ factor $p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)/q(x_1 \mid x_0)$. For $t \ge 2$, because the chain is Markov, $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0)$, and Bayes' theorem turns each forward step into a <em>backward</em> one: $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0) = q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)\, q(x_t \mid x_0) / q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$. The ratios $q(x_t \mid x_0)/q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$ telescope to $q(x_T \mid x_0)/q(x_1 \mid x_0)$. The $q(x_1 \mid x_0)$ left over cancels the one in the peeled-off factor, and the bound becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[ & -\log \frac{p(x_T)}{q(x_T \mid x_0)} \\
& - \sum_{t>1} \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)} \\
& - \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

Averaging the $t$-th log-ratio over $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ gives the KL divergence, and the outer expectation is over $x_t \sim q(x_t \mid x_0)$; the first term is a KL in the same way, averaged over $q(x_T \mid x_0)$. [Luo (2022)](#references) writes out every step.

</aside>

<aside class="callout">

<strong>The 2015 form of the bound.</strong> [Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015)](#references), §2.4, Eq. 14, write the same bound with entropies in place of the two end terms. As a lower bound $K \le \mathbb E[\log p_\theta(x_0)]$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
K = {} & -\sum_{t=2}^{T} \mathbb E_q\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\big) \\
& + H_q(X_T \mid X_0) - H_q(X_1 \mid X_0) - H_p(X_T).
\end{aligned}
$$

Two choices turn the end terms into constants (their Appendix B). The last reverse step is not learned: it is fixed to the reversal of the first forward step under the prior $\pi = \mathcal N(0, I)$, $p(x_0 \mid x_1) = q(x_1 \mid x_0)\,\pi(x_0)/\pi(x_1)$. And for data scaled to mean $0$ and variance $1$, the cross-entropy $\mathbb E_q[-\log \pi(x_t)]$ is the same at every $t$, because $\mathbb E\lVert x_t\rVert^2 = \bar\alpha_t\,\mathbb E\lVert x_0\rVert^2 + (1 - \bar\alpha_t)\,d = d$; it equals the entropy $H_p(X_T)$ of the prior. Every entropy left is that of a Gaussian, $H = \tfrac d2\big(1 + \ln(2\pi\sigma^2)\big)$ for $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2 I)$, with $\sigma^2 = 1 - \bar\alpha_t$ for $H_q(X_t \mid X_0)$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$ for $H_p(X_T)$. With DDPM's linear schedule, per dimension, $H_q(X_T \mid X_0)$ and $H_p(X_T)$ are both about $1.419$ nats and almost cancel, while $H_q(X_1 \mid X_0) = \tfrac12\ln(2\pi e \cdot 10^{-4}) \approx -3.19$ nats. Sohl-Dickstein et al. report $K - L_{\text{null}}$ in bits, the gain over the log-likelihood $L_{\text{null}}$ that the isotropic Gaussian $\pi$ itself assigns to the data.

</aside>

Read the three terms one by one.

- **First term.** $q(x_T \mid x_0)$ is the fixed forward process and $p(x_T)$ is the fixed standard normal. Nothing here depends on $\theta$, so for training it is a constant. (It is also tiny for a well-chosen schedule: DDPM's linear schedule reaches $\bar\alpha_{1000} \approx 4 \times 10^{-5}$, so $q(x_T \mid x_0) \approx \mathcal N(0, I)$. A toy schedule with $T = 100$ steps and $\beta_t$ rising from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-2}$ ends near $\bar\alpha_{100} \approx 0.6$ and does not get there.)
- **Last term.** $-\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)$ measures how likely the model is to recreate the image from the slightly noisier $x_1$. At $x_1$ barely any noise is left, so for the derivation we set this term aside. This is a simplification: DDPM keeps the term as a discretized decoder for 8-bit pixel values ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §3.3, Eq. 13; callout below), and in its simplified loss the $t = 1$ noise-regression term approximates it (§3.4).
- **Middle terms.** These do the work. Each compares the "**true posterior**" $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ with the model's **reverse diffusion step** $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$.

<aside class="callout">

<strong>The discretized decoder and bits per dimension.</strong> Pixels are integers $0, \dots, 255$ scaled linearly to $[-1, 1]$, so neighbouring values are $2/255$ apart. DDPM's last step gives each value the probability mass of its bin under the Gaussian of the reverse step ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 13):

$$
\begin{gathered}
p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1) = \prod_{i=1}^{D} \int_{\delta_-(x_0^i)}^{\delta_+(x_0^i)} \mathcal N\big(x;\ \mu_\theta^i(x_1, 1),\ \sigma_1^2\big)\,dx, \\
\delta_\pm(x) = x \pm \tfrac{1}{255}, \qquad \delta_+(1) = +\infty, \qquad \delta_-(-1) = -\infty .
\end{gathered}
$$

$D$ is the number of pixel values in the image, and the bins of the two extreme values extend to infinity, so the probabilities of the 256 values sum to $1$. Each factor is a difference of two values of the standard normal CDF $\Phi$. **Worked example** with $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{\beta_1} = 0.01$. Pixel value 128 becomes $x_0 = 128/127.5 - 1 \approx 0.0039$. With the mean exactly on it, the bin $\pm 1/255$ spans $\pm 0.392$ standard deviations, so its probability is $2\Phi(0.392) - 1 = 0.305$, or $-\log_2 0.305 = 1.71$ bits. A mean off by $0.004$ gives $0.283$ and $1.82$ bits. The extreme value 255 with the mean on it gets $\Phi(0.392) = 0.653$, $0.62$ bits, because its bin is open to the right. The same construction with a mixture of logistic distributions in place of the Gaussian is the output layer of [PixelCNN++](/notes/generative-models/autoregressive-models#pixelcnn-plus-plus).

Likelihoods are reported in **bits per dimension**: the bound on $-\ln p_\theta(x_0)$ in nats, divided by $D \ln 2$ ([definition](/notes/generative-models/autoregressive-models#pixels-as-discrete-values)). Every term is converted the same way, the KL terms of the middle steps and the decoder term at $t = 1$ alike. For CIFAR-10, $D = 32 \cdot 32 \cdot 3 = 3072$, so the $3.75$ bits per dimension of DDPM below mean about $11{,}520$ bits per image.

</aside>

Simplified, the objective is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[ \sum_{t > 1} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\Big( & \underbrace{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)}_{\text{true posterior}} \\
& \Big\|\, \underbrace{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}_{\text{reverse step}} \Big) \Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

## The true posterior

The three states and the three conditional distributions fit into one small graph. $x_{t-1}$ is noisy, $x_t$ is more noisy, and $x_0$ is the original image.

<PosteriorGraph />

The forward step $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ goes right. The model's step $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ goes left, using only $x_t$. The true posterior $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$ also goes left, but it looks at both $x_t$ and the original image $x_0$.

The true posterior has a **closed-form expression** (derived below). So why do we need a neural network at all? Because the true posterior requires knowing $x_0$. At generation time we have no image; we have to produce one starting from pure noise alone. The network $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ must therefore imitate the true posterior without seeing $x_0$.

So we **train the model's reverse step to match the true posterior**:

$$
\begin{gathered}
p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal N\!\big(\mu_\theta(x_t, t),\; \sigma_\theta^2(x_t, t)\, I\big) \\
\big\updownarrow \\
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal N\!\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0),\; \tilde\beta_t I\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

Why Gaussians?

1. **Small steps keep the reverse step Gaussian.** For a diffusion with small steps, the reverse of a Gaussian Markov chain is also (approximately) Gaussian: as $\beta_t \to 0$, the reverse step $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ has the same Gaussian functional form as the forward step ([Feller, 1949](#references); used by [Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015](#references), §2.2). For finite steps $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ is *not* exactly Gaussian, because it mixes over all images $x_0$ in the data. The posterior conditioned on $x_0$, $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$, is exactly Gaussian for any $\beta_t$ when $t \ge 2$. At $t = 1$ it collapses to the point mass at $x_0$ ($\tilde\beta_1 = 0$), which is why that step is handled by the reconstruction term.
2. **Everything is just simpler.** KL divergences between Gaussians have closed forms, and sampling from a Gaussian is trivial.

**The closed form.** Let $t \ge 2$. By Bayes' theorem, $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \propto q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\, q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)$. Both factors are Gaussian in $x_{t-1}$. The first has the exponent $-(x_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1})^2/(2\beta_t) = -\alpha_t\,(x_{t-1} - x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t})^2/(2\beta_t)$ (per coordinate), so as a function of $x_{t-1}$ it has mean $x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$ and precision $\alpha_t/\beta_t$. The second is $\mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0,\; 1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}\big)$. Completing the square in the product, the precisions add and the mean is the precision-weighted average of the two means:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{\tilde\beta_t} &= \frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}
= \frac{\alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t}{\beta_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})} \\
&= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\beta_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}, \\
\tilde\mu_t &= \tilde\beta_t \left( \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t}\, x_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0 \right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The numerator simplifies by $\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t = \alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t + \beta_t = 1 - \bar\alpha_t$, using $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}$ and $\alpha_t + \beta_t = 1$. Inverting the precision and multiplying out the mean gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) &= \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 \\
&\quad + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_t, \\
\tilde\beta_t &= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \beta_t .
\end{aligned}
$$

The true posterior mean is a weighted mix of the image and the current noisy state.

## Matching two Gaussians: KL becomes L2

We would still have to predict both $\mu_\theta$ and $\sigma_\theta$. DDPM simplifies further: **fix $\sigma_\theta$ to a schedule $\sigma_t$**, so it is not learned, and only learn the mean. Ho et al. use $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$ or $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t$ and report similar results ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §3.2); [Nichol and Dhariwal (2021)](#references), §3.1, later learn a variance that interpolates between the two.

With fixed variances, the KL divergence between the two Gaussians depends on $\theta$ only through the distance between the means. For $q = \mathcal N(\tilde\mu_t, \tilde\beta_t I)$ and $p_\theta = \mathcal N(\mu_\theta, \sigma_t^2 I)$ in $d$ dimensions, the [KL divergence between two 1-D Gaussians](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#what-the-gradients-are), summed over the $d$ coordinates, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\|\, p_\theta)
&= \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\,\big\|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\big\|^2 \\
&\quad + \underbrace{\frac d2\left(\frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2} - 1 - \log\frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2}\right)}_{\text{const; }0\text{ if }\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The same result follows from the general formula for two Gaussians in $d$ dimensions with positive definite covariances,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu_q, \Sigma_q) \,\|\, \mathcal N(\mu_p, \Sigma_p)\big) \\
&\quad = \tfrac12\Big[\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_p^{-1}\Sigma_q) - d + \ln\frac{\det\Sigma_p}{\det\Sigma_q} \\
&\qquad\quad + (\mu_p - \mu_q)^\top \Sigma_p^{-1} (\mu_p - \mu_q)\Big],
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\Sigma_q = \tilde\beta_t I$ and $\Sigma_p = \sigma_t^2 I$: the trace gives $d\,\tilde\beta_t/\sigma_t^2$, the log-determinant ratio $d \ln(\sigma_t^2/\tilde\beta_t)$, and the quadratic form $\lVert\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\rVert^2/\sigma_t^2$.

So in fact the KL term is an **L2 loss**, $\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta\|^2$, plus a constant. For one pair $(x_t, x_0)$ it would be zero with $\mu_\theta$ on $\tilde\mu_t$. But $\tilde\mu_t$ depends on $x_0$, which the network does not see, and many images are compatible with the same $x_t$. Since $\mu_\theta$ sees only $x_t$, the best it can do is the average of $\tilde\mu_t$ over all images $x_0$ compatible with $x_t$, because the conditional mean minimises the expected squared error:

$$
\mu_\theta^*(x_t, t) = \mathbb{E}_q\big[\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) \mid x_t\big],
$$

the average over $x_0 \sim q(x_0 \mid x_t)$. By the tower property this is also $\mathbb{E}_q[x_{t-1} \mid x_t]$, the mean of the true reverse step $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$. At the optimum the KL term is therefore not zero: what remains reflects how much $x_t$ leaves the image undetermined.

<EqualVarianceKL />

So:

$$
\mathbb{E}_q\!\left[ \sum_{t > 1} \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\, \big\| \tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 \right] \;\to\; \min_\theta .
$$

## From means to noise

The true posterior mean still contains $x_0$. Remove it with the **reparameterisation** of the forward shortcut, as in the VAE:

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I) \\
\Longrightarrow\quad
x_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\big(x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

Substitute into $\tilde\mu_t$. The key identity is $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}$, so $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$. The coefficient of $x_t$ collects to

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)} + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \\
&\quad= \frac{\beta_t + \alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}
= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}
= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}},
\end{aligned}
$$

using $\beta_t + \alpha_t = 1$. The coefficient of $\varepsilon$ is $-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}$. Hence

$$
\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon \right).
$$

The denoiser has access to $x_t$ and $t$, but not to $\varepsilon$. So give the model's mean exactly the same form and let the network predict only the missing piece, the noise ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 11):

$$
\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \right).
$$

Now both $\tilde\mu_t$ and $\mu_\theta(x_t, t)$ are expressed using only $x_t$ and a noise. In the difference $x_t$ cancels, leaving $\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta = \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\big(\varepsilon_\theta - \varepsilon\big)$, and the loss becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
L = \mathbb{E}_q\Bigg[ & \sum_{t > 1} \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\, \alpha_t\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)} \\
& \cdot \big\| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 \Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

This is Eq. 12 of [Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020)](#references), summed over $t$. Here $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ is the **network's prediction of the noise**. As for the means, it cannot recover the particular $\varepsilon$ that produced $x_t$, because it does not see $x_0$. The optimum is $\varepsilon_\theta^*(x_t, t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t]$, the average noise consistent with $x_t$; this is the same optimum as $\mu_\theta^*$, since $\tilde\mu_t$ is an affine function of $\varepsilon$ for fixed $x_t$. The denoiser is a noise predictor.

## Final simplification: a random timestep per sample

Summing over all $T - 1$ terms for every training image is too expensive: it costs $T - 1$ network evaluations per image. Instead, pick a **random timestep for each sample**. Since $\sum_{t=2}^{T} f(t) = (T - 1)\, \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{2, \dots, T\}}\big[f(t)\big]$, one uniformly drawn $t$ gives an unbiased estimate of the sum divided by $T - 1$, and a constant factor does not change the minimiser. Averaging also over images $x_0 \sim q(x_0)$ and noises $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, with $t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{2, \dots, T\}$, the final loss is

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbb{E}_{x_0,\, t,\, \varepsilon}\!\left[ \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\, \alpha_t\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\, \big\| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\|^2 \right], \\
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon .
\end{gathered}
$$

**Pairing timesteps.** The estimate stays unbiased however the timesteps of a batch depend on each other, as long as each one on its own is uniform. A variance-reduction trick used in the public DDIM code ([Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021](#references)) draws $t$ for half of the batch and gives the other half the mirrored steps, $t' = T + 1 - t$ when $t$ runs over $1, \dots, T$, which are uniform too. Let $f(t)$ be the expected loss at step $t$. A mirrored pair estimates the mean of $f$ with variance $\tfrac12\big[\operatorname{Var} f(t) + \operatorname{Cov}\big(f(t), f(t')\big)\big]$, two independent draws with $\tfrac12 \operatorname{Var} f(t)$. If $f$ is monotone in $t$, then $f(t)$ and $f(T + 1 - t)$ move in opposite directions, the covariance is negative by Chebyshev's sum inequality, and the pair wins. **Toy example:** $T = 4$ and $f(t) = 1/t$, a loss that is largest at the least noisy step. Two independent draws give variance $0.0423$, a mirrored pair $0.0109$, about $3.9$ times less. The per-step loss of a real network is only roughly monotone in $t$, so the gain in practice is an empirical matter.

**The training step.** One training step needs no chain at all: draw an image, a timestep and a noise, jump straight to $x_t$ with the shortcut, and regress the noise.

## Sampling: running the chain backwards

Training never runs the chain, but generation does. Start from pure noise $x_T \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and apply the learned reverse step $T$ times. Each step samples from $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_t, t), \sigma_t^2 I)$, using the noise form of the mean derived above, with fresh noise $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ for $t > 1$ and $z = 0$ for $t = 1$ ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Algorithm 2):

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{t-1} = {} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \right) \\
& + \sigma_t z .
\end{aligned}
$$

Each step removes the network's estimate of the noise, rescales, and adds a little fresh noise; the last step returns the mean without extra noise. Generation therefore costs $T$ network evaluations, one per step, which is the main practical drawback of DDPM. Later samplers such as DDIM ([Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021](#references)) reuse the same trained $\varepsilon_\theta$ with far fewer steps.

## The denoising network

The derivation treats $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ as a function of two arguments. In practice it is one network for all steps, a U-Net, that receives the timestep as an embedding added inside every block.

<DenoiserFlow />

1. The noisy image $x_t$ enters the encoder. Each level halves the resolution; DDPM adds self-attention at $16 \times 16$.
2. The timestep $t$ becomes a vector of sines and cosines $e(t)$.
3. A small MLP turns $e(t)$ into $m(t)$, and every residual block adds its own linear projection $A_k\,m(t)$ to its channels.
4. The decoder doubles the resolution level by level; skip connections bring in the encoder's features of the same size.
5. The output $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ has the shape of $x_t$ and is compared with the true noise $\varepsilon$.

### One network for all timesteps

A separate network per step would mean $T = 1000$ networks, each trained on a thousandth of the updates. Neighbouring steps solve almost the same problem: $x_{t-1}$ and $x_t$ differ by one small step: a rescaling by $\sqrt{1 - \beta_t} \ge 0.99$ and noise of variance $\beta_t \le 0.02$. DDPM therefore shares the parameters across time and tells the network which step it is at ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §4). The step is needed because the same input calls for different outputs at different noise levels.

**Example.** For data $\mathcal N(0, 0.5^2)$ in one dimension, $\varepsilon$ and $x_t$ are jointly Gaussian with covariance $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$, so the optimal prediction is

$$
\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t] = \frac{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}{0.25\,\bar\alpha_t + 1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_t .
$$

With the linear schedule and $x_t = 1$ it is $0.17$ at $t = 10$ ($\bar\alpha_t = 0.998$), where $x_t$ is mostly image, and $1.02$ at $t = 500$ ($\bar\alpha_t = 0.079$), where $x_t$ is mostly noise.

### A U-Net

The output $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ has the shape of the input, one noise value per pixel and channel, so the network solves a dense prediction problem, like segmentation. To tell noise from image detail at one pixel it needs context from far away and precision at the pixel itself. A **U-Net** ([Ronneberger, Fischer and Brox, 2015](#references)) provides both. An encoder halves the resolution level by level, so that the coarse levels see the whole image; a decoder doubles it back; and **skip connections** pass the encoder's features at each resolution to the decoder level of the same size, where they are concatenated with the upsampled features, so that fine detail reaches the output. Noise-conditional score networks use a U-Net variant for the same reason ([Song and Ermon, 2019](#references), §4.1 and App. A.3).

DDPM's network ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Appendix B):

- the backbone of an unmasked [PixelCNN++](/notes/generative-models/autoregressive-models#pixelcnn-plus-plus), a U-Net built from Wide ResNet residual blocks;
- group normalization ([Wu and He, 2018](#references)) instead of weight normalization: statistics over groups of channels within one image, so that, unlike [batch normalization](/notes/generative-models/training-background#batch-normalization), they do not depend on the other images of the batch;
- four feature-map resolutions for $32 \times 32$ images ($32 \times 32$ down to $4 \times 4$) and six for $256 \times 256$, with two residual blocks per resolution;
- self-attention at the $16 \times 16$ resolution, between the convolutional blocks;
- the timestep added to every residual block through the Transformer sinusoidal embedding;
- 35.7 million parameters for CIFAR-10, 114 million for LSUN and CelebA-HQ at $256 \times 256$, and about 256 million for a larger LSUN Bedroom variant.

### Telling the network the timestep

DDPM encodes $t$ the way a Transformer encodes a position ([positional encodings](/notes/nlp/positional-encoding#sinusoidal-encoding)):

$$
\begin{gathered}
e(t)_{2i} = \sin(\omega_i t), \qquad e(t)_{2i+1} = \cos(\omega_i t), \\
\omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d}, \qquad i = 0, \dots, d/2 - 1 .
\end{gathered}
$$

A small MLP turns the code into a feature vector, $m(t) = W_2\,\mathrm{SiLU}(W_1\, e(t))$ with $\mathrm{SiLU}(z) = z \cdot \mathrm{sigmoid}(z)$, and every residual block $k$ has its own linear map $A_k$ to its number of channels $C_k$. The result is added to the block's feature maps as one bias per channel, the same at every pixel:

$$
h_k[c, i, j] \;\leftarrow\; h_k[c, i, j] + \big(A_k\, m(t)\big)_c .
$$

Why not feed $t$ as a plain number? A scalar enters the first layer as one more input, and the network has to build every dependence on $t$ from it. The sinusoidal code is a vector whose components turn at rates from $1$ down to about $10^{-4}$ radians per step, so linear combinations of it can follow both fast and slow changes in $t$, and nearby steps get nearby codes. The order of the components, interleaved or sines first, does not matter: the next layer is linear and can permute its inputs.

Other ways to give a network the step or another condition:

- **Scale and shift.** The embedding sets a scale and a shift per channel after normalization, $h \leftarrow y_s(t) \odot \mathrm{GroupNorm}(h) + y_b(t)$: *adaptive group normalization* ([Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021](#references), §3.1). It is the diffusion counterpart of the adaptive instance normalization (AdaIN) with which StyleGAN injects its style vector ([Karras, Laine and Aila, 2019](#references), Eq. 1). On ImageNet $128 \times 128$ it improved FID from $15.08$, for addition followed by group normalization as in DDPM, to $13.06$ (their Table 3). Noise-conditional score networks use the same idea with one learned scale and shift per noise level ([score matching note](/notes/generative-models/score-matching#noise-conditional-scores-and-annealing)).
- **An extra input channel.** Append $t/T$ to the image as a constant channel. This is the simplest option, but the step then enters only the first convolution and has to be carried through every later layer.
- **A learned vector per step.** A table with one trainable vector per step, added to or multiplied with a hidden layer, works for small networks and short chains. The $T$ vectors share nothing, though: each is trained only by the examples drawn at its own step.
- **Other conditions.** A class label or a pooled text embedding can be projected to the size of the time embedding and added to it, as Imagen does ([Saharia et al., 2022](#references), §2.5); a whole sequence of text vectors enters through cross-attention ([latent diffusion](/notes/generative-models/latent-diffusion#conditioning-through-cross-attention)).

### Why the pixels are scaled to [−1, 1] \{#why-the-pixels-are-scaled\}

DDPM maps the pixel values $0, \dots, 255$ linearly to $[-1, 1]$ so that the reverse process works on inputs of a consistent scale, starting from the standard normal prior ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §3.3). Three reasons make this matter:

1. **The chain must end at the prior.** The forward process removes the image only by shrinking it, $x_T = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_T}\,\varepsilon$, and with DDPM's schedule $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T} = 0.0064$. For values in $[-1, 1]$ the leftover image is at most $0.0064$, negligible next to noise of standard deviation $1$. For raw values in $[0, 255]$ it would reach $1.6$: $x_T$ would still show the image, and sampling from $\mathcal N(0, I)$ would start from the wrong distribution.
2. **Every step sees inputs of the same size.** By the [variance recursion](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process#the-objective-and-the-rescaled-step) $\operatorname{Var}(x_t) = \bar\alpha_t \operatorname{Var}(x_0) + 1 - \bar\alpha_t$, which lies between $\operatorname{Var}(x_0)$ and $1$ when $\operatorname{Var}(x_0) \le 1$, as values in $[-1, 1]$ guarantee. One network can then serve all steps; for raw pixel values the variance would fall from thousands to $1$ along the chain.
3. **The data are centred like the prior**, and the [discretized decoder](#splitting-the-bound-into-kl-terms) has bins of width $2/255$ with its open edge bins at $\pm 1$.

To display an image, $x$ is mapped back by $255\,(x + 1)/2$ after clipping to $[-1, 1]$. Noisy states leave the interval routinely: a pixel with $x_0 = 0.9$ at a step with $\bar\alpha_t = 0.5$ has $x_t \sim \mathcal N(0.64,\ 0.5)$, which falls outside $[-1, 1]$ with probability $0.31$. Without the clipping, the conversion to 8-bit integers turns such values into wrong colours.

## Training in practice: averaged weights and clipped gradients

Two stabilisers are common in diffusion training loops.

**An exponential moving average of the weights.** Keep a second copy $\bar\theta$ of the parameters and update it after every optimizer step:

$$
\bar\theta \;\leftarrow\; \mu\, \bar\theta + (1 - \mu)\, \theta .
$$

Unrolled over $n$ steps, $\bar\theta$ is a weighted average of the past iterates with weights $(1 - \mu)\,\mu^{n-k}$, plus $\mu^n$ times its starting value: roughly an average over the last $1/(1 - \mu)$ steps. Training continues with $\theta$; samples are drawn with $\bar\theta$. The reason is the noise of the gradient: every example brings its own image, timestep and noise, so the iterates $\theta$ jitter around a good region, and their average lies closer to its centre. DDPM uses $\mu = 0.9999$ (Appendix B), an average over about $10{,}000$ steps. [Nichol and Dhariwal (2021)](#references) swept the rate on class-conditional ImageNet $64 \times 64$ and found $0.9999$ and $0.99995$ best (their Figure 17). **Worked example:** the starting value keeps the weight $\mu^n$, which is $0.9999^{10\,000} = 0.37$ after ten thousand steps and $0.9999^{50\,000} = 0.0067$ after fifty thousand. The averaged weights are therefore worth using only after training has run several times longer than the window. With $\mu = 0.9$ the window is about $10$ steps, too short to average out much.

**Gradient clipping.** Before each step, rescale the gradient $g$ when its norm exceeds a threshold $c$, $g \leftarrow g \cdot \min(1, c/\lVert g\rVert)$ ([training background](/notes/generative-models/training-background#common-remedies)). The direction is kept and only rare large gradients are shortened: with $c = 1$, a gradient of norm $4$ is divided by $4$, and one of norm $0.5$ is left as it is. The loss of a batch varies with the timesteps it happens to contain, and clipping keeps an unusual batch from moving the weights far.

**DDPM's settings** (Appendix B): Adam with learning rate $2 \times 10^{-4}$ ($2 \times 10^{-5}$ at $256 \times 256$), batch size 128 for CIFAR-10 and 64 for larger images, dropout $0.1$ on CIFAR-10 and none elsewhere, random horizontal flips, the EMA rate $0.9999$ and $T = 1000$. The CIFAR-10 model trained for 800K steps at 21 steps per second on a TPU v3-8, about 10.6 hours; sampling a batch of 256 images took 17 seconds.

- **Cells 33–36** reload the best checkpoint and generate 100 faces into a GIF; there are no saved samples.

Issues:

- `show_images` (cell 7) maps $x$ to $255\,(x + 1)/2$ and casts to `uint8` without clipping. Noised images leave $[-1, 1]$, so the forward grids of cell 26 show saturated, noise-like colours already at 25% of $T$; clip to $[-1, 1]$ before the cast. How much of the face would remain depends on the schedule, which the template leaves blank; with DDPM's linear schedule $\bar\alpha_{250} = 0.52$, so more than half of the signal variance is left at 25%.
- The dataset class of cell 10 returns bare images, so a batch from the loader is a tensor of images, and `batch[0]` in `show_first_batch`, `show_forward` and `training_loop` picks the first image instead of the batch. The template comes from a dataset that returns (image, label) pairs; with this dataset, use the batch itself.
- Cell 33 rebuilds the model as `DDPM(UNet(), n_steps=n_steps, device=device)`: `min_beta` and `max_beta` are not passed, so the class defaults are used, and `UNet()` takes its default 1000 steps. A schedule other than the defaults has to be passed again by hand.
- `sinusoidal_embedding` computes $d$ frequencies $w_k = 10000^{-2k/d}$, $k = 0, \dots, d - 1$, but fills only $d$ values. The Transformer encoding uses the first $d/2$ frequencies, each once in a sine and once in a cosine. Taking the cosines from the second half instead gives frequencies of at most $10^{-4}$, for which $\cos(w_k t) \ge 0.995$ over all $t \le 1000$: those components barely change.
