A DDPM is trained with one line of code: take an image, add Gaussian noise of a random strength, and ask the network to say what noise was added. The loss is the squared error of that guess. Nothing in it mentions likelihoods, chains or posteriors, yet the network it trains generates images by running the [forward process](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process) backwards. Why is this the right regression, and what does it give up?

The answer comes from treating the noisy images $x_1, \dots, x_T$ as latent variables, as in the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae). The encoder is the fixed forward chain, the decoder is the learned reverse chain, and the loss starts as the VAE's bound on the likelihood. Every simplification from that bound to the one-line loss can be derived, and one of them is a deliberate change of objective.

We keep the notation of the forward process: $\beta_t \in (0, 1)$ is the noise variance of step $t$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_t &= 1 - \beta_t , \\
\bar\alpha_t &= \textstyle\prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s , \\
\bar\alpha_0 &= 1 , \\
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) &= \mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1},\; \beta_t I\big) , \\
q(x_t \mid x_0) &= \mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\; (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second argument of $\mathcal N$ is the variance, and $x_{s:t}$ is short for $(x_s, \dots, x_t)$.

## The reverse process

Generation starts from $p(x_T) = \mathcal N(0, I)$, a fixed distribution with no parameters, and applies a learned step $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ for $t = T, \dots, 1$. The complete forward and reverse processes are

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) &= \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) , \\
p_\theta(x_{0:T}) &= p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) .
\end{aligned}
$$

<InverseProcessChain />

What family should $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ belong to? The step it imitates is the true reverse step, by Bayes' theorem

$$
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) \propto q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\, q(x_{t-1}) ,
$$

which involves the unknown marginal $q(x_{t-1})$. When $\beta_t$ is small this step is close to a Gaussian anyway:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) &\approx \mathcal N\big(y + v\, \nabla \log q(y),\; v\big) , \\
y &= x_t / \sqrt{\alpha_t} , \\
v &= \beta_t / \alpha_t ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with an error that vanishes as $\beta_t \to 0$, provided $\log q$ has bounded curvature near $y$ (derivation below). The general statement, that for small steps the reverse of a Gaussian diffusion step is again Gaussian, goes back to Feller (1949); [Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015](#references), §2.2) build on it, and [Isachenko (2025](#references), lecture 7) states it as a theorem.

<details>
<summary>Why a small reverse step is nearly Gaussian</summary>

As a function of $x_{t-1}$, the first factor is a Gaussian centred at $y$ with the tiny variance $v$; its exponent is $-\alpha_t (x_{t-1} - y)^2 / (2\beta_t)$. Over a width of order $\sqrt v$ around $y$, the smooth function $\log q(x_{t-1})$ is close to its first-order Taylor expansion, $\log q(y) + (x_{t-1} - y)\, \nabla \log q(y)$. A Gaussian multiplied by the exponential of a linear function is a Gaussian with the same variance and its mean moved by the variance times the slope (complete the square). That gives the display above.

The error comes from the neglected quadratic term of the Taylor expansion. If the second derivative of $\log q$ stays within $\pm K$ near $y$, that term is at most $\tfrac12 K (x_{t-1} - y)^2$, which is of order $K v$ over the width $\sqrt v$ and goes to $0$ with $v$.

</details>

This is why DDPM takes about a thousand small steps, and why it sets

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal N\big(\mu_\theta(x_t, t),\; \sigma_t^2 I\big) ,
$$

a Gaussian whose mean a network computes from $x_t$ and $t$, and whose variance $\sigma_t^2$ is a fixed number for each step. The gradient $\nabla \log q$ in the mean is the **score** of the noised data; the [score matching note](/notes/generative-models/score-matching#the-same-model-as-ddpm) returns to it.

## An upper bound via Jensen's inequality

We want real images to be likely under the model: minimize $\mathbb E_{q(x_0)}[-\log p_\theta(x_0)]$. The likelihood of one image integrates the reverse process over all intermediate states, every path from noise to the image:

$$
p_\theta(x_0) = \int p_\theta(x_{0:T})\, dx_{1:T} .
$$

The network is nonlinear in $x_t$, so the composed reverse steps are not Gaussian and the integral has no closed form. Numerically it runs over $T$ times the number of pixels dimensions.

<AllPathsIntegral />

The way out is the one of the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from). Multiply and divide by the forward process, which is positive everywhere and easy to sample, then apply Jensen's inequality to the concave logarithm, $\log \mathbb E[Y] \ge \mathbb E[\log Y]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\log p_\theta(x_0)
&= -\log \mathbb E_{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\Big[\frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\Big] \\
&\le \mathbb E_{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\Big[-\log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}\Big] \\
&=: L(x_0) .
\end{aligned}
$$

$L$ is the negative ELBO, an upper bound on the negative log-likelihood. The gap is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)\big)$, by the same decomposition as in [variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference#the-evidence-lower-bound-and-the-reverse-kl). Training minimizes $\mathbb E_{q(x_0)}[L(x_0)]$. As written, $L$ is still an expectation over all $T$ noisy states at once. A one-sample estimate draws a whole path and adds $T$ random log-ratios, so the randomness of every step enters it. The next two sections rewrite $L$ as a sum of terms that each involve one step, and replace the average over each step by its closed form; [Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020](#references), §2) introduce this rewriting as variance reduction.

## The lemma: the reversed chain is Markov given the image

The bound pairs the reverse steps $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ with forward steps $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$, which are densities of different variables. To compare like with like we need the forward process written backwards. The tool is a lemma ([lecture notes](#references), lecture 5, Lemma 1).

**Lemma.** If $q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$, then for every $t < T$

$$
q(x_t \mid x_{t+1}, \dots, x_T, x_0) = q(x_t \mid x_{t+1}, x_0) .
$$

Reversed in time and with $x_0$ held fixed, the chain is still Markov: once $x_{t+1}$ and the image are known, the later, noisier states say nothing more about $x_t$.

<details>
<summary>Proof of the lemma</summary>

Fix $t$. Integrating $x_1, \dots, x_{t-1}$ out of the forward product turns its first $t$ factors into the marginal $q(x_t \mid x_0)$:

$$
q(x_t, \dots, x_T \mid x_0) = q(x_t \mid x_0) \prod_{s=t+1}^{T} q(x_s \mid x_{s-1}) . \tag{1}
$$

By the definition of a conditional density, the same joint is

$$
q(x_t, \dots, x_T \mid x_0) = q(x_t \mid x_{t+1:T}, x_0)\; q(x_{t+1:T} \mid x_0) . \tag{2}
$$

Now rewrite the first two factors of (1). The chain is Markov, so $q(x_{t+1} \mid x_t) = q(x_{t+1} \mid x_t, x_0)$, and Bayes' theorem for the pair $(x_t, x_{t+1})$ given $x_0$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_t \mid x_0)\, q(x_{t+1} \mid x_t)
&= q(x_t \mid x_0)\, q(x_{t+1} \mid x_t, x_0) \\
&= q(x_t \mid x_{t+1}, x_0)\, q(x_{t+1} \mid x_0) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Substituted into (1), the factors left after $q(x_t \mid x_{t+1}, x_0)$ are $q(x_{t+1} \mid x_0) \prod_{s=t+2}^{T} q(x_s \mid x_{s-1})$, which is (1) written for $t + 1$, that is $q(x_{t+1:T} \mid x_0)$:

$$
q(x_t, \dots, x_T \mid x_0) = q(x_t \mid x_{t+1}, x_0)\; q(x_{t+1:T} \mid x_0) .
$$

Comparing with (2) and dividing by $q(x_{t+1:T} \mid x_0) > 0$ proves the lemma.

</details>

The chain rule of probability, applied from the last state backwards, and then the lemma, give the factorization the bound needs:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)
&= q(x_T \mid x_0) \prod_{t=1}^{T-1} q(x_t \mid x_{t+1:T}, x_0) \\
&= q(x_T \mid x_0) \prod_{t=1}^{T-1} q(x_t \mid x_{t+1}, x_0) .
\end{aligned}
$$

## Splitting the bound into KL terms

Put the two factorizations into the ratio inside $L$. In the numerator, peel off the last reverse step $p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)$; in the denominator, shift the index of the product by one, so that both products run over $t = 2, \dots, T$ and pair a reverse step with a backward forward step of the same variables:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}
&= \log \frac{p(x_T)}{q(x_T \mid x_0)} \\
&\quad + \sum_{t=2}^{T} \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)}{q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)} \\
&\quad + \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Each term involves at most two states, so its expectation over all of $x_{1:T}$ reduces to those two. For the term with $(x_{t-1}, x_t)$, apply the definition of a conditional density twice:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0) &= q(x_t \mid x_0)\; q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \\
&\quad \cdot q(\text{other states} \mid x_{t-1}, x_t, x_0) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last factor integrates to $1$ over the other states. What remains is an average over $x_t \sim q(x_t \mid x_0)$ of an inner average over $x_{t-1} \sim q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$, and the inner average of $\log(q / p_\theta)$ is by definition a KL divergence ([lecture notes](#references), lecture 5). Changing the sign, the bound splits into

$$
L(x_0) = L_T + \sum_{t=2}^{T} L_{t-1} + L_0 ,
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_T &= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_T \mid x_0) \,\|\, p(x_T)\big) , \\
L_{t-1} &= \mathbb E_{q(x_t \mid x_0)}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_{t-1} \,\|\, p_{\theta, t-1}\big) , \\
q_{t-1} &= q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) , \\
p_{\theta, t-1} &= p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) , \\
L_0 &= -\mathbb E_{q(x_1 \mid x_0)} \log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

the form of [Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020](#references), Eq. 5). The three kinds of terms behave differently.

- $L_T$ compares the end of the forward chain with the fixed prior. Neither contains a trainable parameter, so $L_T$ is a constant during training; for DDPM's schedule it is about $10^{-5}$ bits per dimension ([forward process](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process#where-it-breaks-a-chain-too-short-to-forget)).
- $L_0$ is the decoder's reconstruction term. It is not a constant: even with the family of $p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)$ fixed, its mean $\mu_\theta(x_1, 1)$ is the network's output, so $L_0$ changes with $\theta$.
- $L_{t-1}$ for $t = 2, \dots, T$ does the main work. Each compares the model's step with $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$, the forward step reversed with the image known. Both are Gaussians, as the next section shows, so every KL has a closed form instead of a noisy Monte Carlo estimate.

<details>
<summary>The decoder at t = 1 and bits per dimension</summary>

Pixels are integers $0, \dots, 255$ scaled linearly to $[-1, 1]$, so neighbouring values are $2/255$ apart. DDPM's last step gives each value the probability mass of its bin under the Gaussian of the reverse step ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 13), one factor per pixel value $i = 1, \dots, D$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1) &= \prod_{i=1}^{D} \int_{\delta_-(x_0^i)}^{\delta_+(x_0^i)} \mathcal N\big(x;\ \mu_\theta^i(x_1, 1),\ \sigma_1^2\big)\, dx , \\
\delta_\pm(x) &= x \pm \tfrac{1}{255} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with the outer edges $\delta_+(1) = +\infty$ and $\delta_-(-1) = -\infty$, so that the 256 probabilities sum to $1$. Each factor is a difference of two values of the standard normal CDF $\Phi$. With $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{\beta_1} = 0.01$ and the mean exactly on the pixel value, the bin $\pm 1/255$ spans $\pm 0.392$ standard deviations, so its probability is $2\Phi(0.392) - 1 = 0.305$, or $-\log_2 0.305 = 1.71$ bits.

Likelihoods are reported in **bits per dimension**: the bound in nats divided by $D \ln 2$ ([definition](/notes/generative-models/autoregressive-models#pixels-as-discrete-values)). For CIFAR-10, $D = 32 \cdot 32 \cdot 3 = 3072$, so $3.75$ bits per dimension mean $11{,}520$ bits per image.

</details>

## The true posterior

<PosteriorGraph />

The forward step reversed with the image known, $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)$, is the "true posterior" that each $L_{t-1}$ compares with. Bayes' theorem conditioned on $x_0$, followed by the Markov property $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$, gives

$$
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) = \frac{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\; q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)}{q(x_t \mid x_0)} .
$$

Both factors of the numerator are Gaussian in $x_{t-1}$, and the denominator does not depend on $x_{t-1}$. Take logarithms, per coordinate, and collect the terms in $x_{t-1}$; every term without $x_{t-1}$ goes into a constant $c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0)
&= -\frac{(x_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1})^2}{2\beta_t} \\
&\quad - \frac{(x_{t-1} - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0)^2}{2(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})} + c \\
&= -\tfrac12 \big(A\, x_{t-1}^2 - 2 B\, x_{t-1}\big) + c ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where expanding the squares gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}} , \\
B &= \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t}\, x_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Completing the square, $A x^2 - 2 B x = A (x - B/A)^2 - B^2/A$, so the posterior is Gaussian with variance $1/A$ and mean $B/A$. Over a common denominator, and with $\alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1} = \bar\alpha_t$ and $\alpha_t + \beta_t = 1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \frac{\alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t}{\beta_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})} \\
&= \frac{\alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t + \beta_t}{\beta_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})} \\
&= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\beta_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Inverting $A$ gives the variance, and multiplying $B$ by $1/A$ term by term gives the mean:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t} \cdot \frac{\beta_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} &= \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\, (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} , \\
\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}} \cdot \frac{\beta_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} &= \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Together:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) &= \mathcal N\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0),\; \tilde\beta_t I\big) , \\
\tilde\beta_t &= \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \beta_t , \\
\tilde\mu_t &= \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \beta_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\, (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_t .
\end{aligned}
}
$$

This is DDPM's Eq. 6–7, and the same computation as the [normal–normal conjugate update](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#normal-normal-an-unknown-mean): the precisions add, and the mean is a precision-weighted average. **Toy example:** $\beta_1 = 0.1$ and $\beta_2 = 0.2$, so $\bar\alpha_1 = 0.9$ and $\bar\alpha_2 = 0.72$. Then $q(x_1 \mid x_2, x_0)$ has variance $\tilde\beta_2 = 0.1/0.28 \cdot 0.2 = 0.0714$ and mean $0.678\, x_0 + 0.319\, x_2$; the Code section recovers these numbers by simulation. At $t = 1$, $\bar\alpha_0 = 1$ gives $\tilde\beta_1 = 0$ and $\tilde\mu_1 = x_0$: given the image, there is nothing left to guess, which is why that step is handled by the decoder term $L_0$.

The posterior needs $x_0$, and at generation time there is no image. The network must imitate it from $x_t$ alone.

## Matching two Gaussians: KL becomes L2

DDPM does not learn the variance of the reverse step: $\sigma_t^2$ is fixed in advance. With $p_\theta = \mathcal N(\mu_\theta, \sigma_t^2 I)$, the [KL divergence between two 1-D Gaussians](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#what-the-gradients-are), summed over the $d$ coordinates, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta\big)
&= \frac{\lVert \tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta \rVert^2}{2\sigma_t^2} + C_t , \\
C_t &= \frac d2 \Big(\frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2} - 1 - \ln \frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2}\Big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

$C_t$ does not depend on $\theta$, and it is $0$ when $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t$. So each $L_{t-1}$ is a squared error between means, plus a constant ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 8).

<EqualVarianceKL />

Which $\sigma_t^2$? Ho, Jain and Abbeel tried $\beta_t$ and $\tilde\beta_t$ and found similar results (§3.2). They are the right variance in two extreme cases. If the data are a single point, the true reverse step is the posterior itself, with variance $\tilde\beta_t$. If the data are $\mathcal N(0, I)$, every $x_t$ is $\mathcal N(0, I)$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(x_{t-1}, x_t) = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, I$, so the [Gaussian conditional](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals) has variance $1 - \alpha_t = \beta_t$.

What does the network learn? The target $\tilde\mu_t$ depends on $x_0$, which it does not see, and many images are compatible with the same $x_t$. Since the [conditional mean minimizes the expected squared error](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation#the-best-predictor), the optimum is the average of $\tilde\mu_t$ over the images compatible with $x_t$, and by the tower property that is the mean of the true reverse step:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_\theta^*(x_t, t) &= \mathbb E\big[\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) \mid x_t\big] \\
&= \mathbb E[x_{t-1} \mid x_t] .
\end{aligned}
$$

At this optimum the KL terms are not zero. What remains of the squared error is the variance of $\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0)$ given $x_t$. Since $\tilde\mu_t$ is linear in $x_0$, that is the square of its coefficient of $x_0$ times $\operatorname{Var}(x_0 \mid x_t)$, summed over pixels: it is zero only when $x_t$ determines the image.

## From means to noise

The network could output $\mu_\theta$ directly. The [lecture notes](#references) take a route through the image first. Since $\tilde\mu_t$ is linear in $x_0$ with coefficient $c_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \beta_t / (1 - \bar\alpha_t)$, let a network $\hat x_\theta(x_t, t)$ guess the image and plug the guess into the posterior mean, $\mu_\theta = \tilde\mu_t(x_t, \hat x_\theta)$. Then the means differ only through the images:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) - \mu_\theta &= c_t\, \big(x_0 - \hat x_\theta(x_t, t)\big) , \\
L_{t-1} &= \frac{c_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\, \mathbb E\, \lVert x_0 - \hat x_\theta(x_t, t) \rVert^2 + C_t .
\end{aligned}
$$

Training is regression of the clean image on the noisy one: a **denoiser**.

DDPM goes one step further and predicts the noise ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §3.2). The one-draw jump $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon$ can be solved for the image:

$$
x_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} \big(x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon\big) .
$$

Substitute this into $\tilde\mu_t$ and use $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}} / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$, from $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}$. The coefficient of $x_t$ collects two terms, which add up by $\beta_t + \alpha_t = 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)} + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}
&= \frac{\beta_t + \alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)} \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The coefficient of $\varepsilon$ is $-\beta_t \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} / \big(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_t)\big) = -\beta_t / \big(\sqrt{\alpha_t} \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\big)$. Hence

$$
\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon\Big) .
$$

The network sees $x_t$ and $t$ but not $\varepsilon$, so DDPM gives the model's mean the same form and lets a network $\varepsilon_\theta$ predict the missing noise (Eq. 11):

$$
\boxed{\;\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big)\;}
$$

In the difference $\tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta$ the $x_t$ terms cancel, leaving $\beta_t (\varepsilon_\theta - \varepsilon) / (\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t})$. Squaring and dividing by $2\sigma_t^2$ gives Eq. 12:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{t-1} - C_t &= w_t\, \mathbb E_{x_0, \varepsilon} \big\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\rVert^2 , \\
w_t &= \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\, \alpha_t\, (1 - \bar\alpha_t)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The three parameterizations describe the same model with the same bound. Define the image guess from the noise guess by the same solved jump, $\hat x_\theta = (x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon_\theta) / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$; subtracting it from the expression for $x_0$ gives $x_0 - \hat x_\theta = -\sqrt{(1 - \bar\alpha_t)/\bar\alpha_t}\, (\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta)$, and $c_t^2 (1 - \bar\alpha_t)/\bar\alpha_t = 2\sigma_t^2 w_t$. They differ in what the network finds easy to output, and in what the unweighted losses below emphasize. Ho, Jain and Abbeel found that predicting $x_0$ gave worse samples early in their experiments, and that predicting $\tilde\mu$ trained well only with the true weights (§3.2, §4.2). The optimal noise predictor is $\varepsilon_\theta^* = \mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t]$, the average noise consistent with $x_t$. Up to the factor $-\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ it is the score of $q(x_t)$, the gradient that appeared in the small-step mean of the reverse process ([score matching](/notes/generative-models/score-matching#the-same-model-as-ddpm)).

## Final simplification: a random timestep per sample

The bound now reads

$$
L(x_0) = \text{const} + \sum_{t=2}^{T} w_t\, \mathbb E_\varepsilon \lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \rVert^2 + L_0 .
$$

Summing all $T - 1$ terms for every image would cost $T - 1$ network evaluations per image. Instead we draw one timestep per image. For any $f$, $\sum_{t=2}^{T} f(t) = (T - 1)\, \mathbb E_{t \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{2, \dots, T\}} f(t)$, so one uniform $t$ gives an unbiased estimate of the sum up to a constant factor, which does not move the minimizer. Because $x_t$ comes from $x_0$ in one draw, no chain is ever simulated during training.

Ho, Jain and Abbeel then drop the weight (Eq. 14):

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{\text{simple}}(\theta) &= \mathbb E_{t, x_0, \varepsilon} \big\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \big\rVert^2 , \\
x_t &= \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with $t$ uniform on $\{1, \dots, T\}$. The $t = 1$ term stands in for $L_0$: the decoder's bin probability approximated by the Gaussian density times the bin width, ignoring $\sigma_1^2$ and the edge bins (§3.4).

Dropping $w_t$ changes the objective. With DDPM's linear schedule and $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$, the weight simplifies to $w_t = \beta_t / (2\alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_t))$. It is $0.273$ at $t = 2$, falls to a minimum of $0.0050$ at $t = 349$ and ends at $0.0102$ at $t = T$: the bound puts $55$ times more weight on the nearly clean step $t = 2$ than at the minimum. $L_{\text{simple}}$ weights every step equally, so relative to the bound it down-weights the small-$t$ steps, where the noise is tiny and easy to remove, and spends the network's capacity on the harder, noisier ones. With unlimited capacity the weights would not matter: each step's term is minimized separately by $\varepsilon_\theta^* = \mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t]$, whatever its positive weight. They matter because one network serves all steps and trades errors at one step against another, so minimizing $L_{\text{simple}}$ no longer minimizes the bound; Ho, Jain and Abbeel call it a weighted variational bound (§3.4). On CIFAR-10 it gave much better samples, FID $3.17$ against $13.51$ for the true bound, and a slightly worse likelihood, $3.75$ against $3.70$ bits per dimension (their Table 1).

<LossWeight />

The training loop is DDPM's Algorithm 1:

1. Draw an image $x_0$ from the data.
2. Draw a timestep $t$ uniformly from $\{1, \dots, T\}$ and noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$.
3. Form $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon$.
4. Take a gradient step on $\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \rVert^2$, and repeat until converged.

<TrainingStep />

## Sampling: running the chain backwards

Generation does run the chain. DDPM's Algorithm 2 samples each reverse step $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_t, t), \sigma_t^2 I)$ with the noise form of the mean:

1. Draw $x_T \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$.
2. For $t = T, \dots, 1$, draw $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ if $t > 1$, else set $z = 0$, and compute

$$
x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big) + \sigma_t z .
$$

3. Return $x_0$.

Each step removes the network's estimate of the noise, rescales, and adds a little fresh noise of variance $\sigma_t^2$, either $\beta_t$ or $\tilde\beta_t$.

The last step, with $z = 0$, returns the decoder's mean $\mu_\theta(x_1, 1)$, not a draw from $p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)$; Ho, Jain and Abbeel display this mean (§3.3). With $\sigma_1^2 = \tilde\beta_1 = 0$ the two coincide. With $\sigma_1^2 = \beta_1$ an exact sample needs the noise: it adds $\sigma_1 z$, or, for the discrete decoder, draws each pixel's bin with the probabilities of the folded block above. The mean is a cleaner picture, at the cost of no longer being a sample of the model.

<details>
<summary>How far an exact last step moves a pixel</summary>

Take a mean exactly on a pixel value and $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{\beta_1} = 0.01$. The bins have half-width $1/255 = 0.39\,\sigma_1$, so the draw stays in the mean's bin with probability $2\Phi(0.392) - 1 = 0.305$, moves one level up or down with probability $2\big(\Phi(1.176) - \Phi(0.392)\big) = 0.456$, and moves further with probability $0.239$.

</details>

Generation costs $T = 1000$ network evaluations, one per step, against one for training on an image; this is the main practical cost of DDPM. Samplers that reuse the trained network with far fewer steps are in the [latent diffusion note](/notes/generative-models/latent-diffusion#fewer-steps).

## Where it breaks: too few, too large steps

The whole construction rests on the reverse step being nearly Gaussian, which holds only for small $\beta_t$. Take data that are two points, $x_0 \in \{-1, +1\}$ with equal weights. For this data the optimal noise predictor is known exactly: $\mathbb E[x_0 \mid x_t] = \tanh\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_t / (1 - \bar\alpha_t)\big)$, from Bayes' theorem with two Gaussian likelihoods, and $\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t]$ follows from the one-draw jump. Run Algorithm 2 with this perfect network and $\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t$, for two schedules that end at the same $\bar\alpha_T = 4.0 \times 10^{-5}$:

| chain | samples within $0.05$ of $\pm 1$ | samples with $\lvert x \rvert < 0.5$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $T = 1000$, DDPM's linear schedule | $1.0$ | $0.0$ |
| $T = 10$, constant $\beta = 0.636$ | $0.03$ | $0.47$ |

With a thousand small steps the sampler reproduces the data. With ten large steps it fails although every step has the exact optimal mean $\mathbb E[x_{t-1} \mid x_t]$. The true reverse step averages the two posteriors over the image, by the law of total probability:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \textstyle\sum_{x_0 = \pm 1} {} & P(x_0 \mid x_t) \\
& \cdot q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The two Gaussians have the same variance $\tilde\beta_t$, and their means differ by $2\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \beta_t / (1 - \bar\alpha_t)$, the coefficient of $x_0$ in $\tilde\mu_t$ times the distance between the images. The mixture is far from one Gaussian only where both weights $P(x_0 \mid x_t)$ are substantial and this gap is large next to the standard deviation $\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}$. In the thousand-step chain the two never meet. In the ten-step chain they do at the last steps. At $t = 1$, where $\tilde\beta_1 = 0$, the true step goes to $-1$ or $+1$, while the sampler returns their weighted average, which lies between $-0.8$ and $0.8$ for $74\%$ of the $x_1$ of the forward process. Almost half the samples end near $0$, where the data have none.

<details>
<summary>The gap in numbers</summary>

Divided by the standard deviation, the gap is

$$
r_t = \frac{2\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}\, \beta_t}}{\sqrt{(1 - \bar\alpha_t)(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}} ,
$$

which shrinks like $\sqrt{\beta_t}$ at fixed noise levels. In the thousand-step chain, both weights first exceed $0.1$ for at least $1\%$ of the $x_t$ at $t = 110$, where $r_t = 0.74$, and $r_t$ only falls after that. The early steps, where $r_t$ is large ($148$ at $t = 2$), see an $x_t$ that still identifies the image, so one weight is close to $1$. In the ten-step chain $r_2 = 1.29$ with almost every $x_2$ ambiguous, and $r_1$ is infinite.

</details>

## The denoising network

The derivation treats $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ as a function of two arguments. In practice it is one network for all steps, a U-Net, that receives the timestep as an embedding added inside every block.

<DenoiserFlow />

### One network for all timesteps

Neighbouring steps solve almost the same problem, so DDPM shares the parameters across time instead of training a thousand networks, and tells the network which step it is at (§4). The same input calls for different outputs at different noise levels: the noise in a given $x_t$ is a small part of it when $t$ is small and almost all of it when $t$ is large.

<details>
<summary>An example: one input, two noise levels</summary>

For 1-D data $\mathcal N(0, 0.5^2)$, $\varepsilon$ and $x_t$ are jointly Gaussian with covariance $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$, so the [Gaussian conditional mean](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals) is

$$
\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t] = \frac{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}{0.25\, \bar\alpha_t + 1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, x_t .
$$

With the linear schedule and $x_t = 1$ it is $0.17$ at $t = 10$ ($\bar\alpha_t = 0.998$), where $x_t$ is mostly image, and $1.02$ at $t = 500$ ($\bar\alpha_t = 0.079$), where $x_t$ is mostly noise.

</details>

### A U-Net

The output has the shape of the input, one noise value per pixel: a dense prediction problem that needs context from far away and precision at the pixel itself. A **U-Net**, built in the [segmentation note](/notes/deep-learning/semantic-segmentation#u-net-an-encoder-a-decoder-and-skip-connections) of the Deep Learning course, provides both: an encoder halves the resolution level by level, a decoder doubles it back, and skip connections bring the encoder's features of each size to the decoder. DDPM's network follows the PixelCNN++ backbone, a U-Net of Wide ResNet blocks, with group normalization and self-attention at the $16 \times 16$ resolution (§4 and Appendix B).

### Telling the network the timestep

DDPM encodes $t$ the way a Transformer encodes a position ([positional encodings](/notes/nlp/positional-encoding#sinusoidal-encoding)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
e(t)_{2i} &= \sin(\omega_i t) , \\
e(t)_{2i+1} &= \cos(\omega_i t) , \\
\omega_i &= 10000^{-2i/d} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

for $i = 0, \dots, d/2 - 1$.

A small MLP turns the code into a feature vector $m(t)$, and every residual block $k$ adds its own linear projection $A_k\, m(t)$ to its feature maps, one bias per channel. A raw number $t$ would give the MLP one input running up to $1000$, from which it would have to build every function of $t$ it needs. The sinusoidal code supplies them ready-made: its components turn at rates from $\omega_0 = 1$ down to $\omega_{d/2-1} \approx 10^{-4}$ radians per step, periods from about $6$ to about $60{,}000$ steps, so linear combinations of it can follow both fast and slow changes in $t$. Alternatives set a scale and a shift of each channel after group normalization from the embedding ([Murphy, 2023](#references), §25.2), and text enters through [cross-attention](/notes/generative-models/latent-diffusion#conditioning-through-cross-attention).

### Why the pixels are scaled to [−1, 1] \{#why-the-pixels-are-scaled\}

DDPM maps pixel values $0, \dots, 255$ linearly to $[-1, 1]$ (§3.3). The chain removes the image only by shrinking it, and with $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T} = 0.0064$ the leftover is negligible only for values of order $1$ ([forward process](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process#where-it-breaks-a-chain-too-short-to-forget)). By the variance recursion $\operatorname{Var}(x_t) = \bar\alpha_t \operatorname{Var}(x_0) + 1 - \bar\alpha_t$, every step then sees inputs of similar size, and the decoder's bins of width $2/255$ end at $\pm 1$.

To display an image, $x$ is clipped to $[-1, 1]$ and mapped back by $255\,(x + 1)/2$, because noisy states leave the interval routinely.

<details>
<summary>How often, and what goes wrong without the clip</summary>

A pixel with $x_0 = 0.9$ at a step with $\bar\alpha_t = 0.5$ has $x_t \sim \mathcal N(0.64,\ 0.5)$, which falls outside $[-1, 1]$ with probability $0.31$. Without the clipping, the conversion to 8-bit integers wraps such values around into wrong colours.

</details>

## Training in practice: averaged weights and clipped gradients

**An exponential moving average of the weights.** Keep a second copy $\bar\theta$ of the parameters and update it after every optimizer step:

$$
\bar\theta \leftarrow \mu\, \bar\theta + (1 - \mu)\, \theta .
$$

Unrolled over $n$ steps, $\bar\theta$ is a weighted average of the past iterates with weights $(1 - \mu)\, \mu^{n-k}$, plus $\mu^n$ times its starting value: roughly an average over the last $1/(1 - \mu)$ steps, which carry the weight $1 - \mu^{1/(1-\mu)} \approx 1 - 1/e = 0.63$. Training continues with $\theta$; samples are drawn with $\bar\theta$. Every example brings its own image, timestep and noise, so the iterates jitter around a good region. By the triangle inequality, the distance from an average to any point is at most the average distance, so the average lies no farther from the centre than a typical iterate, and closer when their deviations point in different directions. DDPM uses $\mu = 0.9999$ (Appendix B), a window of about $10{,}000$ steps, and [Nichol and Dhariwal (2021)](#references) found $0.9999$ and $0.99995$ best in a sweep.

<details>
<summary>When the average becomes usable</summary>

The starting value keeps the weight $\mu^n$: $0.9999^{10\,000} = 0.37$ and $0.9999^{50\,000} = 0.0067$, so the average is worth using only after training has run several times longer than its window.

</details>

**Gradient clipping.** Before each step, rescale the gradient $g$ when its norm exceeds a threshold $c$, $g \leftarrow g \cdot \min(1, c / \lVert g \rVert)$ ([training background](/notes/generative-models/training-background#common-remedies)). The direction is kept and only rare large gradients are shortened. The loss of a batch varies with the timesteps it happens to contain, and clipping keeps an unusual batch from moving the weights far.

## What you should remember

- The bound splits into $L_T$ (constant), $L_{t-1} = \mathbb E\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) \,\|\, p_\theta)$ and the decoder term $L_0$, which depends on $\theta$.
- Given $x_0$ the reversed chain is Markov, and $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal N(\tilde\mu_t, \tilde\beta_t I)$ by Bayes and completing the square.
- With fixed variances each KL is $\lVert \tilde\mu_t - \mu_\theta \rVert^2 / (2\sigma_t^2)$ plus a constant.
- $\mu_\theta = \big(x_t - \beta_t \varepsilon_\theta / \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\big) / \sqrt{\alpha_t}$ turns it into $w_t \lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta \rVert^2$.
- $L_{\text{simple}}$ sets $w_t = 1$: better samples, a slightly worse likelihood; it trades the steps off differently from the bound.
- Training jumps straight to $x_t$; sampling takes $T$ steps, which must be small for the Gaussian step to hold.

## Code

The posterior of the toy example checked by simulation, the weights of the bound for DDPM's schedule, and Algorithm 2 with the exact noise predictor for two-point data, with many small steps and with ten large ones. It uses NumPy and runs in a few seconds.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. the posterior q(x1 | x2, x0) for beta1 = 0.1, beta2 = 0.2, by regression on simulated pairs
b1, b2 = 0.1, 0.2
ab1, ab2 = 1 - b1, (1 - b1) * (1 - b2)
x0 = 1.0
x1 = np.sqrt(ab1) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - ab1) * rng.standard_normal(1_000_000)
x2 = np.sqrt(1 - b2) * x1 + np.sqrt(b2) * rng.standard_normal(x1.size)
slope, icpt = np.polyfit(x2, x1, 1)                      # for fixed x0, E[x1 | x2] is linear in x2
resid = (x1 - slope * x2 - icpt).var()
print(np.round([slope, icpt, resid], 4))                  # simulated
print(np.round([np.sqrt(1 - b2) * (1 - ab1) / (1 - ab2),  # formula: coefficient of x_t,
                np.sqrt(ab1) * b2 / (1 - ab2) * x0,       # coefficient of x0 times x0,
                (1 - ab1) / (1 - ab2) * b2], 4))          # and the variance tilde beta
# [0.3202 0.6771 0.0715]
# [0.3194 0.6776 0.0714]

# 2. the bound's weight on ||eps - eps_theta||^2 for DDPM's linear schedule, sigma_t^2 = beta_t
T = 1000
beta = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T); alpha = 1 - beta; abar = np.cumprod(alpha)
w = beta / (2 * alpha * (1 - abar))                       # beta^2 / (2 sigma^2 alpha (1 - abar)) with sigma^2 = beta
t_min = np.argmin(w[1:]) + 2                              # steps t = 2..T
print(round(w[1], 4), round(w.min(), 4), t_min, round(w[-1], 4), round(w[1] / w.min(), 1))
# 0.2727 0.005 349 0.0102 54.9

# 3. sampling (Algorithm 2) with the exact optimal noise predictor for data x0 in {-1, +1}
def sample(beta, n=20_000):
    alpha = 1 - beta; abar = np.cumprod(alpha)
    abar_prev = np.concatenate([[1.0], abar[:-1]])
    x = rng.standard_normal(n)
    for t in range(len(beta) - 1, -1, -1):
        x0_hat = np.tanh(np.sqrt(abar[t]) * x / (1 - abar[t]))          # E[x0 | x_t] for two equal points
        eps = (x - np.sqrt(abar[t]) * x0_hat) / np.sqrt(1 - abar[t])    # E[eps | x_t]
        mean = (x - beta[t] / np.sqrt(1 - abar[t]) * eps) / np.sqrt(alpha[t])
        var = (1 - abar_prev[t]) / (1 - abar[t]) * beta[t]              # sigma_t^2 = tilde beta_t
        x = mean + np.sqrt(var) * rng.standard_normal(n) if t > 0 else mean
    return x

few = np.full(10, 1 - abar[-1] ** 0.1)                    # 10 steps with the same abar_T as the 1000-step chain
for b in (beta, few):
    x = sample(b)
    print(len(b), round(np.prod(1 - b), 7), round(np.mean(np.abs(np.abs(x) - 1) < 0.05), 3), round(np.mean(np.abs(x) < 0.5), 3))
# 1000 4.04e-05 1.0 0.0
# 10 4.04e-05 0.03 0.47
```

The notebook has no saved outputs, so it documents procedures rather than results.

- The 2015 model: one small network for all steps predicts a mean and a log-variance and trains on the bound in bits (Adam at $10^{-3}$, batches of 128, 5001 passes, gradient norm clipped at $1$). Its `ConditionalLinear` layers multiply each linear output by a learned vector per step, $\gamma_t \odot (W h + b)$, instead of using a sinusoidal code.
- Mirrored timesteps: both losses draw half a batch of steps $t$ and add their mirrors `n_steps - t - 1`. Each step alone is still uniform, so the estimate stays unbiased; for a per-step loss monotone in $t$ the two halves are negatively correlated, which lowers the variance.
- DDPM: the same layers predict the noise and train on `noise_estimation_loss`, the unweighted $L_{\text{simple}}$, for 1000 passes. `p_sample_ddpm` uses $\sigma_t = \sqrt{\beta_t}$ and also adds noise at the last step, where Algorithm 2 adds none.
- Averaged weights: the loop uses $\mu = 0.9$, a window of about 10 updates, and `apply_shadow` is never called, so sampling uses the raw weights.
