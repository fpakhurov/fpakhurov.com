A DCGAN with a [Wasserstein critic](/notes/generative-models/wgan) trains well on $64 \times 64$ faces. Ask the same recipe for $1024 \times 1024$ faces, or for all thousand classes of ImageNet, and it fails in three different ways.

At high resolution the discriminator wins too easily: a large image carries many details by which a fake can be told apart ([Karras et al., 2018](#references), Section 1), and the generator's gradient suffers as in the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#step-2-substitute-the-optimal-discriminator-and-recognize-js). Large images also force small minibatches. On ImageNet, convolutional GANs drew texture classes such as sky or ocean well and failed on classes with consistent geometry: dogs with realistic fur and no clearly separated feet ([Zhang et al., 2019](#references)). And a thousand classes are a thousand groups of modes to cover without [collapsing](/notes/generative-models/gans#mode-collapse-and-how-to-fight-it) onto a few.

Between 2017 and 2019 three papers answered one problem each. Their results are reported in [FID](/notes/generative-models/fid) and [Inception Score](/notes/generative-models/inception-score), lower and higher is better respectively.

## ProGAN: growing the networks

ProGAN's answer to the resolution problem is a curriculum ([Karras et al., 2018](#references)). Small images carry less information and have fewer modes, so start there and add detail later. Both networks begin at $4 \times 4$. Each time the resolution doubles, a block of layers is added to the end of $G$ and its mirror image to the start of $D$, and all existing layers keep training. Eight doublings take the networks to $1024 \times 1024$. The networks first learn the large-scale structure of a face and then finer and finer detail, and most iterations run at low resolution, where they are cheap.

### Fade-in of new layers

A new block starts with random weights; switching it on at once would shock the trained layers. ProGAN fades it in. Let $a_r$ be the generator's features at resolution $r$, $\mathrm{up}$ nearest-neighbour upsampling by 2, $\mathrm{toRGB}$ a $1 \times 1$ convolution from features to colours, and $\mathrm{block}_{2r}$ the new convolutions. During the transition the output image is

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{2r} ={}& (1 - \alpha)\,\mathrm{up}\big(\mathrm{toRGB}_r(a_r)\big) \\
&+ \alpha\,\mathrm{toRGB}_{2r}\big(\mathrm{block}_{2r}(\mathrm{up}(a_r))\big),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\alpha$ rising linearly from 0 to 1. At $\alpha = 0$ the network is the old one with its output upsampled; at $\alpha = 0.25$ three quarters of each pixel still come from the old path. The discriminator mirrors this with average pooling and $1 \times 1$ $\mathrm{fromRGB}$ layers, and real images are downscaled and blended between the two resolutions in the same way.

### Minibatch standard deviation

The [minibatch discrimination](/notes/generative-models/gans#mode-collapse-and-how-to-fight-it) of the GAN note lets the discriminator see batch statistics through a learned tensor. ProGAN replaces it with a layer that has no parameters. Near the end of $D$ it computes the standard deviation of every feature at every position over the minibatch, averages these into one number, and appends that number as an extra constant feature map.

A toy example, with one feature at one position and a batch of four. Real images give $(1, -1, 2, 0)$, with standard deviation $1.118$. A collapsed generator gives $(1.0, 1.1, 0.9, 1.0)$, with standard deviation $0.071$. The extra channel carries $1.118$ for the real batch and $0.071$ for the fake one, and a single weight on it separates them.

### Equalized learning rate

ProGAN draws every weight from $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ and multiplies it at every forward pass by the per-layer constant of [He initialization](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#initialization-that-keeps-the-factor-at-one):

$$
\hat w = \sqrt{2 / n_{\text{in}}}\;w ,
$$

where $n_{\text{in}}$ is the fan-in of the layer. At initialization this is He initialization; the difference shows during training. Adam divides each update by a running estimate of the gradient's scale, so it moves every stored parameter by roughly the learning rate per step, whatever that parameter's own scale. Stored weights of different scales then change at different relative speeds.

A worked example with ProGAN's learning rate $10^{-3}$:

| Layer | $n_{\text{in}}$ | He scale | Step relative to scale |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $3 \times 3$ convolution, 512 channels | 4608 | 0.0208 | 4.8% |
| $1 \times 1$ $\mathrm{fromRGB}$, 3 channels | 3 | 0.816 | 0.12% |

With He-initialized stored weights, the first layer changes 39 times faster relative to its scale than the second. With the scaling at run time every stored weight has scale 1, and every layer changes by about $0.1\%$ per step: equal learning speed, hence the name.

### Pixelwise feature normalization

The ProGAN authors observed signal magnitudes escalating in both networks as they compete ([Karras et al., 2018](#references), Section 4.2). Instead of batch normalization, the ProGAN generator normalizes the feature vector at each pixel after every convolution:

$$
b_{x,y} = \frac{a_{x,y}}{\sqrt{\frac1C\sum_{j=1}^{C}\big(a^{j}_{x,y}\big)^2 + \varepsilon}} ,
$$

where $a_{x,y} \in \mathbb R^C$ holds the $C$ channels at pixel $(x, y)$ and $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$. For $a = (3, 4)$, the mean square is $12.5$ and $b = (0.849, 1.131)$: the root mean square of the channels becomes 1. The layer has no parameters and does not mix examples.

The loss is [WGAN-GP](/notes/generative-models/wgan#wgan-gp-gradient-penalty) with one critic step per generator step and a small extra term that keeps the critic's output near zero; samples come from a moving average of the generator's weights. In the authors' ablation on faces at $128 \times 128$, progressive growing alone more than halved the distance between patch statistics of real and generated images, from 9.28 to 4.28 in their sliced Wasserstein measure ([Karras et al., 2018](#references), Table 1). It also produced $1024 \times 1024$ faces, trained on CelebA-HQ, a set of 30,000 curated celebrity photos built for the purpose.

## SAGAN: self-attention for long-range structure

Faces are aligned and of one kind. ImageNet has many classes, objects anywhere in the frame, and structure that spans the whole image.

### Why convolutions miss long-range structure

A $3 \times 3$ convolution mixes each position with its immediate neighbours. A stack of $L$ such layers sees a $(2L + 1) \times (2L + 1)$ window, its [receptive field](/notes/deep-learning/convolution#how-far-a-unit-sees-the-receptive-field), so an output depends on an input 16 positions away only after 16 layers. A network can relate distant regions, but only through many layers that must coordinate, which a small model may not represent and an optimizer may not find. Larger kernels would help at the cost of the efficiency of convolutions. Self-attention connects every pair of positions in one layer.

### The self-attention block

SAGAN adds one attention block to both networks ([Zhang et al., 2019](#references)). Here $x$ is the feature map that enters the block, viewed as $N = HW$ vectors $x_i \in \mathbb R^C$, one per position. Three $1 \times 1$ convolutions $W_f, W_g, W_h \in \mathbb R^{\bar C \times C}$ act on each position separately and give $f(x_i) = W_f x_i$, $g(x_i) = W_g x_i$ and $h(x_i) = W_h x_i$. For every pair of positions the block computes a score and an attention weight:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_{ij} &= f(x_i)^\top g(x_j), \\
\beta_{j,i} &= \frac{\exp(s_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\exp(s_{kj})} .
\end{aligned}
$$

$\beta_{j,i}$ is how much position $j$ attends to position $i$; for each $j$ the weights sum to 1. The output at $j$ is the weighted sum of the values $h(x_i)$, mapped back to $C$ channels by a fourth $1 \times 1$ convolution $W_v$ and added to the input with a learned scalar $\gamma$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
o_j &= W_v \sum_{i=1}^{N} \beta_{j,i}\, h(x_i), \\
y_j &= \gamma\, o_j + x_j .
\end{aligned}
$$

<SelfAttentionFlow />

A toy example with three positions and $\bar C = 1$. Let $f(x) = (2, 0, -1)$ over the positions and take position $j = 1$ with $g(x_1) = 1$. The scores are $(2, 0, -1)$, and the softmax gives $\beta_{1,\cdot} = (0.844, 0.114, 0.042)$. With values $h(x) = (1, 3, 5)$, position 1 collects $0.844 \cdot 1 + 0.114 \cdot 3 + 0.042 \cdot 5 = 1.396$, which $W_v$ maps to $o_1$. While $\gamma = 0$, $y_1 = x_1$ whatever $o_1$ is.

$\gamma$ starts at 0, so at initialization the block is the identity. The network first learns from local evidence, which is easier, and lets in distant evidence as $\gamma$ grows. The three maps are narrow, $\bar C = C/8$, to save memory. In the terms of the [attention note](/notes/nlp/attention#scaled-dot-product-attention), $g(x_j)$ is the query of position $j$ and $f(x_i)$, $h(x_i)$ the key and value of position $i$. SAGAN's scores are not divided by $\sqrt{\bar C}$, and there is a single head.

The attention map has $N^2$ entries per image, $1{,}048{,}576$ on a $32 \times 32$ feature map, so the block sits at one moderate resolution. The learned maps follow object shapes and similar textures instead of fixed neighbourhoods, which is the long-range structure convolutions missed.

### Spectral normalization in both networks, and two learning rates

[Spectral normalization](/notes/generative-models/wgan#sn-gan-spectral-normalization) keeps a discriminator Lipschitz. SAGAN applies it to every layer of the generator too. There it is a stabilizer, with no Lipschitz requirement behind it. After the division every linear layer satisfies $\lVert \hat W h\rVert \le \lVert h\rVert$, so no layer can amplify its input however large the stored weights grow; the SAGAN authors credit it with preventing escalating parameter magnitudes and unusual gradients ([Zhang et al., 2019](#references), Section 4.1). With spectral normalization in both networks, one discriminator step per generator step was enough.

A regularized discriminator also learns slowly, which is why WGAN-GP and SN-GAN take several discriminator steps per generator step. SAGAN gives the two networks different learning rates instead, the **two time-scale update rule** (TTUR) of [Heusel et al. (2017)](#references): $4 \cdot 10^{-4}$ for $D$ and $10^{-4}$ for $G$, one step each. One faster step is cheaper than five slow ones.

### The hinge loss

SAGAN trains with the **hinge** version of the adversarial loss:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_D ={}& \mathbb E_{x\sim P}\big[\max(0,\, 1 - D(x))\big] \\
&+ \mathbb E_{z}\big[\max(0,\, 1 + D(G(z)))\big], \\
\mathcal L_G ={}& -\mathbb E_{z}\big[D(G(z))\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

$D$ outputs a real number and is asked to score real images at least 1 and fakes at most $-1$; beyond these margins an example contributes nothing. The generator's loss is the [WGAN](/notes/generative-models/wgan#wgan-losses) one. In the class-conditional version $D$ also receives the label.

A toy example. Real scores $(1.5, 0.4)$ and fake scores $(-2, 0.3)$ give

$$
\mathcal L_D = \tfrac12(0 + 0.6) + \tfrac12(0 + 1.3) = 0.95 .
$$

Only the second real image and the second fake are inside their margins, so only they produce gradients for $D$. The generator's loss is $-\tfrac12(-2 + 0.3) = 0.85$. Compared with the WGAN critic loss, the hinge stops pushing scores apart once they clear the margin. The margin of 1 means something only because spectral normalization fixes the scale of $D$.

Together these changes took class-conditional ImageNet at $128 \times 128$ from an FID of 27.62 to 18.65, with the largest gains on classes with geometric structure, such as goldfish and Saint Bernard ([Zhang et al., 2019](#references)).

## BigGAN: the same recipe at scale

BigGAN starts from SAGAN: its residual networks with one attention block, the hinge loss, spectral normalization in both networks, two learning rates ([Brock, Donahue and Simonyan, 2019](#references)). Then it scales. Eight times the batch, 2048 instead of 256, raised the Inception Score by 46%; the authors conjecture that larger batches cover more modes and give both networks better gradients. Layers 50% wider added more. Progressive growing turned out to be unnecessary, even at $512 \times 512$.

### Class conditioning: conditional batch norm and projection

**In the generator.** A [batch-norm layer](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#batch-normalization-in-training-and-in-evaluation) standardizes each channel and applies a learned scale and shift. **Conditional batch norm** makes the scale and shift functions of a conditioning vector $u$ ([de Vries et al., 2017](#references)):

$$
\mathrm{CBN}(h_k; u) = \big(1 + a_k^\top u\big)\,\frac{h_k - \mu_k}{\sqrt{\sigma_k^2 + \varepsilon}} + b_k^\top u ,
$$

for channel $k$ with batch statistics $\mu_k$, $\sigma_k^2$ and learned vectors $a_k$, $b_k$. The label then changes the scale and shift of every channel of every layer, so it acts on the whole generator. BigGAN uses one **shared embedding** $e(c)$ of the class $c$ for all layers, projected linearly to each layer's gains and biases.

**In the discriminator.** The [conditional GAN](/notes/generative-models/gans#conditional-gan) feeds the label to $D$ by concatenation. The **projection discriminator** ([Miyato and Koyama, 2018](#references)) derives a different form from the [optimal discriminator](/notes/generative-models/gans#step-1-the-optimal-discriminator-for-a-fixed-generator), whose logit is the log density ratio. On pairs $(x, c)$ it splits by the product rule $p(x, c) = p(c \mid x)\,p(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln\frac{p_{\text{data}}(x, c)}{p_g(x, c)} ={}& \ln\frac{p_{\text{data}}(c \mid x)}{p_g(c \mid x)} \\
&+ \ln\frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_g(x)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Let both class posteriors be softmax classifiers on a shared feature vector $\phi(x)$, $\ln p(c \mid x) = v_c^\top\phi(x) - \ln Z(x)$. The first term becomes $(v_c^{\text{data}} - v_c^{g})^\top\phi(x)$ plus a term in $x$ alone. Everything in $x$ alone is absorbed into one scalar function $\psi$, and the difference of class vectors becomes a learned embedding $e(c)$:

$$
D(x, c) = \psi\big(\phi(x)\big) + e(c)^\top\phi(x) .
$$

The discriminator is an unconditional realism score plus an inner product that measures how well the image features match the class.

### A hierarchical latent

In a DCGAN the latent enters only the first layer. BigGAN splits $z$ into chunks of 20 and concatenates each with the shared class embedding. At $128 \times 128$, $z \in \mathbb R^{120}$ gives six chunks: the first enters the input linear layer, which makes the $4 \times 4$ starting map, and each of the other five forms the conditioning vector $u$ of one of the five residual blocks ([Brock, Donahue and Simonyan, 2019](#references), Appendix B and Table 4). The latent then reaches features at every scale directly.

### The truncation trick

A GAN never back-propagates through its prior, so the latent distribution used for sampling need not be the one used for training. BigGAN trains with $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and samples from a **truncated normal**: every coordinate of $z$ whose magnitude exceeds a threshold $\tau$ is resampled until it falls inside $[-\tau, \tau]$. Smaller thresholds pull $z$ toward the mode of the prior and the samples toward the most typical outputs of $G$. Single images look better while the set becomes less varied: [precision rises and recall falls](/notes/generative-models/precision-recall#where-the-trade-off-appears).

<TruncationCurves />

How far does truncation move the input? One coordinate keeps its value with probability $2\Phi(\tau) - 1$, and after truncation its variance is

$$
1 - \frac{2\tau\varphi(\tau)}{2\Phi(\tau) - 1} ,
$$

with $\Phi$ and $\varphi$ the standard normal distribution function and density. The mean stays 0 by symmetry, so the variance is $\mathbb E[z^2 \mid \lvert z\rvert \le \tau]$, the integral below divided by the kept mass $2\Phi(\tau) - 1$. Integrate by parts with $\varphi'(z) = -z\varphi(z)$, taking $z$ as one factor and $z\varphi(z)$, whose antiderivative is $-\varphi(z)$, as the other:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-\tau}^{\tau} z^2\varphi(z)\,dz &= \big[-z\varphi(z)\big]_{-\tau}^{\tau} + \int_{-\tau}^{\tau}\varphi(z)\,dz \\
&= -2\tau\varphi(\tau) + 2\Phi(\tau) - 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

For the 120-dimensional $z$:

| $\tau$ | mass kept per coordinate | standard deviation | $\sqrt{\mathbb E\lVert z\rVert^2}$ | no coordinate resampled |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\infty$ | 1 | 1 | 10.95 | 1 |
| 2 | 0.9545 | 0.880 | 9.64 | 0.0037 |
| 1 | 0.6827 | 0.540 | 5.91 | $1.3 \cdot 10^{-20}$ |
| 0.5 | 0.3829 | 0.284 | 3.11 | $9.4 \cdot 10^{-51}$ |

Even the mild $\tau = 2$ changes almost every vector, because a 120-dimensional $z$ nearly always has a coordinate beyond 2. At $\tau = 1$ the vectors are short in a way the generator has practically never seen. Under the training prior, 90% of codes have norms between 9.78 and 12.11, and a norm below 5.91 has probability about $10^{-15}$. Truncation is a shift of the input distribution, and only a generator that behaves smoothly far from its training inputs can take it.

### Orthogonal regularization

To make $G$ smooth enough for that, BigGAN pulls the generator's weights toward orthogonality. For a weight matrix $W$ whose columns are the filters, the full penalty $\lVert W^\top W - I\rVert_F^2$ asks for more than smoothness. With the SVD $W = U\Sigma V^\top$, $W^\top W = V\Sigma^2 V^\top$, which equals $I$ only when every singular value is 1: the layer must stretch all directions equally and cannot weight some features more than others ([Miyato et al., 2018](#references), Section 3). BigGAN found it often too limiting and drops the diagonal:

$$
R_\beta(W) = \beta\,\big\lVert W^\top W \odot (\mathbf 1 - I)\big\rVert_F^2 ,
$$

where $\mathbf 1$ is the all-ones matrix, $\odot$ the elementwise product and $\beta = 10^{-4}$. The diagonal of $W^\top W$ holds the squared norms of the filters and the off-diagonal entries their dot products, so the penalty pushes filters apart without constraining their norms.

A toy example with two filters, $w_1 = (2, 0)$ and $w_2 = (0.6, 0.8)$. $W^\top W$ has diagonal $(4, 1)$ and off-diagonal entries $1.2$. The full penalty is $(4 - 1)^2 + 2 \cdot 1.2^2 = 11.88$, most of it the demand that $\lVert w_1\rVert = 1$. The modified penalty is $2 \cdot 1.2^2 = 2.88$: it cares only about the overlap $w_1^\top w_2 = 1.2$.

Across the authors' runs, 16% of the models without this penalty tolerated truncation, against 60% with it. With truncation tuned so that its Inception Score matches that of real validation images, the deeper BigGAN variant reached IS 166.5 and FID 7.4 on ImageNet at $128 \times 128$, against SAGAN's 52.52 and 18.65.

### Where it breaks: collapse at scale

The large settings train well and then fail suddenly: within a few hundred iterations sample quality drops from its peak to its lowest value. BigGAN looked for the cause in the singular values of the weights. In the generator, the largest singular value of a few layers grows and explodes at the collapse, yet clamping it does not prevent the collapse. In the discriminator, the spectra are noisy and jump at the collapse.

The discriminator turned out to memorize. Uncollapsed discriminators classified over 98% of ImageNet training images as real and only 50–55% of validation images, no better than chance. Regularizing it bought stability with quality: a gradient penalty on real data of strength 10 kept training stable and cut the Inception Score by 45%, and strength 1, the weakest without a collapse, still cost 20%. Freezing $G$ before a collapse left $D$ stable, while freezing $D$ made $G$ collapse at once, so stability lives in the interaction of the two networks. BigGAN accepts the collapse and keeps the checkpoint from just before it.

## What the scores hide

All three papers report single numbers, and each number hides something. The Inception Score ignores variety within a class, so a class-conditional model can raise it with one perfect image per class; truncation pushes toward that, and at BigGAN's maximum IS its FID is about three times the untruncated value. FID mixes lost quality and lost variety into one point, so a truncated model is honestly described by its whole curve of FID against $\tau$. A single FID over all classes also hides classes a model handles badly, which per-class scores such as SAGAN's **intra-FID** expose. And a memorizing discriminator raises the question of whether the generator copies training images, which nearest-neighbour searches answer.

## What changed

Against the DCGAN of the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#architecture-the-dcgan-guidelines), the networks grow during training or become residual networks with attention, batch normalization leaves the discriminator, the loss is WGAN-GP or hinge, and the class label enters every layer of the generator. The latent stayed as it was: one entangled vector whose directions mix attributes.

## What you should remember

- ProGAN: grow $G$ and $D$ from $4 \times 4$, fading each block in with $\alpha$ from 0 to 1.
- Equalized learning rate scales weights by $\sqrt{2/n_{\text{in}}}$ at run time, so Adam moves every layer at the same relative speed.
- SAGAN: $y = \gamma\,o + x$ with $\gamma = 0$ at the start; any two positions interact in one layer.
- Hinge loss with spectral normalization in both networks and TTUR ($4 \cdot 10^{-4}$ for $D$, $10^{-4}$ for $G$).
- BigGAN: large batches, shared class embedding in conditional batch norm, projection discriminator $\psi(\phi(x)) + e(c)^\top\phi(x)$.
- Truncation trades recall for precision; at scale the discriminator memorizes and training is early-stopped.

## Code

The numbers of the toy examples and the truncation table, and the self-attention block in NumPy. It runs in a fraction of a second on Python 3.14.7 with NumPy 2.5 and SciPy 1.18.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# ProGAN: minibatch standard deviation of one feature, for a real and a collapsed batch
real, fake = np.array([1, -1, 2, 0.0]), np.array([1.0, 1.1, 0.9, 1.0])
print(real.std().round(3), fake.std().round(3))                      # 1.118 0.071

# ProGAN: pixelwise normalization of one feature vector
a = np.array([3.0, 4.0])
b = a / np.sqrt((a ** 2).mean() + 1e-8)
print(b.round(3), np.linalg.norm(b).round(3))                        # [0.849 1.131] 1.414

# ProGAN: one Adam step of 1e-3 relative to the He scale sqrt(2 / n_in)
for n_in in (3 * 3 * 512, 3):
    print(n_in, round(np.sqrt(2 / n_in), 4), round(1e-3 / np.sqrt(2 / n_in), 4))
# 4608 0.0208 0.048 | 3 0.8165 0.0012

# SAGAN: the self-attention block on a C x N feature map; gamma = 0 makes it the identity
def softmax(s, axis):
    e = np.exp(s - s.max(axis=axis, keepdims=True))
    return e / e.sum(axis=axis, keepdims=True)

def attention(x, Wf, Wg, Wh, Wv, gamma):
    f, g, h = Wf @ x, Wg @ x, Wh @ x             # (C/8, N) each
    s = f.T @ g                                  # s[i, j] = f(x_i)^T g(x_j), (N, N)
    beta = softmax(s, axis=0)                    # normalize over i for every j
    o = Wv @ (h @ beta)                          # o_j = W_v sum_i beta[i, j] h(x_i)
    return gamma * o + x, beta

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
C, N = 64, 16 * 16
x = rng.standard_normal((C, N))
Wf, Wg, Wh = (rng.standard_normal((C // 8, C)) / np.sqrt(C) for _ in range(3))
Wv = rng.standard_normal((C, C // 8)) / np.sqrt(C // 8)
y, beta = attention(x, Wf, Wg, Wh, Wv, gamma=0.0)
print(beta.shape, np.allclose(beta.sum(axis=0), 1), np.array_equal(y, x))   # (256, 256) True True

w = softmax(np.array([2.0, 0.0, -1.0]), axis=0)  # the toy example: scores of position 1
print(w.round(3), (w @ np.array([1.0, 3.0, 5.0])).round(3))                 # [0.844 0.114 0.042] 1.396

# SAGAN: the hinge loss on two real and two fake scores
real_s, fake_s = np.array([1.5, 0.4]), np.array([-2.0, 0.3])
L_D = np.maximum(0, 1 - real_s).mean() + np.maximum(0, 1 + fake_s).mean()
print(round(L_D, 2), round(-fake_s.mean(), 2))                              # 0.95 0.85

# BigGAN: truncating a 120-dimensional z; per coordinate the mass kept and the standard deviation,
# then the RMS norm of the vector and the probability that no coordinate is resampled
for tau in (2.0, 1.0, 0.5):
    kept = 2 * stats.norm.cdf(tau) - 1
    var = 1 - 2 * tau * stats.norm.pdf(tau) / kept
    print(tau, round(kept, 4), round(np.sqrt(var), 3), round(np.sqrt(120 * var), 2), f"{kept ** 120:.1e}")
# 2.0 0.9545 0.88 9.64 3.7e-03 | 1.0 0.6827 0.54 5.91 1.3e-20 | 0.5 0.3829 0.284 3.11 9.4e-51
var1 = 1 - 2 * stats.norm.pdf(1.0) / (2 * stats.norm.cdf(1.0) - 1)
print(np.sqrt(stats.chi2(120).ppf([0.05, 0.95])).round(2), f"{stats.chi2(120).cdf(120 * var1):.0e}")
# [ 9.78 12.11] 1e-15: the 90% range of training norms, and P(norm below 5.91)

# BigGAN: orthogonal regularization of two filters, full and without the diagonal
W = np.array([[2.0, 0.6], [0.0, 0.8]])           # columns are the filters w1 = (2, 0), w2 = (0.6, 0.8)
G = W.T @ W
off = G * (1 - np.eye(2))
print(round(((G - np.eye(2)) ** 2).sum(), 2), round((off ** 2).sum(), 2))   # 11.88 2.88
```

## Next

BigGAN gets its quality from scale and conditioning, and offers little control over what it draws. The label picks the category; everything else is decided by one entangled vector $z$, whose chunks reach different blocks while nothing ties a direction in $z$ to one attribute. The [next note](/notes/generative-models/stylegan) rebuilds the generator around that problem: StyleGAN maps $z$ to an intermediate latent space and injects it as a "style" at every resolution, which separates coarse attributes such as pose from fine ones such as texture.

- ProGAN ([arXiv v3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10196v3)): $1024 \times 1024$ faces in Figures 5 and 11, LSUN categories at $256 \times 256$ in Figure 7, nearest training neighbours in Figure 10, and the [repository](https://github.com/tkarras/progressive_growing_of_gans) with the result video.
- SAGAN ([arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.08318v2)): ImageNet samples at $128 \times 128$, one class per row, in Figure 6; attention maps in Figures 1 and 5.
- BigGAN ([arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.11096v2)): class-conditional samples in Figure 1, increasing truncation in Figure 2(a), samples at $256 \times 256$ and $512 \times 512$ in Figures 5 and 6, easy and difficult classes in Figure 7.
