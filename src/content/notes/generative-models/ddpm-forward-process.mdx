Take a photo and add a faint layer of Gaussian noise. Add another, and another. After a thousand layers nothing of the photo is left, and the result looks like any other draw from $\mathcal N(0, I)$. Destroying an image this way needs no learning. Played backwards, the same film would turn noise into a photo, one small denoising step per frame.

That reversed film is a **diffusion model**. Unlike the [energy-based models](/notes/generative-models/energy-based-models) before it, it never runs a sampler during training: the noising process is fixed, and a network learns only the way back. **DDPM**, *Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models* ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references)), made this work at image quality competitive with GANs, building on the diffusion models of [Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015)](#references).

Before any network appears, the noising process has to be designed. Two properties make it useful. Wherever the image starts, the process must end at one fixed distribution that we can sample, because generation will start there. And it must be possible to jump from the image to any intermediate step in one draw, because training will need noisy images at random steps millions of times.

## Running a photo into noise

Write $x_0$ for a data point, an image whose pixels are scaled to $[-1, 1]$, and $x_t$ for its noised version after $t$ steps, $t = 1, \dots, T$. One noising step is a conditional distribution $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$, and the steps form a **Markov chain**: $x_t$ depends on the past only through $x_{t-1}$. The data distribution is written $q(x_0)$, since the chain starts there. The learned reverse step, from $x_t$ back to $x_{t-1}$, is written $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$, with $\theta$ the parameters of a network.

$$
\begin{gathered}
\underset{\text{image}}{x_0} \;\longrightarrow\; x_1 \;\longrightarrow\; \cdots \;\longrightarrow\; \underset{\text{noise}}{x_T}, \\
x_{t-1} \;\xleftarrow{\;p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\;}\; x_t .
\end{gathered}
$$

<ForwardChain />

We work in one dimension, a single pixel. Every pixel of an image is noised independently with the same rule, so for images $1$ becomes the identity matrix $I$. Throughout, $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ is standard Gaussian noise, drawn fresh at every step, and the second argument of $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is the **variance**.

## A first attempt: just add noise

The simplest step adds noise of variance $\beta$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_t &= x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta}\,\varepsilon, \\
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) &= \mathcal N(x_{t-1}, \beta) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The square root is there because $c\,\varepsilon$ has variance $c^2$. Two steps add two independent noises, and the variances of independent Gaussians add:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_2 &= x_1 + \sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon_2 \\
&= x_0 + \sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon_1 + \sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon_2 , \\
q(x_2 \mid x_0) &= \mathcal N(x_0, 2\beta) .
\end{aligned}
$$

After $t$ steps, $q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N(x_0, t\beta)$. The jump from $x_0$ to any $x_t$ is one Gaussian draw, so the second property holds. The first does not. The mean stays at $x_0$ forever, and the variance $t\beta$ grows without bound, so there is no fixed distribution at the end of the chain to start generation from. This is the **variance-exploding** process ([Isachenko, 2025](#references), lecture 11). Models built on it stop at a large finite noise level and start sampling from a wide Gaussian of that size; the [score matching note](/notes/generative-models/score-matching#noise-conditional-scores-and-annealing) works with one.

## The objective, and the rescaled step

We want the chain to forget its start:

$$
q(x_t \mid x_0) \xrightarrow[t \to \infty]{} \mathcal N(0, 1)
$$

for every image $x_0$. Adding noise alone cannot do it, because the mean never moves. So each step also shrinks the previous point by a factor $a$, pulling the mean towards $0$:

$$
x_t = a\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon .
$$

The noise variance $\beta_t \in (0, 1)$ may now change from step to step. Which $a$? The fresh noise is independent of $x_{t-1}$, so the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances, and scaling by $a$ multiplies a variance by $a^2$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(x_t) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}) + \beta_t .
$$

The target $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ has variance $1$, and a point already at the target must stay there. Setting both variances to $1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 &= a^2 + \beta_t , \\
a &= \sqrt{1 - \beta_t} .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is DDPM's step ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 2):

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_t &= \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\; x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\;\varepsilon, \\
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) &= \mathcal N\big(\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\; x_{t-1},\; \beta_t\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

<StepVsShortcut />

The step leaves $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ exactly unchanged. If $x_{t-1} \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$, then $x_t$ is a linear combination of two independent standard normals, hence Gaussian ([affine maps of Gaussians](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps)), with mean $0$ and variance $(1 - \beta_t) + \beta_t = 1$. A process whose step keeps unit variance is called **variance-preserving**.

There is also a reason the step pulls towards $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ in particular. For small $\beta_t$ a Taylor expansion gives $\sqrt{1 - \beta_t} \approx 1 - \beta_t/2$; at $\beta_t = 0.02$ the two are $0.98995$ and $0.99$. So

$$
x_t \approx x_{t-1} + \frac{\beta_t}{2}\,(-x_{t-1}) + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon .
$$

The score of $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, the gradient of its log-density, is $-x$. The step is therefore one step of [unadjusted Langevin dynamics](/notes/generative-models/mcmc-samplers#one-euler-step-unadjusted-langevin) with step size $\beta_t$ towards the standard normal: a sampler for $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, run from the image ([Isachenko, 2025](#references), lecture 7).

### The jump from x₀ to any step

Write

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_t &= 1 - \beta_t , \\
\bar\alpha_t &= \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\bar\alpha_0 = 1$. Two steps first, as in the [lecture notes](#references) (lecture 5). Substitute the first step into the second, then expand:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_2 &= \sqrt{\alpha_2}\, x_1 + \sqrt{\beta_2}\,\varepsilon_2 \\
&= \sqrt{\alpha_2}\big(\sqrt{\alpha_1}\, x_0 + \sqrt{\beta_1}\,\varepsilon_1\big) + \sqrt{\beta_2}\,\varepsilon_2 \\
&= \sqrt{\alpha_2 \alpha_1}\, x_0 + \sqrt{\alpha_2 \beta_1}\,\varepsilon_1 + \sqrt{\beta_2}\,\varepsilon_2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Given $x_0$, this is $x_0$ times a constant plus two independent Gaussians, so it is Gaussian. Linearity of expectation gives its mean, and independence of $\varepsilon_1$ and $\varepsilon_2$ gives its variance as a sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[x_2 \mid x_0] &= \sqrt{\alpha_2 \alpha_1}\, x_0 = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_2}\, x_0 , \\
\operatorname{Var}(x_2 \mid x_0) &= \alpha_2 \beta_1 + \beta_2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The variance simplifies once we subtract it from $1$ and use $\beta = 1 - \alpha$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 - \alpha_2 \beta_1 - \beta_2
&= \alpha_2 - \alpha_2 \beta_1 \\
&= \alpha_2 \alpha_1 = \bar\alpha_2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

So $q(x_2 \mid x_0) = \mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_2}\, x_0,\ 1 - \bar\alpha_2\big)$. With toy values $\beta_1 = 0.1$ and $\beta_2 = 0.2$: $\bar\alpha_2 = 0.72$, the mean coefficient is $\sqrt{0.72} = 0.849$, and the variance is $0.8 \cdot 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.28 = 1 - 0.72$.

The same argument, repeated, is an induction on $t$. Suppose $x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\varepsilon'$, where $\varepsilon'$ is a standard normal built from the noises of steps $1, \dots, t-1$, so it is independent of the fresh $\varepsilon$. Substituting into the step:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_t &= \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon \\
&= \sqrt{\alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0 + \sqrt{\alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}\,\varepsilon' + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon .
\end{aligned}
$$

The mean coefficient is $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ by the definition of $\bar\alpha_t$. The two independent noises merge into one Gaussian whose variance is the sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_t (1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t
&= \alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t + 1 - \alpha_t \\
&= 1 - \bar\alpha_t .
\end{aligned}
$$

The base case $t = 1$ is the step itself. Hence, for every $t$,

$$
\boxed{\;q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\; 1 - \bar\alpha_t\big)\;}
$$

or, as a recipe, $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$ ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 4). Here $\varepsilon$ is the merged standard normal, so the equality holds in distribution. The two squared coefficients always add up to $1$, the variance-preserving property again.

### Why this reaches N(0, 1)

If every $\beta_t$ lies in some interval $[\beta_{\min}, 1)$ with $\beta_{\min} > 0$, then $\bar\alpha_t \le (1 - \beta_{\min})^t \to 0$. The image term $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0$ goes to $0$ and the noise coefficient $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ goes to $1$. A Gaussian density is a continuous function of its mean and variance, so it converges pointwise to $\mathcal N(0, 1)$: $q(x_t \mid x_0) \to \mathcal N(0, 1)$ for every $x_0$. For a constant $\beta = 0.02$, so that $\bar\alpha_t = 0.98^t$, the two chains compare as follows:

| $t$ | $\bar\alpha_t$ | mean coefficient $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ | noise std $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ | naive noise std $\sqrt{t\beta}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.980 | 0.990 | 0.141 | 0.141 |
| 10 | 0.817 | 0.904 | 0.428 | 0.447 |
| 50 | 0.364 | 0.603 | 0.797 | 1.000 |
| 100 | 0.133 | 0.364 | 0.931 | 1.414 |
| 200 | 0.0176 | 0.133 | 0.991 | 2.000 |

For small $t$ the two chains almost agree. Then the naive noise passes the target spread and keeps growing, while DDPM's approaches $1$ from below as the image fades.

<VarianceExplodingVsPreserving />

The whole data distribution follows. Its marginal at step $t$ is $q(x_t) = \int q(x_t \mid x_0)\, q(x_0)\, dx_0$. The variance recursion above, with $a^2 = \alpha_t$ and $\beta_t = 1 - \alpha_t$, moves the distance of the variance from $1$ by a factor $\alpha_t$ at each step:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(x_t) - 1
&= \alpha_t \operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}) + 1 - \alpha_t - 1 \\
&= \alpha_t \big(\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}) - 1\big) \\
&= \bar\alpha_t \big(\operatorname{Var}(x_0) - 1\big) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the last line repeats the step down to $x_0$. Hence $\operatorname{Var}(x_t) = \bar\alpha_t \operatorname{Var}(x_0) + 1 - \bar\alpha_t$, a weighted average of the data's variance and $1$. For a mixture of Gaussians the marginal stays a mixture. The integral over $x_0$ is linear, so each component can be pushed through separately, and a component $\mathcal N(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$ scaled by $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ plus independent noise of variance $1 - \bar\alpha_t$ is again Gaussian ([affine maps](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_0) &= \textstyle\sum_k \pi_k\, \mathcal N(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2) , \\
q(x_t) &= \textstyle\sum_k \pi_k\, \mathcal N\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu_k,\; s_{k,t}^2\big) , \\
s_{k,t}^2 &= \bar\alpha_t \sigma_k^2 + 1 - \bar\alpha_t .
\end{aligned}
$$

Every component is pulled towards mean $0$ and variance $1$. The figure below takes $q(x_0) = 0.45\, \mathcal N(-1.3,\ 0.42^2) + 0.55\, \mathcal N(1.25,\ 0.45^2)$ and DDPM's linear schedule. The two humps move together; counting the local maxima of the exact $q(x_t)$ on a fine grid, the density has two up to $t = 266$ and one from $t = 267$, where $\bar\alpha_t = 0.48$. It ends as a single bell curve.

## In practice: a β schedule

The sequence $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$ is the **schedule**. DDPM uses $T = 1000$ steps and a **linear schedule** from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$ ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §4). Small steps at the start keep the early $x_t$ close to the image; larger steps later finish the job. With this schedule $\bar\alpha_t$ falls below $0.5$ at $t = 260$ and below $0.01$ at $t = 674$, and it ends at $\bar\alpha_{1000} = 4.0 \times 10^{-5}$: the image term of $x_{1000}$ is scaled by $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{1000}} = 0.0064$.

<LinearSchedule />

A schedule can also be read as a **signal-to-noise ratio**,

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_t = \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} ,
$$

the squared coefficient of the image over the variance of the noise. For the linear schedule it falls from $10^4$ at $t = 1$ to $4.0 \times 10^{-5}$ at $t = T$. Continuous-time diffusion models parameterize the schedule by this ratio directly ([Murphy, 2023](#references), §25.2.4).

### Linear, quadratic, sigmoid and cosine schedules

Write $u_t = (t - 1)/(T - 1) \in [0, 1]$ for the position in the chain. With the same endpoints $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ and $\beta_T = 0.02$, three common shapes are defined through $\beta_t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{linear:}\quad & \beta_t = \beta_1 + (\beta_T - \beta_1)\, u_t , \\
\text{quadratic:}\quad & \beta_t = \big(\sqrt{\beta_1} + (\sqrt{\beta_T} - \sqrt{\beta_1})\, u_t\big)^2 , \\
\text{sigmoid:}\quad & \beta_t = \beta_1 + (\beta_T - \beta_1)\, s_t , \\
& s_t = \operatorname{sigmoid}(12\, u_t - 6) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The quadratic schedule interpolates the noise amplitude $\sqrt{\beta_t}$ linearly, so its steps stay small for longer. The sigmoid schedule is flat at both ends and makes its change in the middle; since $\operatorname{sigmoid}(\pm 6)$ is not exactly $0$ or $1$, its $\beta_t$ actually run from $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$ to $0.01995$.

The **cosine schedule** prescribes $\bar\alpha_t$ instead and derives the steps from it, using $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}$ ([Nichol and Dhariwal, 2021](#references), Eq. 17):

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(t) &= \cos^2\!\Big(\frac{t/T + s}{1 + s} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2}\Big) , \\
\bar\alpha_t &= \frac{f(t)}{f(0)} , \\
\beta_t &= 1 - \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_{t-1}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The offset $s = 0.008$ keeps the first step's noise amplitude just below the width $1/127.5$ of one pixel level, and $\beta_t$ is clipped at $0.999$: without the clip, $f(T) = 0$ would give $\beta_T = 1$.

With $T = 1000$:

| schedule | $\bar\alpha_t < 0.5$ from | $\bar\alpha_t < 0.01$ from | $\bar\alpha_{1000}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| linear | $t = 260$ | $t = 674$ | $4.0 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| quadratic | $t = 419$ | $t = 851$ | $7.3 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| sigmoid | $t = 440$ | $t = 721$ | $4.0 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| cosine | $t = 497$ | $t = 936$ | $2.4 \times 10^{-9}$ |

The linear schedule destroys the image fastest: from $t = 674$ on less than $1\%$ of the signal variance is left, so its last third works on almost pure noise. Nichol and Dhariwal observed on ImageNet $64 \times 64$ that a model trained with it barely loses FID when up to $20\%$ of the reverse process, the noisiest end, is skipped. Their cosine schedule falls almost linearly through the middle and crosses $0.5$ near $T/2$; with $T = 4000$ it improved FID from $31.3$ to $27.0$ on that dataset (their Table 1).

## Where it breaks: a chain too short to forget

Generation starts from $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, but the chain ends at $q(x_T \mid x_0)$. The mismatch is the KL divergence between the two, which the [next note](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective#splitting-the-bound-into-kl-terms) meets as the term $L_T$ of the training bound. For one pixel, the KL divergence between two 1-D Gaussians ([derived here](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#what-the-gradients-are)) with $\mu_P = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}\, x_0$, $\sigma_P^2 = 1 - \bar\alpha_T$ and $Q = \mathcal N(0, 1)$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_T &= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_T \mid x_0) \,\|\, \mathcal N(0, 1)\big) \\
&= \tfrac12\big(1 - \bar\alpha_T + \bar\alpha_T x_0^2 - 1 - \ln(1 - \bar\alpha_T)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Compare two chains on a pixel at $x_0 = 1$, the brightest value:

| chain | $\bar\alpha_T$ | $q(x_T \mid x_0 = 1)$ | $L_T$, bits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DDPM, $T = 1000$, linear | $4.0 \times 10^{-5}$ | $\mathcal N(0.0064,\ 1.0000)$ | $2.9 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| toy, $T = 100$, sigmoid from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-2}$ | $0.605$ | $\mathcal N(0.78,\ 0.40)$ | $0.67$ |

For pixels spread uniformly over $[-1, 1]$, $\mathbb E[x_0^2] = 1/3$ and DDPM's chain gives $L_T = 9.7 \times 10^{-6}$ bits per pixel, in line with the "$\approx 10^{-5}$ bits per dimension" that Ho, Jain and Abbeel report (§4). The toy chain keeps $78\%$ of the image's amplitude at its last step. A sampler that starts from $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ then starts from the wrong distribution: its first reverse steps are trained on inputs that still show the image, and they are handed pure noise.

Scale matters for the same reason. The image term $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}\, x_0$ is small only relative to $x_0$. DDPM maps pixel values $0, \dots, 255$ to $[-1, 1]$; on the raw scale the leftover image at $T = 1000$ would reach $255 \cdot 0.0064 = 1.6$, larger than the noise.

## What you should remember

- One step: $x_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon$; the factor $\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}$ keeps unit variance fixed.
- Any step in one draw: $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$ with $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s \le t}(1 - \beta_s)$.
- Without rescaling, the variance $t\beta$ explodes and the mean never moves.
- For small $\beta_t$ the step is a Langevin step towards $\mathcal N(0, 1)$.
- DDPM: $T = 1000$, linear $\beta$ from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$, $\bar\alpha_T = 4 \times 10^{-5}$; the cosine schedule spends more steps at medium noise.
- A chain that ends far from $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ makes sampling start from the wrong distribution.

## Next

We now have a process that turns any image into noise indistinguishable from $\mathcal N(0, I)$, and a formula for every intermediate step. Running it backwards needs the reverse step $q(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$, which depends on the unknown data distribution.
