Flows obtained an exact likelihood at the price of a strong architectural restriction: every layer must be invertible with a cheap Jacobian determinant. Diffusion models drop that restriction. They fix a simple noising chain whose latents keep the data's dimension, train an unconstrained network to reverse it, and settle for a bound on the likelihood. **DDPM**, *Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models* ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references)), is the model that brought diffusion to image quality comparable with GANs. This note covers its first half, the **forward process**: a fixed procedure that slowly turns an image into Gaussian noise. The second half, a neural network that runs the procedure backwards, is in [the next note](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective).

**Notation.** $x_0$ is a data point (an image) and $x_t$ its noised version after $t$ steps, $t = 1, \dots, T$. $q$ denotes the fixed forward (noising) process: $q(x_0)$ is the distribution of the data and $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ one noising step. $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ is the learned reverse step, with network parameters $\theta$. $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ is standard Gaussian noise drawn fresh at every step, and the second argument of $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is the **variance**. We work in one dimension; for images every pixel is noised independently and $1$ becomes the identity matrix $I$, so the target is $\mathcal N(0, I)$.

## A little history

- In 2015, when [GANs](/notes/generative-models/gans) were the state of the art in image generation, [Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015)](#references) published *Deep Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics*. It introduced the idea of diffusion models: destroy the structure of the data with a slow noising process, then learn to reverse it.
- Five years later DDPM simplified the parameterization and the loss and reached image quality competitive with GANs. Its training objective is still derived from a **variational** lower bound on the likelihood, the same tool as in the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae).
- Shortly afterwards diffusion models overtook GANs on standard image benchmarks ([Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021](#references)).

## The problem

We do not know the distribution of real images, but we want to sample new images from it. In diffusion notation this distribution is written $q(x_0)$, because it is where the forward process $q$ starts.

The diffusion idea is to connect $q(x_0)$ to a distribution we *can* sample from. Take a real image $x_0$, the original photo, and add a little noise to get $x_1$, then a little more to get $x_2$, and so on until $x_T$ is pure noise. Each forward step is fixed and needs no learning. The model, the learned reverse step $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$, goes the other way: from $x_t$ it produces $x_{t-1}$, one step back towards the image. To generate, we sample pure noise $x_T$ and apply the model $T$ times.

$$
\begin{gathered}
\underset{\text{image}}{x_0} \;\longrightarrow\; x_1 \;\longrightarrow\; \cdots \;\longrightarrow\; \underset{\text{noise}}{x_T}, \\
x_{t-1} \;\xleftarrow{\;p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\;}\; x_t .
\end{gathered}
$$

## A first attempt: just add noise

The simplest step adds noise with variance $\beta$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_1 = x_0 + \sqrt{\beta}\,\varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)
\\ \Longrightarrow\quad
q(x_1 \mid x_0) = \mathcal N(x_0, \beta),
\end{gathered}
$$

where $\beta$ is the **noise variance**. Note the square root: $c\,\varepsilon$ has variance $c^2$, so noise with variance $\beta$ is $\sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon$. The next step does the same to $x_1$: $q(x_2 \mid x_1) = \mathcal N(x_1, \beta)$.

**Now we can shorten the chain.** Two steps add two independent noises, and the variances of independent Gaussians add up:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_2 &= x_1 + \sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon_2 \\
&= x_0 + \sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon_1 + \sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon_2 \\
&= x_0 + \sqrt{2\beta}\,\varepsilon, \\
q(x_2 \mid x_0) &= \mathcal N(x_0, 2\beta),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\varepsilon = (\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)/\sqrt2$ is again a standard normal. Whenever several noises are merged below, $\varepsilon$ denotes the merged standard normal in this sense, so such equalities hold in distribution.

Continuing in the same way, after $t$ steps

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_t = x_0 + \sqrt{t\beta}\,\varepsilon, \\
q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N(x_0, t\beta).
\end{gathered}
$$

This is a useful shortcut: we can jump from $x_0$ to any $x_t$ in one draw. But look at the two parameters:

- the **mean does not change**: it stays $x_0$ forever;
- the **variance increases indefinitely**: $t\beta \to \infty$.

So the chain never reaches $\mathcal N(0, 1)$: the variance grows without bound, and there is no fixed limiting distribution from which generation could start. The image's influence fades only relative to the growing spread. This process is called **variance-exploding** diffusion ([Song et al., 2021](#references), §3.4). Models built on it stop at a large finite noise level $\sigma_{\max}$ and start sampling from $\mathcal N(0, \sigma_{\max}^2)$ ([Song et al., 2021](#references), §2.1).

## The objective, and the rescaled step

What we want is

$$
q(x_t \mid x_0) \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} \mathcal N(0, 1),
$$

whatever the starting image. This needs a new way to go from step $t-1$ to step $t$, and in particular the step has to **change the mean** each time, pulling it towards $0$. So we look for a step of the form $x_t = a\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon$. The Gaussian kernel of diffusion models, introduced by Sohl-Dickstein et al. ([2015](#references), Table App.1) and kept by DDPM ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 2), scales the previous point by $a = \sqrt{1-\beta}$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_t = \sqrt{1-\beta}\;x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta}\;\varepsilon, \\
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{1-\beta}\;x_{t-1},\; \beta\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

<StepVsShortcut />

**Why exactly $\sqrt{1-\beta}$?** The fresh noise is independent of $x_{t-1}$, so the variance obeys $\operatorname{Var}(x_t) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}) + \beta$. This recursion settles at $1$, the variance of the target, only if $a^2 + \beta = 1$. So $a = \sqrt{1-\beta}$ is the only positive scale that keeps a unit variance at $1$, and it also pulls any other variance towards $1$ (callout below). With this $a$ the jump from $x_0$ to any $x_t$ takes an especially simple closed form, whose two squared coefficients sum to $1$ ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), Eq. 4):

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon, \\
\bar\alpha_t = (1-\beta)^t,
\end{gathered}
$$

$$
q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\; 1-\bar\alpha_t\big).
$$

With $\bar\alpha_0 = 1$ the formula also covers $t = 0$. So we can produce $x_t$ for any timestep directly from $x_0$, in one draw, without running the $t$ steps before it.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Where √(1 − β) comes from.</strong> (1) <em>The variance.</em> For a general scale $a$ the recursion $\operatorname{Var}(x_t) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}) + \beta$ has the fixed point $\beta/(1-a^2)$ when $a^2 < 1$, and it grows without bound when $a^2 \ge 1$. The fixed point equals $1$ exactly when $a^2 = 1-\beta$. For this $a$, subtracting $1$ from both sides gives $\operatorname{Var}(x_t) - 1 = (1-\beta)\big(\operatorname{Var}(x_{t-1}) - 1\big)$, so $\operatorname{Var}(x_t) = \bar\alpha_t\operatorname{Var}(x_0) + 1 - \bar\alpha_t$. Unit-variance data keep variance $1$ at every step; any other variance moves monotonically to $1$. That is why this is called a <em>variance-preserving</em> process ([Song et al., 2021](#references), §3.4), the opposite of the naive chain ($a = 1$), whose variance grows to $\operatorname{Var}(x_0) + t\beta$, i.e. $1 + t\beta$ for unit-variance data. (2) <em>The closed form.</em> A chain of linear Gaussian steps stays Gaussian, so some closed form exists for any $a$; the choice $a = \sqrt{1-\beta}$ makes it tidy. By induction: the base case is $t = 1$, where $x_1 = \sqrt{1-\beta}\,x_0 + \sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon$ and $\bar\alpha_1 = 1-\beta$. Suppose $x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\varepsilon'$, where $\varepsilon'$ depends only on the noises of steps $1, \dots, t-1$ and is therefore independent of the fresh $\varepsilon$. Then $x_t = \sqrt{(1-\beta)\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\beta}\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\varepsilon' + \sqrt\beta\,\varepsilon$. The mean coefficient is $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ with $\bar\alpha_t = (1-\beta)\bar\alpha_{t-1}$, and the two independent noises merge into one with variance $(1-\beta)(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta = 1 - (1-\beta)\bar\alpha_{t-1} = 1-\bar\alpha_t$ (see also [Luo, 2022](#references), Eq. 61–70).</aside>

### Why this reaches N(0, 1)

If $\beta \in (0, 1)$, then $\bar\alpha_t = (1-\beta)^t \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$. Then the image term $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 \to 0$, while the noise coefficient $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t} \to 1$, so the noise term tends to plain $\varepsilon$ with variance $1$:

$$
q(x_t \mid x_0) \xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{} \mathcal N(0, 1).
$$

For example, with a constant $\beta = 0.02$:

| $t$ | $\bar\alpha_t = 0.98^t$ | mean coefficient $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ | noise std $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ | naive noise std $\sqrt{t\beta}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.980 | 0.990 | 0.141 | 0.141 |
| 10 | 0.817 | 0.904 | 0.428 | 0.447 |
| 50 | 0.364 | 0.603 | 0.797 | 1.000 |
| 100 | 0.133 | 0.364 | 0.931 | 1.414 |
| 200 | 0.0176 | 0.133 | 0.991 | 2.000 |

For small $t$ the two chains almost agree. Then the naive one passes the target spread and keeps growing, while DDPM's approaches $1$ from below.

<VarianceExplodingVsPreserving />

The same happens to the whole data distribution. Its marginal at step $t$ is $q(x_t) = \int q(x_t \mid x_0)\,q(x_0)\,dx_0$. To see that it also tends to $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, write $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$ with $x_0 \sim q(x_0)$ independent of $\varepsilon$. The first term is a fixed random variable times a factor that goes to $0$, so it tends to $0$ for any data distribution, and the second term tends to $\varepsilon$; hence $x_t \to \mathcal N(0, 1)$ in distribution. In one dimension, a two-humped density spreads out, its humps move towards $0$ and merge (between $t = 200$ and $t = 300$ for the data in [the figure below](#fig-ddpm-forward-process-marginals)), and it ends as a single bell curve centred at $0$. A finite batch of samples looks jagged along the way, but the density itself stays smooth: it is the data density convolved with a Gaussian. For a mixture of Gaussians the marginal stays a mixture and can be written down exactly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_0) &= \textstyle\sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal N(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2), \\
q(x_t) &= \textstyle\sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu_k,\; s_{k,t}^2\big), \\
s_{k,t}^2 &= \bar\alpha_t\sigma_k^2 + 1 - \bar\alpha_t.
\end{aligned}
$$

Both components are pulled towards mean $0$ and variance $1$, so they merge into one standard normal.

## In practice: a β schedule

In practice $\beta$ is not constant but varies with the step, $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$; this is the **schedule**. The derivation above goes through unchanged with $(1-\beta)$ replaced by $(1-\beta_t)$ at step $t$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\; \beta_t\big), \\
\bar\alpha_t = \textstyle\prod_{i=1}^{t} (1-\beta_i), \\
q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N\!\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\; 1-\bar\alpha_t\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

The usual shorthand is $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$, so $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{i \le t}\alpha_i$. The DDPM paper uses $T = 1000$ steps and a **linear schedule** from $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$ to $\beta_T = 0.02$ ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §4). Later work found that a cosine-shaped schedule, which destroys information more evenly over time, works better at low resolution ([Nichol and Dhariwal, 2021](#references), §3.2). Small steps at the start keep the early $x_t$ close to the image; larger steps later finish the job. With this schedule $\bar\alpha_t$ is about $0.5$ at $t \approx 259$ (it drops below $0.5$ at $t = 260$), falls below $0.01$ at $t = 674$ and ends at $\bar\alpha_{1000} \approx 4.0\times10^{-5}$, so $x_{1000}$ is noise for all practical purposes.

The [diffusion notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/42dd64b535f5bedf1d2b3db28f2f21ce96c9f09f/seminars/10_Diffusion-Models/02_DPM_Models.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* repository (pinned commit; cell numbers count every cell from 1) is based on the ACIDS-IRCAM [diffusion tutorial](https://github.com/acids-ircam/diffusion_models). It has no saved outputs, so it documents procedures rather than results.

The notebook uses toy settings: a 2-D Swiss roll and $T = 100$ steps. Its first demo runs the step-by-step chain with a constant $\beta = 0.0005$, for which $\bar\alpha_{100} \approx 0.951$. The rest of the notebook uses a sigmoid-shaped schedule with start $10^{-5}$ and end $10^{-2}$, for which $\bar\alpha_{100} \approx 0.605$; since the sigmoid of $\pm 6$ is not exactly $0$ or $1$, the actual $\beta_t$ run from about $3.5\times10^{-5}$ to $1.0\times10^{-2}$. It samples $x_t$ directly with the closed form (`q_sample`, `alphas_bar_sqrt`, `one_minus_alphas_bar_sqrt`). Neither toy schedule gets close to $\bar\alpha = 0$: the mean coefficient $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{100}}$ is still about $0.98$ and $0.78$, so $x_{100}$ carries much of the data signal. Real models need many more steps or larger $\beta$.

- **Cells 4–6** sample the 2-D Swiss roll used throughout.
- **Cells 8–10**: `forward_process` implements $x_t = \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\varepsilon$, with $\beta$ as a variance and $\sqrt\beta$ as the noise amplitude, and runs it for 100 steps with constant $\beta = 0.0005$. The returned list starts with the clean data at index 0.
- **Cell 12**: `make_beta_schedule` with linear, quadratic and sigmoid schedules (default start $10^{-5}$ and end $10^{-2}$).
- **Cells 13–16**: the closed-form sampler `q_sample` with the sigmoid schedule, using `alphas_bar_sqrt` and `one_minus_alphas_bar_sqrt`. Coefficient index $i$ in the code corresponds to $t = i + 1$ in the formulas, so the plot label $x_0$ already refers to the first noised marginal. These are direct draws from $q(x_t \mid x_0)$ for each $t$, not one correlated trajectory.

The reverse samplers (cells 24 and 47) start from $\mathcal N(0, I)$, but with the sigmoid schedule $q(x_{100} \mid x_0) = \mathcal N(0.78\,x_0,\; 0.40\,I)$, so the starting distribution does not match the end of the forward chain. The reverse process, the training objective and the sampler are in cells 17–48; they are described in the [next note](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective#notebook).

## Next

We now have a diffusion process that turns any image into noise that is indistinguishable from $\mathcal N(0, I)$ in practice; for DDPM's schedule, $\bar\alpha_T \approx 4\times10^{-5}$. To generate, we have to **invert** it, and that is done with a deep neural network. How to set up and train that network is the topic of [DDPM: reverse process and training objective](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective).
