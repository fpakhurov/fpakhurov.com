Picture a density as a landscape and stand at any point. An arrow tells you which way is uphill and how steep the slope is. For $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2 I)$ the arrow at $x$ is $-(x - \mu)/\sigma^2$: it points back to the mean, longer the farther away you are. Knowing the arrow everywhere, you could walk uphill with a little random jitter and end up spread over the landscape in proportion to its height, that is, sampling from it.

The arrow is the **score**, $s(x) = \nabla_x \log p(x)$, for a density $p$ on $\mathbb R^d$. The [DDPM note](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective) reached a noise-predicting network by bounding a likelihood. The same network can be reached by learning the score of noised data directly, and sampling by following it.

## Why the score

Many models define a density only up to a constant, like the [energy-based models](/notes/generative-models/energy-based-models): $p_\theta(x) = e^{-E_\theta(x)} / Z_\theta$, with a normalizer $Z_\theta = \int e^{-E_\theta(x)}\, dx$ that cannot be computed in high dimension. Maximum likelihood needs $\log Z_\theta$. The score does not:

$$
\nabla_x \log p_\theta(x) = -\nabla_x E_\theta(x) - \underbrace{\nabla_x \log Z_\theta}_{=\,0} ,
$$

because $Z_\theta$ depends on $\theta$ but not on $x$. The score still carries all the information about $p$ where $p$ is positive and smooth on a connected region, such as all of $\mathbb R^d$: two such densities with the same score everywhere have log-densities that differ by a constant, so the densities differ by a constant factor, which normalization fixes.

When the score is parameterized through an energy, $s_\theta = -\nabla_x E_\theta$, fitting it trains an energy-based model with neither its normalizer nor samples from it ([energy-based models](/notes/generative-models/energy-based-models#score-matching-no-samples-and-no-z)). Diffusion models instead let a network $s_\theta: \mathbb R^d \to \mathbb R^d$ output the vector field directly. Its Jacobian need not be symmetric, so it need not be the gradient of any scalar function: an approximate score with no energy behind it.

## Fitting a score: Hyvärinen's trick

The natural loss is the **Fisher divergence**, the mean squared distance between the model's score and the data's:

$$
J(\theta) = \tfrac12\, \mathbb E_p \big\lVert s_\theta(x) - \nabla_x \log p(x) \big\rVert^2 .
$$

It needs the true score, which is unknown. [Hyvärinen (2005)](#references) removes it by integration by parts. In one dimension, expand the square. The term $\tfrac12 \mathbb E_p (\log p)'^2$ does not depend on $\theta$. The cross term becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_p\big[s_\theta\, (\log p)'\big]
&= \int s_\theta(x)\, p(x)\, \frac{p'(x)}{p(x)}\, dx \\
&= \int s_\theta(x)\, p'(x)\, dx \\
&= \big[s_\theta\, p\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} - \int s_\theta'(x)\, p(x)\, dx \\
&= -\mathbb E_p\big[s_\theta'(x)\big] ,
\end{aligned}
$$

by the chain rule $(\log p)' = p'/p$, integration by parts, and the assumption that $s_\theta(x)\, p(x) \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$. In $d$ dimensions the same step is taken in each coordinate $x_i$ for the component $s_{\theta, i}$, and the derivatives $\partial s_{\theta, i} / \partial x_i$ add up to the trace of the Jacobian, $\nabla \cdot s_\theta$:

$$
J(\theta) = \mathbb E_p\Big[\nabla \cdot s_\theta(x) + \tfrac12 \lVert s_\theta(x) \rVert^2\Big] + \text{const} .
$$

The expectation is over data and involves only the model. Hyvärinen's Theorem 1 states it with its conditions: $p$ differentiable, $\mathbb E_p \lVert s_\theta \rVert^2$ and $\mathbb E_p \lVert \nabla \log p \rVert^2$ finite, and $p(x)\, s_\theta(x) \to 0$ as $\lVert x \rVert \to \infty$.

**Worked example.** Fit a Gaussian score $s_\theta(x) = -a(x - \mu)$, $a = 1/\sigma^2$, to the data $\{1, 2, 4, 5\}$. Then $s_\theta' = -a$, and the sample objective is the average of $\tfrac12 a^2 (x - \mu)^2 - a$. Its derivative in $\mu$ vanishes at $\mu = \bar x = 3$. Its derivative in $a$ vanishes when $a \cdot \overline{(x - \mu)^2} = 1$, so $\sigma^2 = \tfrac14 (4 + 1 + 1 + 4) = 2.5$. Score matching recovers the sample mean and variance, the maximum likelihood estimates, without touching the normalizing constant; for the Gaussian the two estimators coincide on every sample ([Hyvärinen, 2005](#references), §3.1).

The cost is the trace. For a network $s_\theta: \mathbb R^d \to \mathbb R^d$, $\nabla \cdot s_\theta$ needs $d$ backward passes, one per diagonal entry of the Jacobian: fine for $d = 2$, impossible for an image with $d = 3 \cdot 64 \cdot 64$.

## Two cheaper objectives

**Sliced score matching** ([Song et al., 2019](#references)) projects onto random directions $v$ with $\mathbb E[v v^\top] = I$:

$$
J_{\text{SSM}}(\theta) = \mathbb E_p\, \mathbb E_v \Big[v^\top \nabla s_\theta(x)\, v + \tfrac12 \big(v^\top s_\theta(x)\big)^2\Big] .
$$

Averaged over $v$ this is Hyvärinen's objective, because $\mathbb E[v^\top A v] = \operatorname{tr}(A\, \mathbb E[v v^\top]) = \operatorname{tr}(A)$ and $\mathbb E[(v^\top s)^2] = s^\top \mathbb E[v v^\top]\, s = \lVert s \rVert^2$. One Jacobian–vector product replaces $d$ backward passes ([Song and Ermon, 2019](#references), §2.1).

**Denoising score matching** ([Vincent, 2011](#references)) removes the derivative of the model from the loss altogether, at a price: it fits the score of noised data. Draw $x \sim p$ and perturb it with a kernel $q(\tilde x \mid x)$ whose score we know, so that the noised data have the density $q(\tilde x) = \int q(\tilde x \mid x)\, p(x)\, dx$. The Fisher divergence to the noised data, the explicit objective, and the denoising objective are

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_{\text{ESM}}(\theta) &= \tfrac12\, \mathbb E_{q(\tilde x)} \big\lVert s_\theta(\tilde x) - \nabla \log q(\tilde x) \big\rVert^2 , \\
J_{\text{DSM}}(\theta) &= \tfrac12\, \mathbb E_{p(x)\, q(\tilde x \mid x)} \big\lVert s_\theta(\tilde x) - \nabla_{\tilde x} \log q(\tilde x \mid x) \big\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first needs the unknown score of $q(\tilde x)$. The second needs only the kernel's score. They differ by a constant:

$$
\boxed{\;J_{\text{ESM}}(\theta) = J_{\text{DSM}}(\theta) + C\;}
$$

with $C$ independent of $\theta$ ([Vincent, 2011](#references), §4.2, proved in the Appendix).

<details>
<summary>Proof that the two objectives differ by a constant</summary>

Expand both squares. Each gives three terms: $\tfrac12 \lVert s_\theta \rVert^2$, a cross term, and the square of the target score.

The squares of the targets, $\tfrac12 \mathbb E \lVert \nabla \log q(\tilde x) \rVert^2$ and $\tfrac12 \mathbb E \lVert \nabla_{\tilde x} \log q(\tilde x \mid x) \rVert^2$, do not depend on $\theta$; call them $C_1$ and $C_2$.

The terms $\tfrac12 \lVert s_\theta(\tilde x) \rVert^2$ depend on $\tilde x$ only, and the marginal of $\tilde x$ under $p(x)\, q(\tilde x \mid x)$ is $q(\tilde x)$, so they have the same expectation in both objectives.

The cross terms are equal too. Starting from the explicit one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S(\theta) &= \mathbb E_{q(\tilde x)}\big[s_\theta^\top \nabla \log q(\tilde x)\big] \\
&= \int s_\theta(\tilde x)^\top \nabla q(\tilde x)\, d\tilde x \\
&= \int s_\theta(\tilde x)^\top \nabla_{\tilde x} \int p(x)\, q(\tilde x \mid x)\, dx\, d\tilde x \\
&= \iint p(x)\, s_\theta(\tilde x)^\top \nabla_{\tilde x} q(\tilde x \mid x)\, dx\, d\tilde x \\
&= \iint p(x)\, q(\tilde x \mid x)\, s_\theta^\top \nabla_{\tilde x} \log q(\tilde x \mid x)\, dx\, d\tilde x \\
&= \mathbb E_{p(x)\, q(\tilde x \mid x)}\big[s_\theta^\top \nabla_{\tilde x} \log q(\tilde x \mid x)\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The steps use, in order: $q \nabla \log q = \nabla q$; the definition of $q(\tilde x)$; differentiation under the integral sign, which needs $\nabla_{\tilde x} q(\tilde x \mid x)$ to be integrable against $p(x)$; the same identity $q \nabla \log q = \nabla q$ for the kernel; and the definition of the expectation over the joint. Subtracting the expanded objectives leaves $J_{\text{ESM}} - J_{\text{DSM}} = C_1 - C_2$. The kernel's form never entered, only that $\log q(\tilde x \mid x)$ is differentiable in $\tilde x$.

</details>

The usual kernel is Gaussian, $\tilde x = x + \sigma \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$; write $q_\sigma$ for the resulting density of the noised data. The kernel's score is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\tilde x} \log \mathcal N(\tilde x; x, \sigma^2 I) &= -\frac{\tilde x - x}{\sigma^2} \\
&= -\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and the objective is a regression of the network's output at the noised point onto $-\varepsilon / \sigma$:

$$
J_{\text{DSM}}(\theta) = \tfrac12\, \mathbb E_{x, \varepsilon} \Big\lVert s_\theta(x + \sigma \varepsilon) + \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma} \Big\rVert^2 .
$$

### Tweedie's formula

There is a second way to see why the regression works. A squared-error regression learns the conditional mean of its target, here $\mathbb E[-\varepsilon / \sigma \mid \tilde x]$. That conditional mean is the score of $q_\sigma$. Differentiate $q_\sigma(\tilde x) = \int p(x)\, \mathcal N(\tilde x; x, \sigma^2 I)\, dx$ under the integral, divide by $q_\sigma(\tilde x)$, and recognize Bayes' theorem, $p(x \mid \tilde x) = p(x)\, \mathcal N(\tilde x; x, \sigma^2 I) / q_\sigma(\tilde x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla \log q_\sigma(\tilde x)
&= \frac{1}{q_\sigma(\tilde x)} \int p(x)\, \mathcal N(\tilde x; x, \sigma^2 I)\, \frac{x - \tilde x}{\sigma^2}\, dx \\
&= \int p(x \mid \tilde x)\, \frac{x - \tilde x}{\sigma^2}\, dx \\
&= \frac{\mathbb E[x \mid \tilde x] - \tilde x}{\sigma^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Rearranged, this is **Tweedie's formula** ([Efron, 2011](#references), Eq. 1.4):

$$
\boxed{\;\mathbb E[x \mid \tilde x] = \tilde x + \sigma^2\, \nabla \log q_\sigma(\tilde x)\;}
$$

The best guess of the clean point is the noisy point moved along the score of the noised data. With $\varepsilon = (\tilde x - x)/\sigma$, it also gives $\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid \tilde x] = -\sigma\, \nabla \log q_\sigma(\tilde x)$, so the optimal regression target $\mathbb E[-\varepsilon / \sigma \mid \tilde x]$ is the score itself. Denoising and score estimation are the same task.

**Worked example.** Data $x \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $\sigma = 0.5$. Then $\tilde x \sim \mathcal N(0, 1.25)$, whose score is $-\tilde x / 1.25 = -0.8\, \tilde x$. Tweedie gives $\mathbb E[x \mid \tilde x] = \tilde x - 0.25 \cdot 0.8\, \tilde x = 0.8\, \tilde x$, the familiar shrinkage of a Gaussian posterior mean. Directly: $\varepsilon$ and $\tilde x$ are jointly Gaussian with $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon, \tilde x) = 0.5$, so $\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid \tilde x] = 0.5\, \tilde x / 1.25 = 0.4\, \tilde x$, and $\mathbb E[-\varepsilon / \sigma \mid \tilde x] = -0.8\, \tilde x$, the score.

### The price of the noise

Denoising score matching estimates the score of $q_\sigma$, not of $p$. A small $\sigma$ keeps the two close, but the target $-\varepsilon / \sigma$ then scatters widely around its mean, the score. Per coordinate, the law of total variance splits $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = 1$ into the average scatter and the variance of the mean, and Tweedie gives that mean, $\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i \mid \tilde x] = -\sigma\, \partial_i \log q_\sigma(\tilde x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 &= \mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i \mid \tilde x)\big] + \sigma^2\, \mathbb E\big[(\partial_i \log q_\sigma)^2\big] , \\
\mathbb E\Big[\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac{\varepsilon_i}{\sigma} \,\Big|\, \tilde x\Big)\Big] &= \frac{1}{\sigma^2} - \mathbb E\big[(\partial_i \log q_\sigma)^2\big] \\
&\le \frac{1}{\sigma^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

In the worked example above, $1/0.25 - 0.8 = 3.2$ against the bound $4$. The subtracted term stays bounded as $\sigma \to 0$. For differentiable $p$ that vanishes at infinity, moving the derivative from the kernel onto $p$ by integration by parts gives $\partial_i \log q_\sigma(\tilde x) = \mathbb E[\partial_i \log p(x) \mid \tilde x]$, and Jensen's inequality bounds its mean square by $\mathbb E_p[(\partial_i \log p)^2]$, finite for smooth data. So the scatter grows like $1/\sigma^2$ ([Murphy, 2023](#references), §24.3.2). It does not depend on $\theta$ and does not move the minimizer, but it is noise in every gradient estimate. A large $\sigma$ gives an easy regression of a blurred density. The way out, below, is many values of $\sigma$ at once.

## Sampling with Langevin dynamics

Given a score, **Langevin dynamics** produces samples:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{k+1} &= x_k + \frac{\eta}{2}\, s(x_k) + \sqrt{\eta}\, z_k , \\
z_k &\sim \mathcal N(0, I) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The update is one Euler step of the Langevin diffusion, whose stationary density is $p$; the [MCMC samplers note](/notes/generative-models/mcmc-samplers#the-langevin-equation) derives both. The drift climbs the density and the noise keeps the chain from collapsing onto a mode: without the noise term the update is gradient ascent on $\log p$ and ends at a mode, not at a sample. Under regularity conditions the distribution of $x_k$ becomes exactly $p$ as $\eta \to 0$ and the number of steps grows without bound ([Song and Ermon, 2019](#references), §2.2).

With a finite step the chain is biased. For $p = \mathcal N(0, 1)$, $s(x) = -x$, and a stationary variance $v$ must reproduce itself under one step, which gives ([derived here](/notes/generative-models/mcmc-samplers#one-euler-step-unadjusted-langevin))

$$
v = \frac{1}{1 - \eta/4} .
$$

With $\eta = 0.1$ the chain settles at variance $1.026$ instead of $1$; with $\eta = 0.5$, at $1.143$. A Metropolis–Hastings correction would remove the bias, at the cost of evaluating the density, which is what the score was meant to avoid.

**Shrinking the step during the run.** The bias also vanishes if the step decreases along the chain, but only if it decreases slowly. [Welling and Teh (2011)](#references), Eq. 2, use the [Robbins–Monro conditions](/notes/bayesian-methods/stochastic-variational-inference#step-sizes-the-robbinsmonro-conditions), $\sum_k \eta_k = \infty$ and $\sum_k \eta_k^2 < \infty$, for example $\eta_k \propto (b + k)^{-\gamma}$ with $\gamma \in (0.5, 1]$. The first condition lets the chain travel any distance from its start. A geometric decay $\eta_k = \eta_0 \rho^k$ breaks it: the steps add up to $\eta_0 / (1 - \rho)$, and the chain freezes before it has mixed. In the Gaussian example the starting point survives in $x_K$ with the factor $\prod_k (1 - \eta_k / 2)$; with $\eta_0 = 0.01$ and $\rho = 0.9$, after ten steps that factor is still $0.968$.

**Temperature.** Some samplers scale the noise by a temperature $\tau$, $x_{k+1} = x_k + \frac\eta2 s(x_k) + \tau \sqrt\eta\, z_k$. Since $\frac\eta2 \nabla \log p = \frac{\tau^2 \eta}{2} \nabla \log p^{1/\tau^2}$, this is the plain sampler for the density proportional to $p^{1/\tau^2}$ with step $\tau^2 \eta$, so a temperature below $1$ concentrates the samples near the modes. In the Gaussian example the stationary variance becomes $\tau^2 / (1 - \eta/4)$: $0.256$ for $\tau = 0.5$ and $\eta = 0.1$, close to the variance $0.25$ of $p^4 \propto \mathcal N(0, 1/4)$.

## Why one noise level fails

Two problems appear as soon as the data are more than one blob ([Song and Ermon, 2019](#references), §3).

**The score is unknown where there are no data.** Score matching weights its error by $p(x)$, so in regions of low density the learned score is barely trained, and a chain started there, as every chain started from noise is, follows arbitrary arrows. When the data lie near a low-dimensional manifold, as images do, almost all of space is such a region, and the score of the data is not even defined off the manifold.

**Langevin dynamics cannot balance separated modes.** Let $p = \pi\, p_1 + (1 - \pi)\, p_2$ with $p_1$ and $p_2$ on disjoint supports. On the support of $p_1$, $\nabla \log p = \nabla \log \pi + \nabla \log p_1 = \nabla \log p_1$, since $\pi$ is a constant; the same holds on the other support. The score does not depend on $\pi$ at all, so neither do samples that use only the score. Modes that merely overlap a little behave almost the same way. Take $p = 0.8\, \mathcal N(-4, 1) + 0.2\, \mathcal N(4, 1)$. The score is $\sum_k r_k(x)(\mu_k - x)$, with $r_k(x)$ the posterior probability of component $k$ at $x$. Near each mode one component dominates, its responsibility is close to $1$, and the weights drop out. They show only in the gap: at $x = 0$ the two components have the same likelihood, so the responsibilities equal the weights and the score is $0.8 \cdot (-4) + 0.2 \cdot 4 = -2.4$. But the density there is $1.3 \times 10^{-4}$, and chains rarely visit. With the exact score, 4000 chains started uniformly on $[-8, 8]$ end with $0.52$ of them in the left mode after 2000 steps of size $0.05$: about the share that started on the left, instead of the true $0.8$.

Noise addresses both problems ([Song and Ermon, 2019](#references), §4). The noised density $q_\sigma$ is positive everywhere, so its score is defined everywhere, and at large $\sigma$ the noised training points spread over a much wider region, so the network is trained where chains from noise begin. A large $\sigma$ also fills the gap between the modes, so that their weights become visible to a chain.

## Noise-conditional scores and annealing

[Song and Ermon (2019)](#references) train one network for many noise levels at once. Choose a geometric sequence $\sigma_1 > \sigma_2 > \dots > \sigma_L$, give the network the level as a second input, and minimize a weighted average of denoising objectives (their Eq. 5–6):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\theta) &= \frac1L \sum_{i=1}^{L} \sigma_i^2\, \ell(\theta; \sigma_i) , \\
\ell(\theta; \sigma) &= \tfrac12\, \mathbb E_{x, \varepsilon} \Big\lVert s_\theta(x + \sigma \varepsilon, \sigma) + \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma} \Big\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Why the weight $\sigma_i^2$? Moving it inside the norm gives $\sigma^2 \ell = \tfrac12 \mathbb E \lVert \sigma s_\theta + \varepsilon \rVert^2$. By Tweedie, the optimal $\sigma s_\theta$ is $-\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid \tilde x]$, a quantity of the size of a standard normal at every level, so every weighted term is of the same order and no level dominates the sum. Song and Ermon arrived at the same weight from the observation that trained networks have $\lVert s_\theta \rVert \propto 1/\sigma$.

The network sees the level. In the NCSN of Song and Ermon every normalization layer has a learned scale and shift for each level (§4.1). In a small fully connected network the level can select a learned vector that is added to a hidden layer, $h \mapsto W h + b + e_i$; diffusion networks receive their timestep in the same ways ([DDPM note](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective#telling-the-network-the-timestep)). Training draws one level per example: the average over levels is $\mathbb E_{i \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{1, \dots, L\}}[\sigma_i^2 \ell(\theta; \sigma_i)]$, so one uniformly drawn level gives an unbiased estimate, as the random timestep does for [DDPM](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective#final-simplification-a-random-timestep-per-sample).

<ScoreField />

Sampling, **annealed Langevin dynamics** (their Algorithm 1), walks down the levels:

1. Start from a fixed prior distribution, such as uniform noise.
2. For $i = 1, \dots, L$, set the step $\alpha_i = \epsilon\, \sigma_i^2 / \sigma_L^2$ and run $T$ Langevin steps with $s_\theta(\cdot, \sigma_i)$, starting from where the previous level stopped.
3. Return the last sample.

At the largest level the chain moves freely and the weights of the modes are visible; each smaller level refines the sample, with a step that shrinks with the noise. For images the paper uses $L = 10$ levels from $\sigma_1 = 1$ to $\sigma_{10} = 0.01$, $T = 100$ steps per level and $\epsilon = 2 \times 10^{-5}$ (§5).

In the mixture example, annealing through ten geometric levels from $\sigma = 5$ to $\sigma = 0.1$, 100 steps each with $\epsilon = 0.002$, with the exact score of each $q_\sigma$, ends with $0.79$ of the chains in the left mode, against the true $0.8$. How well this works depends on the choices: the levels must overlap, each must get enough steps of a suitable size, and a learned score adds its own error.

## The same model as DDPM

DDPM noises an image as $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon$ ([forward process](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process#the-jump-from-x-to-any-step)). This is a Gaussian kernel too, and its score is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t \mid x_0) &= -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0}{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \\
&= -\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Denoising score matching at level $t$ therefore regresses $s_\theta(x_t, t)$ onto $-\varepsilon / \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$. Write the network as $s_\theta(x_t, t) = -\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) / \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$, and weight level $t$ by the noise variance $1 - \bar\alpha_t$, the $\sigma^2$ rule above:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(1 - \bar\alpha_t) \cdot \tfrac12 \Big\lVert s_\theta + \frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}} \Big\rVert^2
&= \tfrac12 \lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Averaged over a uniform $t$, this is $\tfrac12 L_{\text{simple}}$: DDPM's loss is noise-conditional denoising score matching with the noise variance as weight, and its noise predictor is a score, $\varepsilon_\theta = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, s_\theta$ ([Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020](#references), §3.2). Tweedie's formula, applied to the scaled image $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0$ observed with noise of variance $1 - \bar\alpha_t$, turns it into a guess of the image:

$$
\mathbb E[x_0 \mid x_t] = \frac{x_t + (1 - \bar\alpha_t)\, \nabla \log q(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} .
$$

The sampler matches too. Substituting $\varepsilon_\theta = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, s_\theta$ into DDPM's [sampling step](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective#sampling-running-the-chain-backwards):

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{t-1} &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \varepsilon_\theta\Big) + \sigma_t z \\
&= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \big(x_t + \beta_t\, s_\theta(x_t, t)\big) + \sigma_t z .
\end{aligned}
$$

Apart from the rescaling by $1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}$, which undoes the shrinking of the forward step, each step moves along the learned score and adds fresh noise, with the noise level changing from step to step as in annealing. Ho, Jain and Abbeel describe it as resembling Langevin dynamics (§3.2). The weights differ: $\beta_t$ on the score against noise variance $\sigma_t^2 \approx \beta_t$, where a Langevin step puts half the noise variance on the score. They are the weights of the reverse-time SDE, whose drift splits into two halves: one moves the density back along the forward path, the other, with the noise, is a Langevin step that holds it in place ([continuous-time note](/notes/generative-models/continuous-time-diffusion#running-the-sde-backwards)).

## What you should remember

- The score $\nabla_x \log p$ ignores the normalizer; on a connected positive region it fixes $p$.
- Hyvärinen: $J = \mathbb E_p[\nabla \cdot s_\theta + \tfrac12 \lVert s_\theta \rVert^2] + \text{const}$, by integration by parts.
- Denoising score matching regresses onto $-\varepsilon/\sigma$ and equals the Fisher divergence to the noised data up to a constant.
- Tweedie: $\mathbb E[x \mid \tilde x] = \tilde x + \sigma^2 \nabla \log q_\sigma(\tilde x)$, so denoising is score estimation.
- One noise level fails in empty regions and between modes; noise-conditional scores weighted by $\sigma^2$ and annealed Langevin fix both.
- DDPM is this with $\varepsilon_\theta = -\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, s_\theta$.

- Sliced score matching on 1000 points. The directions are normalized to unit length, so $\mathbb E[v v^\top] = I/d$, and both terms are averaged over coordinates; both terms carry the same factor $1/d^2$, so the minimizer is unchanged.
- Denoising score matching with $\sigma = 0.01$ for 5000 steps: targets of size $1/\sigma = 100$, the [price of a small $\sigma$](#the-price-of-the-noise).
- A noise-conditional network with the four levels $1$, $0.215$, $0.046$ and $0.01$, the $\sigma^2$ weight, an additive learned vector per level, and one random level per point. Annealed Langevin sampling is not implemented.

## Next

DDPM's thousand steps and NCSN's noise levels are two discretizations of one idea: noise the data gradually, learn the score at every noise level, and walk back. [Continuous-time diffusion and flow matching](/notes/generative-models/continuous-time-diffusion) lets the number of steps go to infinity, which turns both into one stochastic differential equation with a deterministic counterpart, and from there reaches flow matching.
