A [GAN](/notes/generative-models/gans) produces samples but cannot tell us how likely a given image is. A [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) can, but only through a lower bound. **Normalizing flows** take a third route ([Rezende and Mohamed, 2015](#references); [Papamakarios et al., 2021](#references)): start from a simple density, push it through an *invertible* map, and compute the density of the result exactly with the change-of-variables formula. This note builds that formula, stacks it into a flow, compares flows with the other model families, and ends with the trick that makes flows computable: a triangular Jacobian. How to build layers with that property is the subject of the next note, [Flow architectures](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures).

**Notation.** The map $f$ always goes from the latent space to the data space, $f: \mathcal Z \to \mathcal X$, so $x = f(z)$ generates data and $z = f^{-1}(x)$ encodes it. $p_Z$ is the base (latent) density and $p_\theta(x)$ the model density of the data. For a map $g$, $J_g(u) = \partial g(u)/\partial u$ is its Jacobian matrix at the point $u$.

## The model: invertible maps out of a Gaussian

Take a continuous random variable $z$ with a simple density, a spherical Gaussian:

$$
z \sim p_Z(z) = \mathcal N(z;\, 0,\, I).
$$

“Spherical” means the covariance is a multiple of the identity (here exactly $I$): the same spread in every direction and spherical contours. The data are produced by a chain of $K$ maps,

$$
x = f_\theta(z) = f_K \circ \dots \circ f_2 \circ f_1 (z),
$$

and **each $f_i$ is invertible (bijective)**: every input has exactly one output and every output exactly one input. For the density formula below, each $f_i$ must also be differentiable with a differentiable inverse (a *diffeomorphism*). Then the whole chain is invertible too, with $f^{-1} = f_1^{-1} \circ f_2^{-1} \circ \dots \circ f_K^{-1}$. A diffeomorphism needs $\dim z = \dim x$ (otherwise its Jacobian would not even be square), so unlike in a VAE the latent vector is as large as the data.

## Why not just $p_Z(f^{-1}(x))$?

We want $p_\theta(x)$. The first guess is to map $x$ back and read off the base density, $p_\theta(x) \overset{?}{=} p_Z(f^{-1}(x))$. This is wrong, **because the answer depends on how $f$ behaves**: on how much it stretches or squeezes space around each point.

A one-line counterexample: in one dimension let $z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $x = f(z) = 2z$. The naive guess gives $p_Z(x/2)$, which integrates to $2$ over the real line, so it is not even a density. The true distribution is $x \sim \mathcal N(0, 4)$ with density $p_Z(x/2)/2$. At $x = 1$ the naive value is $0.352$ and the correct one is $0.176$: $f$ spreads the same probability over twice the length, so the density must halve.

## Change of variables

Let $f: \mathcal Z \to \mathcal X$ be invertible, differentiable and with a differentiable inverse, and let $p_Z(z)$ be a density defined over $z \in \mathcal Z$.

**One dimension.** Probability mass is conserved. A small interval of width $|dz|$ around $z$ holds mass $p_Z(z)\,|dz|$, and $f$ sends it to an interval of width $|dx|$ around $x = f(z)$ that must hold the same mass:

$$
p_\theta(x)\,|dx| = p_Z(z)\,|dz|
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p_\theta(x) = p_Z(z)\left|\frac{dz}{dx}\right| = \frac{p_Z(f^{-1}(x))}{\left|f'(f^{-1}(x))\right|}.
$$

**Any dimension.** Widths become volumes, and the factor that converts volumes is the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant:

$$
p_\theta(x)
= p_Z\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)\,\left|\det \frac{\partial f^{-1}(x)}{\partial x}\right|
\;\overset{z = f^{-1}(x)}{=}\;
p_Z(z)\,\left|\det \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right|
= p_Z(z)\,\left|\det \frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial z}\right|^{-1}.
$$

The two forms say the same thing. The Jacobian of the inverse is evaluated at the data point, while the Jacobian of $f$ is evaluated at the latent point $z = f^{-1}(x)$. They are linked by the inverse function theorem, $J_{f^{-1}}(x) = \big[J_f(z)\big]^{-1}$, and $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A$. The factor $|\det J|$ measures the **expansion or contraction of space** around the point ([Papamakarios et al., 2021](#references), §2.1). Where $f$ expands space, the same probability is spread thinner and the density drops; where $f$ contracts space, the density rises.

### A worked one-dimensional example

Take $p_Z = \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and the map in the figure,

$$
f(z) = z + 0.8\tanh(2z),
\qquad
f'(z) = 1 + 1.6\,\operatorname{sech}^2(2z) > 0 .
$$

The derivative is positive everywhere, so $f$ is strictly increasing and therefore invertible (the inverse has no closed form but is easy to find numerically). Then $p_\theta(x) = p_Z(z)/f'(z)$ with $z = f^{-1}(x)$:

| $z$ | $x = f(z)$ | $f'(z)$ | $p_Z(z)$ | $p_\theta(x) = p_Z(z)/f'(z)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $0$ | $2.600$ | $0.3989$ | $0.1534$ |
| $0.5$ | $1.109$ | $1.672$ | $0.3521$ | $0.2106$ |
| $1$ | $1.771$ | $1.113$ | $0.2420$ | $0.2174$ |
| $2$ | $2.799$ | $1.002$ | $0.0540$ | $0.0539$ |

Near the origin $f$ stretches space by a factor of $2.6$, so the density there falls well below the Gaussian peak; in the tails $f' \approx 1$ and the density is almost unchanged. The single bump becomes two bumps with maxima at $x \approx \pm 1.50$ (that is $z \approx \pm 0.77$), $p_\theta \approx 0.234$. Even one smooth invertible map can turn a unimodal base into a bimodal model. In log form, at $z = 0.5$: $\log p_\theta(x) = \log p_Z(z) - \log f'(z) = -1.044 - 0.514 = -1.558$.

The slab $z \in [0.1, 0.5]$ holds probability $\Phi(0.5) - \Phi(0.1) = 0.152$. Its image is $x \in [0.258, 1.109]$, $2.13$ times wider, so the average density over it drops from $0.379$ to $0.178$, by the same factor: same probability mass, different width.

### Example: stretching space twice

The simplest multivariate case is a linear map $x = Jz$, whose Jacobian $\partial x/\partial z = J$ is the same everywhere. With

$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

every direction is stretched 2 times, and $\det J = 2\cdot 2 - 0\cdot 0 = 4$. A unit square of $\mathcal Z$ becomes a $2\times 2$ square of $\mathcal X$, so

$$
p_\theta(x) = p_Z(z)\left|\frac{1}{\det\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial z}\right)}\right| = \frac{p_Z(z)}{4}.
$$

Check with $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ in two dimensions: $x = 2z \sim \mathcal N(0, 4I)$, and indeed $p_\theta(0) = \frac{1}{2\pi\cdot 4} = 0.0398 = \frac14\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi}$.

**Orientation does not matter.** For

$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det J = -4,
$$

the square is also mirrored, but its area is still $4$. The sign of the determinant records orientation; the change of volume, and hence of density, uses only $|\det J| = 4$. That is why the formula has an absolute value.

## Stacking maps: a normalizing flow

Now apply the formula to the whole chain $z \sim p_Z(z)$, $x = f_\theta(z) = f_K\circ\dots\circ f_2\circ f_1(z)$. Name the intermediate points

$$
z_0 = z, \qquad z_i = f_i(z_{i-1}) \;\; (i = 1,\dots,K), \qquad z_K = x .
$$

The inverse runs the chain backwards, $f^{-1} = f_1^{-1}\circ\dots\circ f_K^{-1}$, and by the chain rule its Jacobian is a product of the layer Jacobians, each taken at its own intermediate point: $J_{f^{-1}}(x) = J_{f_1^{-1}}(z_1)\,J_{f_2^{-1}}(z_2)\cdots J_{f_K^{-1}}(z_K)$. Since $\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B$,

$$
p_\theta(x)
= p_Z(z_0)\,\left|\det\frac{\partial f^{-1}(x)}{\partial x}\right|
= p_Z(z_0)\prod_{i=1}^{K}\left|\det\frac{\partial f_i^{-1}(z_i)}{\partial z_i}\right|
= p_Z(z_0)\prod_{i=1}^{K}\left|\det\frac{\partial f_i(z_{i-1})}{\partial z_{i-1}}\right|^{-1}.
$$

To evaluate it we push $x$ backwards through the layers, $z_K = x \to z_{K-1} \to \dots \to z_0$, and collect one determinant per layer. This construction is a **normalizing flow**: the density *flows* through a sequence of invertible maps, and read backwards the chain *normalizes* the data, turning it into a standard normal sample. The name was popularized in machine learning by [Rezende and Mohamed (2015)](#references), building on earlier work on density estimation by transforming data towards a Gaussian ([Tabak and Vanden-Eijnden, 2010](#references)).

Sampling needs only the forward direction: draw $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and compute $x = f_\theta(z)$; evaluating a density needs the inverse. Which of the two is cheap depends on the architecture: MAF evaluates densities fast and samples slowly, IAF the reverse, and coupling layers make both cheap ([Flow architectures](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures)). Sampling turns a simple $z$ into a sample of a complicated distribution; the density formula is what lets us *fit* $f_\theta$ so that this distribution matches the data.

### Maximum likelihood

Take the logarithm and the product becomes a sum:

$$
\log p_\theta(x)
= \log p_Z(z) + \log\left|\det\frac{\partial f^{-1}(x)}{\partial x}\right|
= \log p_Z(z_0) + \sum_{i=1}^{K}\log\left|\det\frac{\partial f_i^{-1}(z_i)}{\partial z_i}\right| .
$$

This is an **exact log-likelihood evaluation**, with no bound and no sampling. Training is plain maximum likelihood, the objective of the [fitting note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl), now with a neural model: minimize the negative log-likelihood over the dataset $x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(N)}$,

$$
\mathcal L(\theta) = -\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[\log p_Z\big(f_\theta^{-1}(x^{(n)})\big) + \sum_{i=1}^{K}\log\left|\det\frac{\partial f_i^{-1}(z_i^{(n)})}{\partial z_i^{(n)}}\right|\right].
$$

The two terms pull against each other. The first wants every encoded point near the centre of the Gaussian. The second penalizes the inverse for squeezing space, so the model cannot score well by collapsing all the data to $z = 0$.

In code this is two lines: `logprob = prior_logprob + log_det` and `loss = -logprob.sum()`, where the model returns the log-density of the encoded point under the base and the accumulated log-determinant (see the [notebook](#notebook) section).

## Recap: GANs, VAEs and flows

<ModelFamilies />

- **GAN** ([Goodfellow et al., 2014](/notes/generative-models/gans)). There is **no log-likelihood evaluation**: the generator defines its distribution only through samples. There is also **no latent-variable inference**: nothing maps $x$ back to $z$, and nothing guarantees that the generator covers the full support of the data, so a real $x$ may have no latent code at all.
- **VAE** ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](/notes/generative-models/vae)). It optimizes a **lower bound on the log-likelihood (the ELBO)** and infers latents through an **approximate posterior** $q_\phi(z\mid x)$.
- **Flow-based generative models**, for example NICE ([Dinh, Krueger and Bengio, 2015](#references)) and RealNVP ([Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein and Bengio, 2017](#references)). They **minimize the exact negative log-likelihood**, and posterior inference is **exact**: $z = f^{-1}(x)$, a deterministic answer.
- **Diffusion models** complete the list; they are the last section of the course and, like VAEs, train with a bound on the likelihood.

| | Log-likelihood | Latent inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GAN | none | none |
| VAE | lower bound (ELBO) | approximate, $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ |
| Flow | exact | exact, $z = f^{-1}(x)$ |

## The trick: a triangular Jacobian

Everything above hinges on $|\det J|$, and for a general network that determinant is expensive. The trick that makes flows practical is to **ensure the Jacobian is triangular**. For a (lower-)triangular matrix

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
a_{11} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
a_{21} & a_{22} & \ddots & \vdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & 0 \\
a_{n1} & a_{n2} & \cdots & a_{nn}
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\det A = a_{11}\, a_{22} \cdots a_{nn}.
$$

To see why this matters, take three steps.

### 1. What is the Jacobian?

Let $f: \mathbb R^d \to \mathbb R^d$ be a vector-valued function,

$$
f(z) = \begin{bmatrix} f^{(1)}(z_1,\dots,z_d) \\ \vdots \\ f^{(d)}(z_1,\dots,z_d) \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its **Jacobian** is the matrix of all first-order partial derivatives:

$$
J_f(z) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} =
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial f^{(1)}}{\partial z_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f^{(1)}}{\partial z_d} \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\dfrac{\partial f^{(d)}}{\partial z_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial f^{(d)}}{\partial z_d}
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

It tells how an infinitesimal volume around $z$ gets stretched, squashed or rotated when passed through $f$. Close to $z$ the map is linear, $f(z + \delta) \approx f(z) + J_f(z)\,\delta$, so $J_f(z)$ is basically **local geometry information**: a tiny square becomes a tiny parallelogram spanned by the columns of $J_f(z)$.

<LocalVolume />

### 2. Why does the determinant matter?

$|\det J_f(z)|$ is **how much $f$ scales small volumes near $z$**:

- $|\det| = 2$: volumes are doubled;
- $|\det| = 0.5$: volumes are halved;
- $|\det| = 1$: volumes are preserved (the shape may still change);
- $\det = 0$: the volume is crushed to zero. The linear map $J$ is not invertible, so the layer has no differentiable inverse there and the density factor $1/|\det J|$ is infinite: unusable for flows, which need $\det J \neq 0$ everywhere.

<DeterminantCases />

The change-of-variables formula, written from the latent side, says

$$
p_{f(z)}\big(f(z)\big) = p_Z(z)\,\big|\det J_f(z)\big|^{-1}.
$$

This is the same formula as before with $x = f(z)$. If we can compute this determinant, we get the exact density. If we cannot, the flow is dead.

### 3. Why a triangular $J$?

Computing the determinant of a general $d\times d$ matrix costs $O(d^3)$ with Gaussian elimination or an LU decomposition ([Golub and Van Loan, 2013](#references)); naive cofactor expansion is even worse, $O(d!)$. Take $d = 1000$ (a $32\times 32$ grayscale image already has $d = 1024$): that is on the order of $10^9$ operations (about $\tfrac23 d^3 \approx 6.7\times 10^8$ for LU) for every example, every layer and every training step.

But if $J$ is triangular,

$$
\det J = \prod_{i=1}^{d} J_{ii}, \qquad \log|\det J| = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \log|J_{ii}|,
$$

which costs $O(d)$: a thousand multiplications instead of a billion. A triangular $J$ is also invertible exactly when every diagonal entry is non-zero, which is easy to guarantee by construction. If, in addition, each output coordinate depends monotonically on its own input coordinate (as in coupling layers), the whole map is invertible and can be inverted one coordinate at a time.

<TriangularJacobian />

The next question is how to build expressive layers whose Jacobian is triangular by design. The answer, coupling layers (NICE, RealNVP), is the start of [Flow architectures](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures).
