A normalizing flow transforms a simple random variable into a more expressive distribution while keeping track of its density. The transformation must support both sampling and inversion, and its Jacobian determinant accounts for the local change in volume.

## Fix the two directions first

Let the base variable and observations have the same dimension $d$:

$$
z\sim p_Z(z),\qquad x=f_\theta(z).
$$

The scan uses a spherical Gaussian as an example, $p_Z=\mathcal N(0,I)$. The associated notebook instead uses a logistic base distribution. The base must have a density we can evaluate and a sampling procedure we can use.

Throughout this note,

$$
\underbrace{f_\theta: z\mapsto x}_{\text{generation}},
\qquad
\underbrace{g_\theta=f_\theta^{-1}:x\mapsto z}_{\text{density evaluation}}.
$$

The ordinary change-of-variables framework assumes a bijective transformation with a differentiable inverse and a nonsingular Jacobian on the relevant domain. Both directions preserve the number of coordinates. An invertible flow does not compress an image into a smaller bottleneck.

## Why substituting into the base density is insufficient

Recovering $z=g_\theta(x)$ identifies which base point generated $x$. It does not yet give the density at $x$:

$$
p_X(x)\ne p_Z(g_\theta(x))\quad\text{in general}.
$$

A transformation can spread probability mass over a larger region or concentrate it into a smaller one. Probability mass is conserved; density changes inversely with volume. Locally,

$$
p_X(x)\,dx=p_Z(z)\,dz,
\qquad
dx=|\det J_{f_\theta}(z)|\,dz.
$$

Thus

$$
\boxed{
p_X(f_\theta(z))=\frac{p_Z(z)}{|\det J_{f_\theta}(z)|}
}
$$

or, in the direction used to evaluate an observed $x$,

$$
\boxed{
p_X(x)=p_Z(g_\theta(x))\,|\det J_{g_\theta}(x)|.
}
$$

The Jacobians are evaluated at different corresponding points:

$$
J_{g_\theta}(x)=
\left[J_{f_\theta}(g_\theta(x))\right]^{-1}.
$$

Consequently the inverse determinant multiplies the density, while the generation determinant divides it. This is the change-of-variables correction crossed out and restored on page 31.

## Stretch, reflect and preserve probability

Page 32 gives $f(z)=2z$ in two dimensions:

$$
J_f=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad |\det J_f|=4.
$$

Lengths double, but area becomes four times larger. The density at a corresponding point becomes one quarter of the base density. If instead

$$
J_f=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix},
$$

one coordinate is reflected, the signed determinant is $-4$, and the absolute volume factor is still $4$. The absolute value prevents a density from becoming negative.

<FlowVolumeSketch />

The one-dimensional version uses $|f'(z)|$. A rotation or a shear with determinant of absolute value $1$ preserves volume, even though it can change coordinates and shape. Volume-preserving does not mean that every point stays fixed.

## What the Jacobian contains

For a vector-valued function $f:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^d$, write its coordinate functions as $f^{(1)},\ldots,f^{(d)}$. Its Jacobian is

$$
J_f(z)=\left[\frac{\partial f^{(i)}(z)}{\partial z_j}\right]_{i,j=1}^{d}.
$$

Rows correspond to output coordinates and columns to input coordinates. Near a point $z$, a small displacement $\delta$ is transformed approximately by

$$
f(z+\delta)=f(z)+J_f(z)\delta+o(\|\delta\|).
$$

The matrix describes local directional changes; $|\det J_f(z)|$ gives the local volume scale. The source's examples on page 35 become:

| Absolute determinant | Effect on a small volume | Density at the corresponding point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $2$ | Doubled | Divided by $2$ |
| $1/2$ | Halved | Multiplied by $2$ |
| $1$ | Preserved | Unchanged by the volume correction |
| $0$ | Singular local linear approximation | Ordinary inverse-Jacobian formula fails at that point |

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial qualification.</strong> A zero determinant at one point does not by itself prove global non-injectivity: $f(z)=z^3$ is a one-dimensional counterexample, although its inverse is not differentiable at zero. Standard smooth flow layers require nonsingular Jacobians. A dimension-collapsing map such as $(z_1,z_2)\mapsto(z_1,0)$ cannot provide an ordinary full-dimensional density through this formula.</aside>

## Compose many tractable transformations

A flow stacks invertible layers:

$$
h_0=z,\qquad h_k=f_k(h_{k-1}),\quad k=1,\ldots,K,
\qquad h_K=x.
$$

The generation map is

$$
f_\theta=f_K\circ\cdots\circ f_1.
$$

Its inverse applies the inverse layers in reverse order:

$$
g_\theta=f_1^{-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_K^{-1}.
$$

The chain rule multiplies the layer Jacobians, so determinants multiply and log absolute determinants add:

$$
\log p_X(x)
=\log p_Z(z)
-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log|\det J_{f_k}(h_{k-1})|,
\qquad z=g_\theta(x).
$$

Equivalently, after recovering the intermediate points by inversion,

$$
\log p_X(x)
=\log p_Z(z)
+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log|\det J_{f_k^{-1}}(h_k)|.
$$

Every layer contributes a density correction at its own input point. Using the total inverse Jacobian or summing these per-layer corrections gives the same mathematical value.

## Maximum likelihood

For a fixed dataset $x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(N)}$, minimize the average negative log-likelihood:

$$
J(\theta)
=-\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}
\left[
\log p_Z(g_\theta(x^{(n)}))
+\log|\det J_{g_\theta}(x^{(n)})|
\right].
$$

No approximate posterior or ELBO is needed for this ordinary flow density. “Exact” means that the model density is tractable from the transformation and base, rather than estimated by a variational bound. It does not imply exact agreement with the real data distribution or error-free floating-point arithmetic.

The source's GAN/VAE/flow recap emphasizes this distinction:

| Model | Observation likelihood in the usual formulation | Code associated with an observed input |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [GAN](/notes/generative-models/gan-objective) | Generally not directly tractable from the generator | No encoder is supplied by the basic GAN |
| [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) | Trained through an evidence lower bound | Learned approximate posterior $q_\phi(z\mid x)$ |
| Normalizing flow | Tractable density with change of variables | Unique deterministic code $z=g_\theta(x)$ |

For a deterministic bijective flow, “exact inference” means recovering that code. A VAE instead uses an approximate posterior from which it can sample latent codes. A flow's recovered code need not provide interpretable semantic coordinates.

## Why triangular Jacobians help

A standard dense determinant calculation via matrix factorization costs $O(d^3)$. A triangular Jacobian instead satisfies

$$
\det J=\prod_{i=1}^{d}J_{ii},
\qquad
\log|\det J|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log|J_{ii}|.
$$

Once those diagonal entries are available, their log sum costs $O(d)$. This does not mean that the whole neural-network evaluation costs $O(d)$, or that we must construct the full Jacobian first. A useful flow architecture computes the needed diagonal terms directly.

Page 35 asks us to take $d=1000$. The corresponding complexity scales are $d^3=10^9$ for a generic dense factorization versus $d=10^3$ diagonal terms for the triangular determinant. These powers illustrate why the structural shortcut matters; they are not measured operation counts or predictions of the complete model's runtime.

<FlowJacobianSketch />

Triangularity is a determinant shortcut, not a complete invertibility condition. Nonzero diagonal entries, an invertible coordinate transformation and a usable inverse are also needed. The [flow architectures note](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures) develops coupling, autoregressive and Glow layers that address these requirements.

The source's final claim that stacking enough layers can represent arbitrarily complicated densities is an expressivity motivation. A particular finite architecture still has restrictions from its transformations, support and optimization. The [normalizing-flow review by Papamakarios et al.](https://jmlr.org/papers/v22/19-1028.html) discusses these design conditions and computational tradeoffs.
