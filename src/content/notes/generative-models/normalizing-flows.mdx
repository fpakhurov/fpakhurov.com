Let $z$ be uniform on $[0, 2]$ and stretch it four times: $x = 4z$. What is the density of $x$ at $x = 4$? The tempting answer maps back to $z = 1$ and reads off $p_Z(1) = \tfrac12$. But $x$ is uniform on $[0, 8]$, so its density is $\tfrac18$ everywhere. The same probability now covers four times the length, and the density must shrink by the same factor.

Keeping track of how a map stretches space is all a normalizing flow needs. The GANs of the previous section, up to [image translation](/notes/generative-models/image-translation), produce images but cannot say how likely an image is; a [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) can, but only through a lower bound. A **normalizing flow** pushes a simple density through an invertible map and records the stretching, so the density of every data point is exact and training is plain maximum likelihood ([Rezende and Mohamed, 2015](#references); [Papamakarios et al., 2021](#references)).

## The model: invertible maps out of a Gaussian

Draw $z \in \mathbb R^d$ from the standard normal base density $p_Z(z) = \mathcal N(z;\, 0, I)$ and produce the data by a chain of $K$ maps,

$$
x = f(z) = f_K \circ \dots \circ f_2 \circ f_1(z) .
$$

Each $f_i$ is a **diffeomorphism**: a bijection that is differentiable and has a differentiable inverse. The chain is then invertible too, with $f^{-1} = f_1^{-1} \circ \dots \circ f_K^{-1}$. Throughout the course the map goes from latent to data, $f: z \mapsto x$, so $f$ generates and $f^{-1}$ encodes. The density that the model gives to data is $p_\theta(x)$, where $\theta$ collects the parameters of the maps.

Write $J_f(z) = \partial f(z)/\partial z$ for the Jacobian matrix of $f$ at $z$: row $k$ holds the partial derivatives of output $k$, column $j$ those with respect to input $z_j$. Differentiating $f^{-1}(f(z)) = z$ and $f(f^{-1}(x)) = x$ with the chain rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_{f^{-1}}(x)\, J_f(z) &= I, \\
J_f(z)\, J_{f^{-1}}(x) &= I, \qquad x = f(z) .
\end{aligned}
$$

If $z$ had $n$ coordinates and $x$ had $m$, these would be products of an $n \times m$ and an $m \times n$ matrix. Such a product has rank at most $\min(m, n)$, while the identities need ranks $n$ and $m$, so both can hold only when $m = n$. Unlike in a VAE, the latent vector of a flow is as large as the data.

## Why not just read off the base density?

The guess of the opening, $p_\theta(x) \overset{?}{=} p_Z(f^{-1}(x))$, fails for every map that stretches or squeezes. Take $z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $x = 2z$. The guess $p_Z(x/2)$ integrates to 2 over the real line, so it is not even a density. The true distribution is $x \sim \mathcal N(0, 4)$, with density $p_Z(x/2)/2$. At $x = 1$ the guess gives $0.352$ and the truth is $0.176$: $f$ spreads the same probability over twice the length.

## Change of variables

Start in one dimension, with an increasing $f$. An increasing map keeps order, so the event $\{f(Z) \le x\}$ is the event $\{Z \le f^{-1}(x)\}$, and the two distribution functions agree. Differentiating them gives the density:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F_X(x) &= P\big(f(Z) \le x\big) \\
&= P\big(Z \le f^{-1}(x)\big) \\
&= F_Z\big(f^{-1}(x)\big), \\
p_\theta(x) &= p_Z\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)\, \big(f^{-1}\big)'(x) \\
&= \frac{p_Z(z)}{f'(z)}, \qquad z = f^{-1}(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The fourth line is the chain rule. The last one uses $(f^{-1})'(x) = 1/f'(z)$, which comes from differentiating $f(f^{-1}(x)) = x$. For a decreasing $f$ the inequality flips, $F_X(x) = 1 - F_Z(f^{-1}(x))$, and the derivative gains a minus sign that $f' < 0$ cancels. Both cases read $p_\theta(x) = p_Z(z)/\lvert f'(z)\rvert$, as the [probability course](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables#the-change-of-variables-formula) derives in full. In the opening example $f'(z) = 4$, and $p_X(4) = p_Z(1)/4 = \tfrac18$.

The formula conserves probability band by band. An interval of width $dz$ around $z$ holds probability $p_Z(z)\, dz$; $f$ sends it to an interval of width $\lvert f'(z)\rvert\, dz$ around $x$, which must hold the same probability.

In $d$ dimensions, widths become volumes. Near a point $z$ the map is close to linear,

$$
f(z + \delta) \approx f(z) + J_f(z)\, \delta ,
$$

so a small cube with edges $\varepsilon e_1, \dots, \varepsilon e_d$ goes to a parallelepiped with edges $\varepsilon J_f(z) e_j$: the columns of $J_f(z)$, scaled by $\varepsilon$. A linear map multiplies every volume by the absolute value of its determinant ([the determinant as a volume factor](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#the-determinant-how-a-map-scales-volume)), so the image has volume $\lvert\det J_f(z)\rvert\, \varepsilon^d$. It must carry the probability $p_Z(z)\, \varepsilon^d$ of the cube, which divides the density by $\lvert\det J_f(z)\rvert$.

<LocalVolume />

The [multiple integrals note](/notes/math/multiple-integrals#densities-under-a-transform) turns this picture into a derivation, by changing variables in the integral $P(X \in B) = P(Z \in f^{-1}(B))$. Its result, in our notation, is the **change-of-variables formula**:

$$
\boxed{\; p_\theta(x) = p_Z(z)\, \big\lvert\det J_f(z)\big\rvert^{-1}, \qquad z = f^{-1}(x) . \;} \tag{1}
$$

The first identity of the previous section gives $J_{f^{-1}}(x) = J_f(z)^{-1}$, and $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A$, so the same formula can be written with the Jacobian of the inverse, evaluated at the data point:

$$
p_\theta(x) = p_Z\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)\, \big\lvert\det J_{f^{-1}}(x)\big\rvert .
$$

Where $f$ expands volumes, $\lvert\det J_f\rvert > 1$, the same probability is spread thinner and the density falls. Where it contracts them, the density rises. A map with $\lvert\det J_f\rvert = 1$ preserves volume and density, although a shear can still change the shape completely. Where $\det J_f(z) = 0$, the linearization $\delta \mapsto J_f(z)\,\delta$ flattens a small cube onto a lower-dimensional set of zero volume. The map itself need not do so: $f(z) = z^3$ is a bijection of $\mathbb R$ with $f'(0) = 0$. But the identity $J_{f^{-1}} J_f = I$ cannot hold at such a point, so the inverse is not differentiable there (here $x^{1/3}$ has an infinite slope at 0), and (1) would divide by zero. Flow layers are built so that $\det J_f \ne 0$ everywhere.

### A worked one-dimensional example

<ChangeOfVariables1D />

Take $p_Z = \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and the map of the figure,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z) &= z + 0.8 \tanh(2z), \\
f'(z) &= 1 + 1.6\, \operatorname{sech}^2(2z) > 0 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the derivative uses $\tfrac{d}{du}\tanh u = \operatorname{sech}^2 u$ and the chain rule. Since $f' > 0$ everywhere, $f$ is strictly increasing and therefore invertible; its inverse has no closed form, and a one-dimensional root search finds it. The density is $p_\theta(x) = p_Z(z)/f'(z)$ with $z = f^{-1}(x)$:

| $z$ | $x = f(z)$ | $f'(z)$ | $p_Z(z)$ | $p_\theta(x)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $0$ | $2.600$ | $0.3989$ | $0.1534$ |
| $0.5$ | $1.109$ | $1.672$ | $0.3521$ | $0.2106$ |
| $1$ | $1.771$ | $1.113$ | $0.2420$ | $0.2174$ |
| $2$ | $2.799$ | $1.002$ | $0.0540$ | $0.0539$ |

Near the origin $f$ stretches space by a factor of $2.6$, so the density there falls well below the Gaussian peak; in the tails $f' \approx 1$ and the density hardly changes. One smooth increasing map has turned a single bump into two, with maxima $p_\theta \approx 0.234$ at $x \approx \pm 1.50$. In log form, at $z = 0.5$, $\log p_\theta(x) = \log p_Z(z) - \log f'(z) = -1.044 - 0.514 = -1.558$.

The band $z \in [0.1, 0.5]$ holds probability $\Phi(0.5) - \Phi(0.1) = 0.152$. Its image $x \in [0.258, 1.109]$ is $2.13$ times wider, so the average density over it drops by the same factor, from $0.379$ to $0.178$.

### Example: stretching space by a factor of 2

A linear map $x = Jz$ has the same Jacobian $J$ everywhere. For $J = 2I$ in two dimensions, every direction is stretched twice and $\det J = 4$: a unit square becomes a $2 \times 2$ square, and $p_\theta(x) = p_Z(z)/4$. As a check, $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ gives $x = 2z \sim \mathcal N(0, 4I)$, whose density at the origin is $1/(2\pi \cdot 4) = 0.0398$, a quarter of $1/(2\pi)$.

For $J = \operatorname{diag}(2, -2)$ the square is also mirrored and $\det J = -4$. The sign of a determinant records orientation; the change of volume, and with it the change of density, uses only $\lvert\det J\rvert = 4$. That is why (1) has an absolute value.

## Stacking maps: a normalizing flow

Name the intermediate points of the chain $z_0 = z$ and $z_i = f_i(z_{i-1})$ for $i = 1, \dots, K$, so that $z_K = x$. By the chain rule, the Jacobian of the composition is the product of the layer Jacobians, each taken at its own input point. The determinant of a product is the product of the determinants ([properties of the determinant](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#properties-used-everywhere)), and the logarithm turns the product into a sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det J_f(z_0) &= \det\big(J_{f_K}(z_{K-1}) \cdots J_{f_1}(z_0)\big) \\
&= \prod_{i=1}^{K} \det J_{f_i}(z_{i-1}), \\
\log p_\theta(x) &= \log p_Z(z_0) - \log\big\lvert\det J_f(z_0)\big\rvert \\
&= \log p_Z(z_0) - \sum_{i=1}^{K} \log\big\lvert\det J_{f_i}(z_{i-1})\big\rvert .
\end{aligned} \tag{2}
$$

The third line is the logarithm of (1). To evaluate (2) we push $x$ backwards through the layers, $z_K \to z_{K-1} \to \dots \to z_0$, and collect one log-determinant per layer. This construction is a **normalizing flow**: the density flows through a sequence of invertible maps, and read backwards the chain normalizes the data, turning it into something close to a standard normal sample once the model fits. The name, and the definition of a flow as a composition of simple maps, come from Tabak and Turner (2013); Rezende and Mohamed carried them into machine learning, for variational inference ([Papamakarios et al., 2021](#references), §2.4). Their own layers were planar and radial maps, whose determinants are cheap for a reason other than the one below ([Flow architectures, part G](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures#g-planar-and-radial-flows-cheap-determinants-without-triangles)).

Sampling runs the chain forwards: draw $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ and compute $x = f(z)$. Evaluating a density runs it backwards. Which direction is cheap depends on the layers. Masked autoregressive flows evaluate densities in one pass and sample slowly, inverse autoregressive flows do the reverse, and coupling layers are cheap both ways ([Flow architectures, part B](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures#b-autoregressive-layers-maf-iaf)).

### Maximum likelihood

Equation (2) is an exact log-likelihood, with no bound and no sampling. Training minimizes the average negative log-likelihood over a dataset $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}$, which estimates the forward KL divergence from the data distribution to the model up to a constant ([why cross-entropy is the loss we minimize](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#why-cross-entropy-is-the-loss-we-minimize)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\theta) = -\frac1N \sum_{n=1}^{N} \Big[ & \log p_Z\big(z_0^{(n)}\big) \\
&- \sum_{i=1}^{K} \log\big\lvert\det J_{f_i}\big(z_{i-1}^{(n)}\big)\big\rvert \Big] ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $z_i^{(n)}$ are the intermediate points of $x^{(n)}$, with $z_K^{(n)} = x^{(n)}$ and $z_0^{(n)} = f^{-1}(x^{(n)})$.

The two terms pull against each other, as the smallest flow shows. Fit $x = az + b$ with $a > 0$ to one-dimensional data. Then $z = (x - b)/a$, $\log p_Z(z) = -z^2/2 + \text{const}$ and $\log\lvert\det J\rvert = \log a$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
-N \mathcal L(a, b) &= \sum_{n} \Big[-\frac{(x^{(n)} - b)^2}{2a^2} - \log a\Big] + \text{const}, \\
\frac{\partial (-N\mathcal L)}{\partial a} &= \sum_{n} \frac{(x^{(n)} - b)^2}{a^3} - \frac{N}{a} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Setting the derivatives to zero gives $b$ equal to the sample mean and $a^2$ equal to the sample variance. Without the log-determinant the first term alone grows with $a$ forever: every $z$ is pushed towards 0, where the Gaussian peaks. The term $-\log a$ charges the model for the volume it squeezes, and so stops it from collapsing all data onto $z = 0$.

### Images: dequantization, rescaling and bits per dimension

*Optional depth: the preprocessing behind a reported number of bits per dimension. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

Formula (1) gives a density, so it suits continuous data. Pixels are discrete, $x \in \{0, \dots, 255\}^d$, and a continuous model can raise its likelihood without bound by piling its mass onto those points. In one dimension, put a Gaussian of width $\sigma$ with weight $\tfrac1{256}$ on each of the 256 values: the density at every value is at least $1/(256\,\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$, so the log-likelihood of any pixel data grows like $-\log\sigma$ as $\sigma \to 0$. Flows are therefore trained on **dequantized** data $\tilde x = x + u$ with $u \sim \mathcal U[0, 1)^d$. The probability the model gives to the discrete value $x$ is $P(x) = \int_{[0,1)^d} p_\theta(x + u)\, du$, and Jensen's inequality for the concave logarithm gives

$$
\mathbb E_u\big[\log p_\theta(x + u)\big] \le \log P(x) :
$$

the continuous log-likelihood of dequantized data is a lower bound on the discrete one ([Theis, van den Oord and Bethge, 2016](#references), §3.1).

Image likelihoods are reported as **bits per dimension**, $-\log_2 P(x)/d$, the length per coordinate of a lossless code built from the model ([bits per dimension](/notes/generative-models/autoregressive-models#pixels-as-discrete-values) in the autoregressive note). A model that spreads its probability evenly over the 256 values scores exactly 8. For a flow the reported number is $-\mathbb E_u[\log_2 p_\theta(x + u)]/d$, by the inequality above an upper bound on the discrete value.

Networks rarely see raw pixel values. With $x' = \tilde x/127.5 - 1$, roughly in $[-1, 1]$, the flow models the density of $x'$. The map $\tilde x \mapsto x'$ has the constant Jacobian $\tfrac{1}{127.5} I$, and (1) relates the two densities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{\tilde x}(\tilde x) &= p_{x'}(x') \cdot 127.5^{-d}, \\
-\frac{\log_2 p_{\tilde x}(\tilde x)}{d} &= -\frac{\log_2 p_{x'}(x')}{d} + \log_2 127.5 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Bits per dimension in pixel units are those in model units plus $\log_2 127.5 \approx 6.99$. In general, if one quantization level has width $a$ after preprocessing, the offset is $-\log_2 a$ bits per dimension ([Kingma and Dhariwal, 2018](#references), eq. 2): $\log_2 255 \approx 7.99$ for $x/255$ and exactly 8 for $x/256$. A flow that reports $-3.50$ bits per dimension on $x'$ (toy numbers) has $-3.50 + 6.99 = 3.49$ in pixel units. A negative value in model units is normal, because a density on a narrow range can exceed 1; only pixel-unit numbers compare with the [likelihood table](/notes/generative-models/autoregressive-models#how-the-likelihoods-compare) of the autoregressive note.

The noise must span exactly one level in the units the network sees. With $x' = x/127.5 - 1$ that means adding $u/127.5$, the same as adding $u$ before rescaling. Noise of width 1 in $x'$ units would smear each value over about 127 levels, and no noise at all brings back the unbounded likelihood.

A nonlinear preprocessing adds a log-determinant that depends on the pixel. Pixel values are bounded while a Gaussian base lives on all of $\mathbb R^d$, so RealNVP models the logit of rescaled pixels ([Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein and Bengio, 2017](#references), §4.1). In the symmetric form of its reference code,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= \alpha + (1 - 2\alpha)\, \frac{\tilde x}{256}, \qquad \alpha = 0.05, \\
y &= \log\frac{p}{1 - p} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

so $p \in [0.05, 0.95)$ and $y$ stays within about $\pm 2.94$. The derivative is $dy/d\tilde x = (1 - 2\alpha)/\big(256\, p(1 - p)\big)$ per coordinate, so by (1)

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\log_2 p_{\tilde x}(\tilde x) &= -\log_2 p_y(y) \\
&\quad + \sum_{i=1}^{d} \log_2 \frac{256\, p_i (1 - p_i)}{1 - 2\alpha} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The correction is about $6.15$ bits for a mid-grey value ($\tilde x = 128.5$) and $3.80$ bits for the darkest and brightest ones ($\tilde x = 0.5$ or $255.5$). Flows built for images are in [Flow architectures, part F](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures#f-flows-on-images-squeeze-masks-and-multi-scale).

## How flows compare with GANs and VAEs

<ModelFamilies />

| | Log-likelihood | Latent of a given $x$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [GAN](/notes/generative-models/gans) | none | none |
| [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) | lower bound (ELBO) | approximate posterior $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ |
| Flow | exact, (2) | exact and deterministic, $z = f^{-1}(x)$ |

A GAN defines its distribution only through samples, and nothing guarantees that a real $x$ has a latent code at all. A flow answers both questions exactly; the price is a latent as large as the data and layers restricted to cheap determinants. Diffusion models, later in the course, return to a lower bound on the log-likelihood, an ELBO.

## The trick: a triangular Jacobian

Everything above hinges on $\log\lvert\det J\rvert$. For a general $d \times d$ matrix it costs $O(d^3)$ by LU factorization, about $\tfrac23 d^3$ floating-point operations ([what elimination costs](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#what-elimination-costs)). A $32 \times 32$ grayscale image already has $d = 1024$; at $d = 1000$ that is about $6.7 \cdot 10^8$ operations for every example, every layer and every training step. For a general network even forming $J$ takes $d$ backward passes, one per row.

The way out is to build layers whose Jacobian is **triangular**. Expanding the determinant of a lower-triangular matrix along its first row leaves $a_{11}$ times the determinant of the remaining lower-triangular block; repeating the step leaves the diagonal ([properties of the determinant](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#properties-used-everywhere)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det J &= \prod_{i=1}^{d} J_{ii}, \\
\log\lvert\det J\rvert &= \sum_{i=1}^{d} \log\lvert J_{ii}\rvert .
\end{aligned}
$$

That costs $O(d)$, about $10^3$ operations instead of about $10^9$, and needs only the diagonal entries.

<TriangularJacobian />

A triangular structure also makes the map easy to invert. If $J_f$ is lower-triangular everywhere, output $i$ depends only on inputs $1, \dots, i$. If each diagonal entry is continuous and nonzero everywhere, it keeps one sign, so each output is strictly monotone in its own input. The map can then be inverted one coordinate at a time: $z_1$ from $x_1$, then $z_2$ from $x_2$ and the known $z_1$, and so on. It is a bijection of $\mathbb R^d$ when each of these one-dimensional maps is onto $\mathbb R$, as the affine maps $z_i \mapsto e^{s} z_i + t$ of coupling layers are.

## How expressive can a flow be?

Triangular maps look like a strong restriction. They are not, in the following sense: every density that is positive everywhere, with differentiable conditional distribution functions, is the image of a uniform density under a triangular map ([Papamakarios et al., 2021](#references), §2.2).

A small case first. Let $x_1 \sim \operatorname{Exp}(1)$ and $x_2 \mid x_1 \sim \mathcal N(x_1, 1)$, and map each coordinate through its conditional distribution function:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_1 &= F_1(x_1) = 1 - e^{-x_1}, \\
u_2 &= F_2(x_2 \mid x_1) = \Phi(x_2 - x_1) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The map is triangular, because $u_1$ ignores $x_2$. Its diagonal entries are the derivatives of the distribution functions, which are the densities $e^{-x_1}$ and $\varphi(x_2 - x_1)$. Their product is the joint density by the chain rule of probability. At $x = (1, 1.5)$ the diagonal is $0.368$ and $0.352$, and the product $0.1295$ equals $p(x)$; the point goes to $u = (0.632, 0.691)$. The density of this example is zero for $x_1 < 0$, so the statement above, which asks for a density positive everywhere, does not cover it as written; the construction still works on the region where the density is positive, $(0, \infty) \times \mathbb R$, which $u$ maps onto the open square $(0, 1)^2$.

The general argument follows the same steps. Write $p(x) = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_{<i})$ and define $u_i = F_i(x_i \mid x_{<i})$, the conditional distribution function. Each $F_i$ is strictly increasing in $x_i$, because its derivative $p(x_i \mid x_{<i})$ is positive, so the map is invertible coordinate by coordinate. Its Jacobian is lower-triangular with $\det J_F = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_{<i}) = p(x)$. Formula (1), applied to $F$, gives the density of $u$:

$$
p_U(u) = p(x)\, \lvert\det J_F(x)\rvert^{-1} = 1, \qquad u \in (0, 1)^d .
$$

So $u$ is uniform on the unit cube, and $F^{-1}$ turns uniform noise into $x$. Applying the standard normal distribution function $\Phi$ to each coordinate of $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ gives uniform noise as well, by the [probability integral transform](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables#inverse-transform-sampling), so $f = F^{-1} \circ \Phi$ is a flow from a Gaussian to $p$. Read backwards, $F^{-1}$ is the sampler of an autoregressive model, coordinate after coordinate; the [next note](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures#b-autoregressive-layers-maf-iaf) builds layers on exactly this structure.

## Where it breaks: separated modes

The construction promises that a flow exists. It does not promise that the flow is easy to represent. Take a toy target with two separated modes, $p(x) = \tfrac12 \mathcal N(-4, 0.5^2) + \tfrac12 \mathcal N(4, 0.5^2)$, and the one-dimensional flow from the previous section, $f = F^{-1} \circ \Phi$. Its slope follows from the one-dimensional form of (1), $f'(z) = p_Z(z)/p(x)$:

| Point | $z$ | $p(x)$ | $f'(z)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| centre of a mode, $x = -4$ | $-0.674$ | $0.399$ | $0.80$ |
| $x = -2$ | $\approx 0$ | $1.3 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | about $3.0 \cdot 10^{3}$ |
| the gap, $x = 0$ | $0$ | $1.0 \cdot 10^{-14}$ | about $4 \cdot 10^{13}$ |

The whole stretch $\lvert x\rvert < 2$ comes from $\lvert z\rvert < 4 \cdot 10^{-5}$. A network's slope is limited by its weights. By the chain rule the derivative of a composition is the product of the layers' derivatives, and with activations of slope at most 1, such as ReLU or tanh, each layer contributes at most the norm of its weight matrix. A slope of $4 \cdot 10^{13}$ needs a product of norms at least that large, for example ten layers of norm 22 each. A flow with moderate weights is far less steep: it leaves visible probability in the gap, and its samples land between the modes.

The same holds in any dimension. A diffeomorphism of $\mathbb R^d$ applied to a Gaussian gives a density that is positive everywhere: in (1) both $p_Z(z)$ and $\lvert\det J_f(z)\rvert$ are finite and nonzero. Clusters separated by empty space, or data near a lower-dimensional surface, can therefore only be approached, with $\lvert\det J_f\rvert$ growing without bound in the gaps. A base with fewer modes than the target forces some probability into the empty space between them ([Papamakarios et al., 2021](#references), §5.5).

## What you should remember

- Change of variables: $p_\theta(x) = p_Z(z)\, \lvert\det J_f(z)\rvert^{-1}$ with $z = f^{-1}(x)$; stretching volume lowers density.
- A flow is a chain of diffeomorphisms with $\dim z = \dim x$; its log-likelihood is $\log p_Z(z_0) - \sum_i \log\lvert\det J_{f_i}\rvert$, exact.
- The log-determinant term stops maximum likelihood from collapsing the data onto the centre of the base.
- A general determinant costs $O(d^3)$; a triangular one is the product of its diagonal, $O(d)$.
- Conditional distribution functions give a triangular flow to any positive smooth density, but separated modes need huge slopes.
- On images: dequantize with noise of one level and add $-\log_2 a$ bits per dimension for a rescaling with level width $a$.

## Notebook

The [normalizing-flows notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/spring-2025-long/seminars/09_Normalizing-Flows/nf.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* repository builds on Andrej Karpathy's `pytorch-normalizing-flows`, included as [`nflib`](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/tree/spring-2025-long/seminars/09_Normalizing-Flows/nflib) ([Karpathy, 2019](#references)). The notebook has no saved outputs, so it documents procedures only.

- Data: three 2-D toy sets, of which two moons is used; the SIGGRAPH set needs a `siggraph.pkl` file that the repository does not include.
- Model: a logistic base density (a uniform pushed through an inverse sigmoid) instead of a Gaussian, which changes only $\log p_Z$ in (2), and three blocks of ActNorm, an invertible $1 \times 1$ mixing matrix and a spline coupling layer with 8 bins, the layers of [Flow architectures](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures). In `nflib`, `forward` runs data to latent, our $f^{-1}$.
- Training: Adam with learning rate $10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-5}$, 1000 steps, each on a fresh batch of 128 points, minimizing the negative log-likelihood summed over the batch.
- Plots: data mapped to latents, prior samples mapped to data, and a grid warped layer by layer. The loop that promises 500 more steps of animation stops with `break` after one step. The final density plot exponentiates `prior_logprob` alone, $p_Z(f^{-1}(x))$ without the log-determinant, which is the naive guess of the opening. The model density is `(prior_logprob + log_det).exp()`.
