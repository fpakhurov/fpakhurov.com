The [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) ended with two complaints. Its samples are blurry, and its fixed Gaussian prior is only roughly matched by the codes the encoder actually produces, so prior samples can land where the decoder was never trained. This note follows two answers. The first keeps the model and changes the objective: a weight $\beta$ on the KL term decides how much information the code may carry. The second keeps the autoencoder and changes the code: a grid of discrete symbols from a learned codebook, with the prior fitted afterwards by a model strong enough to match them.

**Notation.** As in the VAE note: an image $x$, a code $z$, an encoder $q_\varphi(z \mid x)$, a decoder $p_\theta(x \mid z)$, a prior $p(z)$. $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ is the stop-gradient operator: it returns its argument in the forward pass and has zero derivative in the backward pass. A codebook is a set of $K$ vectors $e_1, \dots, e_K \in \mathbb R^D$.

## Two ways to change a VAE

The ELBO of one image has two terms:

$$
\mathcal L(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z \mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{KL to the prior}} .
$$

The first term wants the code to say as much about $x$ as possible; the second charges for every bit it says. Changing their balance is the β-VAE. Changing what a code is, a continuous Gaussian or one symbol out of $K$, is the VQ-VAE. The two are independent: one moves along a trade-off, the other changes where the difficulty of generation lives.

## β-VAE: a weight on the KL term

The β-VAE ([Higgins et al., 2017](#references)) maximizes

$$
\mathcal L_\beta(x) = \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z \mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big) .
$$

$\beta = 1$ is the plain VAE. For $\beta > 1$ the objective is still a lower bound on $\log p_\theta(x)$, because it subtracts more of a non-negative quantity than the ELBO does; for $\beta < 1$ it can exceed $\log p_\theta(x)$ and is no longer a bound. In both cases $\beta$ changes which model training prefers, not only the scale of the loss.

### Rate and distortion

Averaged over the data, the two terms have names from information theory ([Alemi et al., 2018](#references)). The **rate** is the average KL,

$$
R = \mathbb E_{x}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\varphi(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big),
$$

measured in nats. It bounds from above the mutual information between images and their codes under the encoder: the identity behind this, rate equals mutual information plus the KL from the aggregate posterior to the prior, is derived in the [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior). The **distortion** $D = -\mathbb E_x \mathbb E_{q_\varphi(z \mid x)} \log p_\theta(x \mid z)$ is the average reconstruction loss. The negative ELBO is $D + R$, and the β-VAE minimizes $D + \beta R$: the Lagrangian of "reconstruct as well as possible with at most a given rate". Every $\beta$ picks one point of the trade-off; $\beta = 1$ is the point that best bounds the likelihood.

### Worked example: one latent dimension

To see the trade-off with numbers, take one latent dimension, a standard normal prior $p(z) = \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and a fixed linear decoder $p(x \mid z) = \mathcal N(w z, \sigma^2)$ with $w = 1$ and $\sigma^2 = 0.1$. The data come from this very model, so $x \sim \mathcal N(0, w^2 + \sigma^2) = \mathcal N(0, 1.1)$. Holding the decoder fixed isolates what $\beta$ does to the encoder. Let the encoder be $q(z \mid x) = \mathcal N(m, v)$. For one $x$ the β-objective is, up to constants,

$$
J(m, v) = -\frac{(x - w m)^2 + w^2 v}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{\beta}{2}\big(v + m^2 - 1 - \ln v\big),
$$

using $\mathbb E_q (x - w z)^2 = (x - wm)^2 + w^2 v$ and the closed-form KL to a standard normal from the [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior). Setting the derivatives to zero,

$$
\frac{\partial J}{\partial v} = -\frac{w^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{\beta}{2} + \frac{\beta}{2v} = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial m} = \frac{w(x - wm)}{\sigma^2} - \beta m = 0,
$$

gives the optimal encoder

$$
m = \frac{w}{w^2 + \beta\sigma^2}\, x, \qquad v = \frac{\beta\sigma^2}{w^2 + \beta\sigma^2} .
$$

At $\beta = 1$ these are the mean and variance of the exact posterior $p(z \mid x)$, so the ELBO is tight. Averaging over $x \sim \mathcal N(0, 1.1)$ gives the rate and the squared-error distortion $\mathbb E(x - wz)^2$:

| $\beta$ | $m / x$ | $v$ | rate $R$, nats | distortion $\mathbb E(x - wz)^2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.25 | 0.976 | 0.024 | 1.89 | 0.025 |
| 1 | 0.909 | 0.091 | 1.20 | 0.100 |
| 4 | 0.714 | 0.286 | 0.55 | 0.376 |
| 16 | 0.385 | 0.615 | 0.13 | 1.032 |

Raising $\beta$ shrinks the code toward the prior: the mean is pulled to 0 and the variance grows toward 1, so the code says less about $x$ and the reconstruction gets worse. At $\beta = 16$ the distortion, 1.03, is close to the variance of the data, 1.1: the code carries almost nothing. Lowering $\beta$ does the opposite and buys sharper reconstructions with a code that is far from the prior, which is what makes prior samples poor.

### What β buys and what it does not

[Higgins et al. (2017)](#references) report that $\beta > 1$ makes individual latent coordinates track individual factors of variation (position, scale, rotation of a sprite), which they call **disentanglement**. The mechanism is the pressure to spend little rate: a factorized prior is cheapest to match with a posterior whose coordinates are independent. The effect is not guaranteed. [Locatello et al. (2019)](#references) prove that without supervision or inductive biases disentanglement cannot be identified from data, and in a study of more than 12,000 trained models find that random seeds and hyperparameters matter more than the choice among disentangling objectives. [Burgess et al. (2018)](#references) replace the fixed weight by a target rate $C$ that grows during training, $\mathbb E_q \log p_\theta(x \mid z) - \gamma\,\lvert D_{\mathrm{KL}} - C \rvert$, so that the code is allowed one more nat at a time. The useful reading of β-VAE is the one above: a dial on the rate, not a switch for interpretability.

## VQ-VAE: a discrete latent code

The VQ-VAE ([van den Oord, Vinyals and Kavukcuoglu, 2017](#references)) gives up the continuous code. The encoder still outputs a map of vectors, $z_e(x) \in \mathbb R^{h \times w \times D}$, but each vector is replaced by the nearest entry of a learned codebook before it reaches the decoder:

$$
k^*(i) = \arg\min_k \big\lVert z_e(x)_i - e_k \big\rVert^2, \qquad z_q(x)_i = e_{k^*(i)} .
$$

The code of an image is the grid of indices $k^*(i)$: for the $32 \times 32$ images below, 64 integers between 1 and 512 at the bottom level. In ELBO terms, the posterior $q(z = k \mid x)$ is one-hot at $k^*$, and with a uniform prior over the $K$ codes its KL is $\log K$ per position, a constant. The KL term drops out of training, and the VQ-VAE trains as a plain autoencoder with a quantized bottleneck.

### Training through the argmin

The argmin has no useful gradient: a small change of $z_e$ either keeps the same $e_k$ or jumps to another. The VQ-VAE uses the **straight-through estimator** ([Bengio, Léonard and Courville, 2013](#references)): in the forward pass the decoder receives $z_q$, in the backward pass the gradient with respect to $z_q$ is copied to $z_e$ unchanged. In code it is one line, `z_q = z_e + (z_q - z_e).detach()`: the value is $z_q$, the derivative is that of $z_e$. The copied gradient is biased, because the decoder's gradient is evaluated at $e_{k^*}$ and applied at $z_e$; it works when the two are close, which the loss enforces. The loss has three terms:

$$
\mathcal L = -\log p_\theta\big(x \mid z_q(x)\big) + \big\lVert \mathrm{sg}[z_e(x)] - e \big\rVert^2 + \beta_c \big\lVert z_e(x) - \mathrm{sg}[e] \big\rVert^2 ,
$$

summed over positions, with $e = e_{k^*}$. The reconstruction term trains the decoder and, through the straight-through copy, the encoder. The **codebook term** moves $e$ toward the encoder output; it does not touch the encoder. The **commitment term** moves the encoder output toward $e$; it does not touch the codebook. Without it nothing stops the encoder outputs from drifting away from the codebook faster than the codebook can follow. The paper uses $\beta_c = 0.25$ and reports that results are robust to it.

### Worked example: one quantization step

Take $D = 2$, $K = 3$, the codebook $e_1 = (0, 0)$, $e_2 = (2, 0)$, $e_3 = (0, 2)$ and one encoder output $z_e = (1.4, 0.3)$, the red point of the figure. The squared distances are

$$
\lVert z_e - e_1\rVert^2 = 1.96 + 0.09 = 2.05, \quad \lVert z_e - e_2\rVert^2 = 0.36 + 0.09 = 0.45, \quad \lVert z_e - e_3\rVert^2 = 1.96 + 2.89 = 4.85 .
$$

The nearest vector is $e_2$, so the decoder receives $z_q = (2, 0)$ and the code is the index 2. The codebook term is $0.45$ and the commitment term is $0.25 \cdot 0.45 = 0.1125$: the same distance, with gradients sent in opposite directions. If the decoder's loss has gradient $g$ with respect to $z_q$, the encoder receives exactly $g$ at $z_e$, plus the commitment gradient $2\beta_c(z_e - e_2) = 0.5 \cdot (-0.6, 0.3) = (-0.3, 0.15)$, which points from $e_2$ toward $z_e$, so a descent step moves $z_e$ toward $e_2$.

### The codebook as online k-means

The codebook term moves each $e_k$ toward the encoder outputs assigned to it, which is the update of k-means. The appendix of the VQ-VAE paper, and the notebook below, replace it by exponential moving averages. For each code keep a count $N_k$ and a sum $m_k$, update them with the $n_k$ outputs assigned to code $k$ in the batch,

$$
N_k \leftarrow \gamma N_k + (1 - \gamma)\, n_k, \qquad m_k \leftarrow \gamma\, m_k + (1 - \gamma) \sum_{i:\, k^*(i) = k} z_e(x)_i, \qquad e_k = \frac{m_k}{N_k},
$$

with $\gamma = 0.99$, and drop the codebook term from the loss. Each $e_k$ is then a running mean of its cell, exactly the k-means centroid computed online. With $N_2 = 10$, $m_2 = (20, 0)$ (so $e_2 = (2, 0)$) and a batch of two outputs $(1.4, 0.3)$ and $(1.8, -0.1)$ assigned to it, the update gives $N_2 = 9.92$, $m_2 = (19.832, 0.002)$ and $e_2 = (1.9992, 0.0002)$: a small step toward the batch mean $(1.6, 0.1)$, as a moving average with $\gamma = 0.99$ should take.

The weak point of this scheme is the one k-means has: a code that no output selects receives no update and stays where it was initialized, a **dead code**. With many dead codes the effective codebook is small. Practical fixes restart unused codes at recent encoder outputs, as in Jukebox ([Dhariwal et al., 2020](#references)), or look codes up in a low-dimensional, normalized space, as in ViT-VQGAN ([Yu et al., 2022](#references)). The notebook adds a small constant to the counts (Laplace smoothing) only to avoid dividing by zero for unused codes; it does not revive them.

### Where the prior went

Because the KL term is constant, training leaves the prior uniform, and sampling the codes independently and uniformly would give an image of random patches. The VQ-VAE therefore fits the prior **after** the autoencoder: encode the training set into grids of indices and train an autoregressive model on them, a PixelCNN in the paper, which predicts each index from the ones before it. Generation samples a grid from this prior and decodes it. The two-stage structure is the point of the method. The autoencoder handles the pixels, and the prior only has to model a grid that is tens of times smaller and made of symbols, where autoregressive models are strong; how they work is the subject of the [autoregressive models note](/notes/generative-models/autoregressive-models).

## VQ-VAE-2: two levels of codes

A single grid has to carry both the global layout and the fine texture. VQ-VAE-2 ([Razavi, van den Oord and Vinyals, 2019](#references)) uses two: a coarse top grid for the layout and a finer bottom grid for detail that is quantized after seeing the top. For ImageNet at $256 \times 256$ the grids are $32 \times 32$ and $64 \times 64$ with 512 codes each; FFHQ at $1024 \times 1024$ uses three levels. The prior is hierarchical too: an autoregressive model over the top codes, with causal self-attention interleaved with its gated convolutions, and a conditional one over the bottom codes given the top, without attention to keep the larger grid affordable. The paper also filters samples with a pretrained ImageNet classifier, a rejection step that trades diversity for quality.

### How many bits

The figure is the notebook's version for $32 \times 32$ CIFAR-10 images: a $4 \times 4$ top grid and an $8 \times 8$ bottom grid, 512 codes each. An image is $32 \cdot 32 \cdot 3 = 3072$ bytes, or 24,576 bits. Its code is $16 + 64 = 80$ indices of $\log_2 512 = 9$ bits, or 720 bits, about 34 times fewer. Sampling cost scales the same way: an autoregressive model over raw subpixels needs 3072 sequential steps per image, the two priors need $16 + 64 = 80$. At the paper's $256 \times 256$ scale the gap is $196{,}608$ subpixels against $32^2 + 64^2 = 5120$ codes.

## What changed

| | VAE | β-VAE | VQ-VAE / VQ-VAE-2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Code | continuous, Gaussian | continuous, Gaussian | grid of indices into a codebook |
| Objective | ELBO | reconstruction − β · KL | reconstruction + codebook + commitment |
| Role of the prior | fixed $\mathcal N(0, I)$, enforced by the KL | the same, with weight $\beta$ | uniform during training; learned afterwards by an autoregressive model |
| Gradient through the code | reparameterization | reparameterization | straight-through copy |
| Sampling | decode $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ | the same | sample codes autoregressively, then decode |
| Typical trouble | blur, prior holes | the rate–distortion trade-off itself | dead codes, slow sequential prior |

## Limitations

- **β moves along a trade-off; it does not escape it.** Lower distortion costs rate, and a high-rate code is far from the prior. Disentanglement is a possible side effect, not a property of the objective.
- **The straight-through gradient is biased.** It is exact only when $z_e$ sits on its codebook vector; the commitment term keeps them close but does not make them equal.
- **Codebook collapse.** Without restarts or a normalized lookup, a large share of the codes can go unused.
- **Two-stage training.** The prior is fitted to codes of a frozen autoencoder; errors of the first stage, such as a code grid that is hard to predict, cannot be fixed by the second.
- **Sequential sampling.** The autoregressive prior samples one index at a time. Smaller than pixels, the grid is still sequential; later work replaces the prior by transformers over the same codes or by diffusion in a continuous latent space ([latent diffusion](/notes/generative-models/latent-diffusion)).

## Summary

- β-VAE scales the KL term by $\beta$ and moves the model along the rate–distortion curve: $\beta > 1$ gives a lower rate and worse reconstructions, $\beta < 1$ the opposite; only $\beta = 1$ maximizes a bound on the likelihood.
- VQ-VAE replaces the Gaussian code by the nearest codebook vector. The KL becomes a constant, the argmin is crossed by the straight-through estimator, and the loss adds codebook and commitment terms, or an EMA update that makes the codebook an online k-means.
- The prior is learned after the autoencoder by an autoregressive model over the code grid. VQ-VAE-2 uses two grids, a coarse one for layout and a fine one for detail, and a prior for each.

## Notebook

The VQ-VAE part follows the [VQ-VAE-2 notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/spring-2025-long/seminars/06_Variational-Autoencoders/VQ-VAE-2.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* repository (cell numbers count every cell from 1). The notebook has no saved outputs, so it documents the procedure rather than results.

- **Cell 6**: `Quantize` computes squared distances to all 512 codebook vectors at once, picks the nearest, updates the codebook with the EMA above (decay 0.99, counts smoothed with $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$), returns the commitment distance `diff = mean((quantize.detach() - input)²)` and applies the straight-through estimator `input + (quantize - input).detach()`. The codebook is updated only in training mode.
- **Cells 7–10**: residual encoder and decoder blocks and the two-level `VQVAE`: the bottom encoder downsamples by 4 (to $8 \times 8$ for CIFAR-10), the top encoder by 2 more (to $4 \times 4$); the top code is decoded back to $8 \times 8$, concatenated with the bottom features and quantized as the bottom code; the decoder receives the upsampled top code and the bottom code. Codebooks have 512 vectors of dimension 64.
- **Cell 12**: training minimizes the mean squared reconstruction error plus $0.25 \cdot$ `diff`, the commitment term; there is no codebook term because the EMA replaces it.
- **Cells 14–20**: CIFAR-10 at $32 \times 32$ normalized to $[-1, 1]$ (the comment in cell 15 still says $64 \times 64$), batch size 32, Adam at $3 \cdot 10^{-4}$, five epochs.
- **Cells 25–27**: masked convolutions of types A and B and two PixelCNN priors with seven $7 \times 7$ masked layers: an unconditional one for the top codes and a conditional one for the bottom codes that adds an embedding of the nearest-neighbour upsampled top codes.
- **Cells 29–32**: datasets that encode every image on the fly into its code grids, and cross-entropy training of the two priors for 10 epochs each. Encoding should run with the VQ-VAE in evaluation mode, which cell 22 leaves it in; in training mode the quantizer would keep updating its codebook while the codes are being extracted.
- **Cells 34–35**: sampling: 16 sequential steps for the top grid, 64 for the bottom grid given the upsampled top, and decoding. Each step reruns the PixelCNN on the whole grid.
- **Implementation note.** `utils.save_image(..., range=(-1, 1))` in cells 12, 22 and 35 uses the argument name that torchvision renamed to `value_range`; with current versions these calls need the new name.

The β-VAE has no trained example in the course notebooks: the [VAE notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/spring-2025-long/seminars/06_Variational-Autoencoders/VAE.ipynb) defines the β-weighted loss in cell 24 but trains only with $\beta = 1$.
