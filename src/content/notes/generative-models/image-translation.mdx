Four requests that a translation model might get. Repaint this cat as a dog, and show me several dogs. Turn this horse into a zebra without training a second network to turn it back. Render a photograph from a map that says "sky here, road there, a car on the left". Make this $64 \times 64$ thumbnail sharp at $256 \times 256$.

[StarGAN v2](/notes/generative-models/stargan) answered the first with a style code and a cycle. **MUNIT** answers it by splitting an image into content and style, **CUT** answers the second with a contrastive loss between patches, and **SPADE** the third by letting the label map reshape every layer. Super-resolution meets the oldest problem of translation: the image with the smallest pixel error is the blurry average of all plausible answers.

## MUNIT: one content space, a style space per domain

### Two autoencoders with a shared content code

MUNIT ([Huang et al., 2018](#references)) assumes that an image of domain $i$ is made from two codes: a **content code** $c$ shared by both domains, the spatial structure such as a cat's pose and layout, and a **style code** $s_i$ specific to the domain, the rendering such as fur and colour. Corresponding images would be $x_1 = G^*_1(c, s_1)$ and $x_2 = G^*_2(c, s_2)$ with the same $c$. Because $s_2$ is random, one $x_1$ has a whole distribution of translations.

Each domain $i$ gets a content encoder $E^c_i$, a style encoder $E^s_i$ and a decoder $G_i$. Translation keeps the content and swaps the decoder:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{1 \to 2} &= G_2\big(E^c_1(x_1),\, s_2\big), \\
s_2 &\sim \mathcal N(0, I).
\end{aligned}
$$

A new $s_2$ gives a new output. Taking $s_2 = E^s_2(x_2)$ from a real image of the target domain gives example-guided translation, which copies the style of the example.

The shapes of the two codes do much of the separating. Content is a $64 \times 64 \times 256$ feature map for a $256 \times 256$ image, computed with instance normalization, which removes each channel's mean and spread, statistics that carry much of the style. Style is a vector of 8 numbers from global average pooling, with no layout and no instance normalization. The decoder injects the style through [AdaIN](/notes/generative-models/stylegan#styles-through-adain), with the scales and shifts produced by an MLP from $s$.

### Losses in image and latent space

MUNIT trains the encoders and decoders to be inverses in both directions. With $c_1 = E^c_1(x_1)$ for a real $x_1$ and $s_2 \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L^{x_1}_{\mathrm{recon}} &= \mathbb E\, \big\|G_1\big(E^c_1(x_1), E^s_1(x_1)\big) - x_1\big\|_1, \\
\mathcal L^{c_1}_{\mathrm{recon}} &= \mathbb E\, \big\|E^c_2\big(G_2(c_1, s_2)\big) - c_1\big\|_1, \\
\mathcal L^{s_2}_{\mathrm{recon}} &= \mathbb E\, \big\|E^s_2\big(G_2(c_1, s_2)\big) - s_2\big\|_1,
\end{aligned}
$$

plus the same three terms with the domains swapped, and least-squares adversarial losses in which $D_2$ tells real images of the second domain from translations $G_2(c_1, s_2)$. The weights are $\lambda_x = 10$ for the image terms and $\lambda_c = \lambda_s = 1$ for the content and style terms.

Image reconstruction makes each $(E_i, G_i)$ an autoencoder of its own domain. Content reconstruction ties the output to the input: the target domain's encoder must read $c_1$ back from $x_{1 \to 2}$. Style reconstruction makes the translation use $s_2$, which a decoder would otherwise ignore, as the pix2pix generator learned to ignore noise fed next to its input image ([Isola et al., 2017](#references), Section 3.1).

### Why MUNIT has no cycle loss

A cycle loss forces a translation to be deterministic ([Huang et al., 2018](#references), Proposition 5). Suppose translation is random in both directions, translations land in the other domain, and both cycles hold exactly: every output of $x_1$, translated back, returns $x_1$, and every output of $x_2$, translated back, returns $x_2$.

Let $a$ and $b$ be two possible translations of one input $x^*_1$. Every translation of $a$ back to the first domain is $x^*_1$, by the forward cycle from $x^*_1$. Every translation of that $x^*_1$ forward must then return $a$, by the backward cycle from $a$. But $x^*_1$ can also produce $b$. So $a = b$, and the translation has a single output. This is the formal version of CycleGAN's [one output per input](/notes/generative-models/cyclegan#limitations).

MUNIT keeps a weaker cycle that carries the style along (Proposition 4). Translate $x_1$ with a random $s_2$, then translate back with the original's own style $E^s_1(x_1)$, and the result is $x_1$. This **style-augmented cycle** holds at the optimum of the reconstruction losses. StarGAN v2's cycle, which returns with the input's style $E_y(x)$, is the same idea.

### Where it breaks: diversity that is noise

On edges → shoes, Huang et al. measured diversity as the average [LPIPS](/notes/generative-models/lpips#where-it-is-used) distance between outputs for the same input, and quality as the share of human raters who prefer a method's output to MUNIT's, so MUNIT itself scores 50% (Table 1):

| Method | Quality | Diversity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CycleGAN | 36.0% | 0.010 |
| MUNIT without image reconstruction | 6.0% | 0.213 |
| MUNIT without style reconstruction | 28.6% | 0.070 |
| MUNIT | 50.0% | 0.109 |
| real shoes | – | 0.293 |

CycleGAN barely varies its outputs. Without style reconstruction, many outputs are nearly identical: the decoder learns to ignore much of $s$. The most diverse ablation is the worst one. Without image reconstruction, the outputs differ twice as much as MUNIT's and are preferred in only 6% of comparisons, because artifacts make outputs differ and a diversity score counts them. A diversity number means something only next to a quality number.

## CUT: replacing the cycle with a contrastive loss

### Why drop the cycle?

The cycle assumes that the two domains are in bijection, and many are not: a photo holds more than its label map, and a horse can become many zebras. It also costs a second generator and discriminator that only check the first. CUT ([Park et al., 2020](#references)) keeps one generator, $\hat y = G(x)$, and asks for a different kind of tie: a patch of the output should tell us which patch of the input it came from. A zebra's forehead should be traceable to the horse's forehead, and to nothing else in the photo.

In information terms, corresponding input and output patches should have high mutual information. Since $I(X; \hat Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid \hat Y)$ and $H(X)$ does not depend on $G$, this is the same as making the input easy to infer from the output, the goal the cycle pursues through a second network. CUT pursues it directly, with a contrastive estimate.

### InfoNCE: pick the positive among negatives

**InfoNCE** ([van den Oord, Li and Vinyals, 2018](#references)) turns "is $v^+$ related to $v$?" into a classification. A **query** $v$, its **positive** $v^+$ and $N$ **negatives** $v^-_1, \dots, v^-_N$ are unit vectors in $\mathbb R^K$, and a softmax over their dot products must pick the positive:

$$
\ell(v, v^+, v^-) = -\log \frac{e^{v \cdot v^+ / \tau}}{e^{v \cdot v^+ / \tau} + \sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{v \cdot v^-_n / \tau}} .
$$

This is a cross-entropy for an $(N + 1)$-way classification with temperature $\tau = 0.07$. It is the multi-class relative of [noise-contrastive estimation](/notes/generative-models/energy-based-models#noise-contrastive-estimation-learning-z-by-classification), which tells data from noise with a logistic regression.

Why does a low loss mean dependence? If the candidates carried no information about the query, the positive would be as likely as any negative, and the best classifier could only output $1/(N + 1)$ for it: a loss of $\log(N + 1)$. Van den Oord et al. turn this into a bound, $I(v; v^+) \ge \log(N + 1) - \mathbb E\,\ell$, derived in their Appendix A.1 with an approximation that becomes exact as $N$ grows. A loss far below chance requires mutual information.

**Worked example (toy numbers).** A query has cosine $0.5$ with its positive and $0.45$, $0.2$ and $-0.1$ with three negatives. With $\tau = 1$ the softmax gives the positive a probability of only $0.309$, a loss of $1.176$, close to the chance level $\log 4 = 1.386$. With $\tau = 0.07$ the gap of $0.05$ between the positive and the nearest negative becomes $0.71$ in the logits, and the positive gets $0.665$, a loss of $0.408$. A small temperature makes the loss sensitive to small differences of similarity, and the hardest negative dominates the gradient.

### PatchNCE: corresponding patches, negatives from the same image

CUT applies InfoNCE to patches at several scales. The encoder half of the generator, $G_{\mathrm{enc}}$, computes a stack of feature maps, and each position of a map at layer $l$ describes a patch of the input; deeper layers describe larger patches. CUT takes $L = 5$ layers, from the RGB pixels to the fifth residual block, and a small two-layer MLP $H_l$ per layer maps each feature vector to a unit vector, as the projection head of [SimCLR](/notes/deep-learning/self-supervised-learning#simclr-two-views-an-encoder-and-a-projection-head) does.

The same encoder embeds the input and the output: $z_l = H_l(G^l_{\mathrm{enc}}(x))$ and $\hat z_l = H_l(G^l_{\mathrm{enc}}(\hat y))$. At $S_l = 256$ random positions $s$ per layer, the output feature $\hat z^s_l$ is the query, the input feature at the same position $z^s_l$ the positive, and the input features at the other 255 positions $z^{S \setminus s}_l$ the negatives:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{PatchNCE}} = \mathbb E_{x} \sum_{l=1}^{L} \sum_{s=1}^{S_l} \ell\big(\hat z^s_l,\, z^s_l,\, z^{S \setminus s}_l\big).
$$

The negatives come from the same input image. A zebra leg in the output must be closer to the horse leg at that position than to the horse's back, the sky or the grass of the same photo. Negatives drawn from other images worked worse: the encoder would then have to model differences between images that the translation does not care about, such as white against brown horses.

What stops the generator from cheating? If the output kept no trace of the input, $\hat z^s_l$ would be as close to a negative as to the positive, and the loss would sit at about $\log 256 = 5.545$ per query. The encoder learns what "corresponding" means across domains, shapes and parts rather than textures; a fixed distance between $x$ and $\hat y$ would penalize the stripes themselves.

### The objective, and FastCUT

The generator and the MLPs minimize

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathcal L_{\mathrm{GAN}}(G, D) + \lambda_X\, \mathcal L_{\mathrm{PatchNCE}}(G, H, X) \\
&\quad + \lambda_Y\, \mathcal L_{\mathrm{PatchNCE}}(G, H, Y),
\end{aligned}
$$

and the discriminator plays the adversarial game. The last term feeds real images $y$ of the target domain through $G$ and asks that $G(y)$ correspond patch by patch to $y$, a contrastive version of CycleGAN's [identity loss](/notes/generative-models/cyclegan#identity-loss). CUT uses $\lambda_X = \lambda_Y = 1$; FastCUT drops the second term and weights the first by 10. Networks and training are CycleGAN's.

On horse → zebra the FID falls from 77.2 for CycleGAN to 45.5 for CUT, with 0.24 s per iteration instead of 0.40 ([Park et al., 2020](#references), Table 1).

### Where it breaks: matching the statistics of the target

Without a cycle, CUT is free to change more of the image, and it uses that freedom to match the target domain's statistics. In the horse → zebra training photos, zebras cover 36.8% of the pixels and horses 17.9%. CUT's translations enlarge the animals to 30.8% of the pixels; CycleGAN keeps them at 19.1% (Appendix C.4). The adversarial loss sees that real zebra photos are mostly zebra, and nothing in PatchNCE forbids a horse patch from growing stripes beyond its outline.

This helps cat → dog, which needs changes of shape, and hurts horse → zebra when the background grows stripes. Internal negatives add a second cost: two patches of the same sky look alike, yet one is the other's negative, and the loss pushes them apart.

## Semantic image synthesis from a label map

**Semantic image synthesis** turns a segmentation mask $m$, a class label for every pixel, into a photo. The training data are pairs of a photo and its mask, so the problem is supervised, as for pix2pix. Two things are new: street-scene masks come at $2048 \times 1024$, and a mask is mostly large uniform regions, which the normalization layers of a standard generator erase.

### pix2pixHD: multi-scale discriminators and feature matching

**pix2pixHD** ([Wang et al., 2018a](#references)) reaches $2048 \times 1024$ with a coarse-to-fine generator, a global network at half the resolution and a local enhancer that doubles it.

Judging such an image needs a large receptive field, which a deeper discriminator buys at a cost. pix2pixHD instead runs three identical $70 \times 70$ PatchGANs on the image downsampled by 1, 2 and 4: the same window covers a four times wider region of the coarsest image, so $D_3$ judges global structure and $D_1$ fine detail. With one discriminator, the authors saw repeated patterns.

The generator must also match the discriminators' internal features on the real photo $x$ and on its synthesis:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}(G, D_k) = \mathbb E_{(m, x)} \sum_{i=1}^{T} \frac{1}{N_i}\, \Big\| & D^{(i)}_k(m, x) \\
& - D^{(i)}_k\big(m, G(m)\big)\Big\|_1 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $D^{(i)}_k$ is layer $i$ of discriminator $k$, with $N_i$ elements, and the discriminator sees the mask with the image. The weight is $\lambda = 10$, with least-squares GAN losses. It is a perceptual loss with features learned on this very data, and it needs the pairs.

### How normalization washes out the label map

pix2pixHD feeds the one-hot mask to the first layer of its generator, which follows every convolution with instance normalization. Normalization subtracts each channel's mean over the positions and divides by its spread. A mask is piecewise constant, so after one convolution most of its information sits in exactly those statistics ([Park et al., 2019](#references)).

A uniform mask is erased. Let every pixel have the same label. A convolution of a constant input is a constant away from the borders, and everywhere with reflection padding: channel $c$ holds one value $a_c$ at every position. Instance normalization divides by $\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon}$ with a small $\varepsilon > 0$, and here $\sigma = 0$, so the channel becomes $(a_c - a_c)/\sqrt{\varepsilon} = 0$, whatever the label was. An all-grass mask and an all-sky mask produce the same activations. This is the effect that made StarGAN [pad with zeros](/notes/generative-models/stargan#how-the-generator-reads-the-label).

**Worked example (toy numbers).** A mask is sky on a fraction $p$ of the pixels and grass on the rest. After a $1 \times 1$ convolution, a channel holds $a_{\mathrm{sky}}$ on the sky and $a_{\mathrm{grass}}$ on the grass. Its mean is $p\,a_{\mathrm{sky}} + (1 - p)\,a_{\mathrm{grass}}$ and its standard deviation $\sqrt{p(1 - p)}\,|a_{\mathrm{sky}} - a_{\mathrm{grass}}|$, so for $a_{\mathrm{sky}} > a_{\mathrm{grass}}$ instance normalization gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{sky:}\quad & +\sqrt{(1 - p)/p}, \\
\text{grass:}\quad & -\sqrt{p/(1 - p)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $p = \tfrac14$ the values are $1.732$ and $-0.577$, for weights $(2, -1)$, $(0.3, 0.1)$ or $(5, 4)$ alike. Each channel keeps the layout, where the boundary is and which side is larger, and loses the size of the difference. Concatenating the mask to every layer does not cure this, since each copy is again convolved and normalized.

### SPADE: per-pixel scale and shift from the mask

**SPADE** (spatially-adaptive denormalization; [Park et al., 2019](#references)) lets the mask in after the normalization, where nothing can remove it. The activations $h$ of a layer are normalized without learned parameters, with the per-channel mean $\mu_c$ and standard deviation $\sigma_c$ over the batch and all positions, as in [batch normalization](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#batch-normalization-in-training-and-in-evaluation). Then maps computed from the mask scale and shift them:

$$
\mathrm{SPADE}(h, m)_{n, c, y, x} = \gamma_{c, y, x}(m)\, \frac{h_{n, c, y, x} - \mu_c}{\sigma_c} + \beta_{c, y, x}(m).
$$

Batch norm has one learned $\gamma_c$ and $\beta_c$ per channel, and AdaIN's $\gamma_c(s)$ and $\beta_c(s)$ depend on a style vector but not on the position. SPADE's scale and shift have spatial dimensions: a different value at every pixel, set by the labels around it. A small convolutional network computes them from the mask, resized to the layer's resolution: a $3 \times 3$ convolution with ReLU, then two $3 \times 3$ convolutions for $\gamma$ and $\beta$.

A uniform mask now gives a uniform but label-specific $\gamma$ and $\beta$, so sky and grass differ. Since the mask enters every layer, the generator needs no encoder for it: it upsamples a random vector through residual blocks with SPADE, and the vector makes the synthesis multimodal.

The losses are pix2pixHD's, with the hinge loss in place of least squares. On COCO-Stuff the segmentation check on generated photos gives a mean IoU of 37.4 against 14.6 for pix2pixHD ([Park et al., 2019](#references), Table 1). In the ablation, the same decoder reaches 35.2 with SPADE and 31.9 with the mask concatenated at every layer (Table 3): what matters is where the mask enters.

## Super-resolution: why the average looks blurry

### The MSE optimum is the conditional mean

**Single-image super-resolution** recovers a high-resolution image $x^{\mathrm{HR}}$ from a downsampled one $x^{\mathrm{LR}}$, usually $4\times$ along each side, so that 16 pixels are produced for each pixel of the input. Training pairs are free: downsample any photo. The difficulty is the loss. One low-resolution image is consistent with many high-resolution ones, which differ in exactly the fine texture that downsampling removed.

The output that minimizes the expected squared error for a given input is the [conditional mean](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation#the-best-predictor), $\mu = \mathbb E[x^{\mathrm{HR}} \mid x^{\mathrm{LR}}]$, and its expected error is the total conditional variance $\operatorname{tr} \operatorname{Cov}(x^{\mathrm{HR}} \mid x^{\mathrm{LR}})$. **PSNR**, the standard score of super-resolution, is defined as $10 \log_{10}(\text{peak}^2/\mathrm{MSE})$, a decreasing function of the MSE, so the PSNR-optimal output is the same mean. When the plausible answers differ in texture, their mean is a smooth image that matches none of them.

A perfect generator cannot fix the score. Let $x'$ be a sample from the true conditional distribution, drawn independently of $x^{\mathrm{HR}}$ given $x^{\mathrm{LR}}$. Write both as deviations from $\mu$ and expand the square; the cross term vanishes because the two deviations are independent with mean zero:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\,\|x^{\mathrm{HR}} - x'\|^2
&= \mathbb E\,\|(x^{\mathrm{HR}} - \mu) - (x' - \mu)\|^2 \\
&= \mathbb E\,\|x^{\mathrm{HR}} - \mu\|^2 + \mathbb E\,\|x' - \mu\|^2 \\
&= 2 \operatorname{tr} \operatorname{Cov}(x^{\mathrm{HR}} \mid x^{\mathrm{LR}}) .
\end{aligned}
$$

An ideal sampler has exactly twice the MSE of the mean, $10 \log_{10} 2 = 3.01$ dB less PSNR, on any data. A method that produces realistic texture must lose PSNR.

In the toy example the MSE optimum is a flat grey with MSE 0.25, and a sample a sharp stripe pattern with twice that. The $L_1$ loss does no better: at each pixel half the candidates are 0 and half are 1, so every value between 0 and 1 is a per-pixel median, the flat 0.5 included.

### SRGAN: a VGG content loss and an adversarial loss

**SRGAN** ([Ledig et al., 2017](#references)) replaces the squared error by a **perceptual loss** with two parts:

$$
\begin{aligned}
l^{\mathrm{SR}} &= l^{\mathrm{SR}}_{\mathrm{VGG}} + 10^{-3}\, l^{\mathrm{SR}}_{\mathrm{Gen}}, \\
l^{\mathrm{SR}}_{\mathrm{VGG}} &= \frac{1}{WH} \sum_{u, v} \big(\varphi(x^{\mathrm{HR}})_{u,v} - \varphi(G(x^{\mathrm{LR}}))_{u,v}\big)^2 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\varphi$ is a deep feature map of a pretrained VGG19 network, of size $W \times H$, following [Johnson, Alahi and Fei-Fei (2016)](#references), and $l^{\mathrm{SR}}_{\mathrm{Gen}} = -\log D(G(x^{\mathrm{LR}}))$ is the [non-saturating](/notes/generative-models/gans#which-generator-loss-is-used-in-practice) adversarial loss. Deep features are less sensitive to exact pixel values, so the content loss tolerates a texture that differs in detail, and the discriminator pushes the output towards natural images, where an average of textures does not lie.

On the BSD100 test set at $4\times$, 26 raters scored images from 1 (bad) to 5 (excellent) ([Ledig et al., 2017](#references), Table 2):

| | bicubic | SRResNet (MSE) | SRGAN | original |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PSNR, dB | 25.94 | 27.58 | 25.16 | ∞ |
| mean opinion score | 1.47 | 2.29 | 3.56 | 4.46 |

SRGAN has the lowest PSNR, 2.42 dB below SRResNet and below even bicubic interpolation, and by far the best opinion score. The PSNR loss is of the size the argument above predicts for a sampler against the mean. PSNR ranks these methods in the wrong order, which is why perceptual metrics such as [LPIPS](/notes/generative-models/lpips) were later fitted to human judgements.

### ESRGAN: RRDB, a relativistic discriminator and features before activation

**ESRGAN** ([Wang et al., 2018b](#references)) keeps SRGAN's design and changes its three parts. The generator drops batch normalization, whose train and test statistics differ and produced artifacts, and nests dense blocks inside two levels of residual connections. The perceptual loss takes VGG features before the ReLU, since after it only 11.17% of them are active on one test image, so 88.83% are zero. The discriminator becomes the relativistic average discriminator of [Jolicoeur-Martineau (2018)](#references), which, with $C$ the raw output, estimates how likely a real image is more realistic than the fakes of the batch on average:

$$
D_{\mathrm{Ra}}(x_r, x_f) = \sigma\big(C(x_r) - \mathbb E_{x_f}[C(x_f)]\big).
$$

The generator's loss then contains the real images too: as the fakes improve, the reals look less real by comparison. Finally, **network interpolation** blends the weights of a PSNR-trained and a GAN-trained generator, $\theta = (1 - \alpha)\,\theta_{\mathrm{PSNR}} + \alpha\,\theta_{\mathrm{GAN}}$, and trades PSNR for texture at test time without retraining.

The texture these models add is plausible but invented. That is acceptable for a photo and unacceptable where details are evidence, as the SRGAN authors note for medical images and surveillance.

## What changed

The translation models of this and the two previous notes differ mostly in what keeps the output faithful to the input:

| Method | Input → output | Tie to the input | Data | Outputs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pix2pix | image → image | $L_1$ to the target | paired | one |
| CycleGAN | image → image | cycle through a second $G$ | unpaired | one |
| StarGAN v2 | image + style → image | cycle with the input's style | unpaired, labelled | many |
| MUNIT | image + style → image | content and style reconstruction | unpaired | many |
| CUT | image → image | PatchNCE between patches | unpaired | one |
| SPADE | label map → photo | mask in every normalization layer | paired | many |
| SRGAN | small → large image | VGG features of the target | paired | one |

## What you should remember

- MUNIT: a spatial content code shared by two domains and an 8-number style per domain; a new style gives a new output.
- An exact cycle with matched marginals forces one output per input; a style-augmented cycle does not.
- InfoNCE is an $(N + 1)$-way cross-entropy; a loss far below $\log(N + 1)$ needs mutual information.
- CUT: each output patch must pick its own input patch among 255 others of the same image.
- Normalization erases a uniform mask; SPADE puts the mask after it, as a per-pixel $\gamma(m)$ and $\beta(m)$.
- The MSE optimum is the conditional mean; a perfect sampler has twice its MSE, 3.01 dB less PSNR.

## Code

A NumPy version of the InfoNCE and PatchNCE losses, checked on the worked example and on random features:

```python
import numpy as np

def info_nce(q, pos, negs, tau=0.07):
    """One query: unit vectors q, pos [K] and negs [N, K]; returns the loss and p(positive)."""
    logits = np.r_[q @ pos, negs @ q] / tau
    logits -= logits.max()
    p = np.exp(logits) / np.exp(logits).sum()
    return -np.log(p[0]), p[0]

def patch_nce(f_out, f_in, tau=0.07):
    """f_out, f_in: [S, K] unit features at S sampled locations of the output and the input.
    Query s is f_out[s]; its positive is f_in[s]; the other S - 1 input locations are its negatives."""
    logits = f_out @ f_in.T / tau                  # [S, S]; the diagonal holds the positives
    logits -= logits.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    log_p = logits - np.log(np.exp(logits).sum(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return -np.mean(np.diag(log_p))

unit = lambda a: a / np.linalg.norm(a, axis=-1, keepdims=True)

# the worked example: cosines 0.5 (positive) and 0.45, 0.2, -0.1 (negatives), in 2-D for readability
q = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
cosv = lambda c: np.array([c, np.sqrt(1 - c * c)])
pos, negs = cosv(0.5), np.stack([cosv(c) for c in (0.45, 0.2, -0.1)])
for tau in (1.0, 0.07):
    loss, p = info_nce(q, pos, negs, tau)
    print(f"tau={tau}: p(positive)={p:.3f}, loss={loss:.3f}")
# tau=1.0: p(positive)=0.309, loss=1.176
# tau=0.07: p(positive)=0.665, loss=0.408

# 256 locations with 256-dim features: a perfect copy, a noisy copy, unrelated features
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
f_in = unit(rng.standard_normal((256, 256)))
for name, f_out in [("copy", f_in),
                    ("noisy copy", unit(f_in + 3.0 * unit(rng.standard_normal((256, 256))))),
                    ("unrelated", unit(rng.standard_normal((256, 256))))]:
    print(f"{name:>10}: PatchNCE = {patch_nce(f_out, f_in):.3f}")
print(f"log 256 = {np.log(256):.3f}")
#       copy: PatchNCE = 0.000
# noisy copy: PatchNCE = 1.725
#  unrelated: PatchNCE = 5.903
# log 256 = 5.545
```

The noisy copy has a cosine of about 0.31 with its positive. The positive is the most similar candidate for 255 of the 256 queries, yet the softmax gives it a probability of only about 0.2 on average, because 255 negatives with cosines near zero share the rest: the loss keeps pushing until the margin is large compared with $\tau$. Unrelated features score about $\log 256$ or slightly above, since by chance some negatives are more similar to the query than its positive.

## Next

Every translation model above needed a loss to make its inputs recoverable from its outputs, because its generators are not invertible; and no GAN can say how likely an image is. A generator that is invertible by construction would be cycle-consistent for free, as the CS236 course notes for flow-based translation ([Ermon, 2023](#references)), and it gives an exact density through the change of variables. The [next note](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows) builds such generators.
