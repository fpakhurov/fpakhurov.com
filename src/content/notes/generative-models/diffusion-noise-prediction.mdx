The reverse model needs a mean for $p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$. Page 44 rewrites that mean in terms of the noise used to create $x_t$. This gives a supervised target we can generate ourselves: sample Gaussian noise, corrupt a real observation, and ask the network to predict the sampled noise.

The network does not observe the original clean sample. During generation it receives only the current noisy state and its timestep, and its prediction determines one reverse transition.

## Replace the clean sample by its noise representation

Use the notation from the [forward-process note](/notes/generative-models/diffusion):

$$
\alpha_t=1-\beta_t,
\qquad
\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s,
\qquad
x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,
\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

Here $t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}$, and $\epsilon$ is an independent noise draw used to sample this marginal directly. It represents the accumulated corruption at time $t$, rather than just the noise of the last forward transition. Rearranging gives

$$
x_0=\frac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.
$$

The [reverse-process derivation](/notes/generative-models/diffusion-reverse#a-training-posterior-knows-the-original-example) provides the conditional posterior mean:

$$
\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)
=
\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0
+
\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t.
$$

Substitute the expression for $x_0$ and use $\bar\alpha_t=\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}$:

$$
\boxed{
\tilde\mu_t
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}
\left(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon\right).
}
$$

The coefficient of $x_t$ simplifies because

$$
\beta_t+\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})=1-\bar\alpha_t.
$$

This expression still contains the training noise $\epsilon$. Replace it by a network prediction to define the model mean:

$$
\boxed{
\mu_\theta(x_t,t)
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}
\left(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right).
}
$$

The same network is conditioned on $t$, because the amount of corruption and the correction coefficients change with the timestep.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Transcription corrections.</strong> Page 44's first recap of $\tilde\mu_t$ omits $\beta_t$ from the coefficient of $x_0$. Its direct-noising and rearrangement lines also use $\alpha_t$ where $\bar\alpha_t$ is required. The subsequent noise-prediction mean and loss use the accumulated quantity consistently. The equations above restore the factors, matching the preceding posterior derivation and the <a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11239v2#S3.SS2">original DDPM derivation</a>.</aside>

## The posterior-mean loss becomes a noise loss

For an interior transition, $t\geq2$, use a fixed reverse variance:

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)
=\mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\sigma_t^2I).
$$

The Gaussian KL from page 43 has a mean-dependent term

$$
\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}
\|\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)-\mu_\theta(x_t,t)\|_2^2.
$$

All remaining terms are independent of $\theta$ when the forward schedule and reverse variance are fixed. The two mean expressions differ only through $\epsilon$ versus $\epsilon_\theta$, so

$$
\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta
=
\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}
(\epsilon_\theta-\epsilon).
$$

Squaring therefore gives the weighted objective written on page 44:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{interior}}(\theta)
=
\sum_{t=2}^{T}
\mathbb E_{x_0,\epsilon}
\left[
\underbrace{
\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}
}_{w_t}
\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\|_2^2
\right].
$$

This is the trainable mean contribution of the interior KL terms. The full negative ELBO also contains the terminal prior KL, the final $t=1$ reconstruction term, and the variance-dependent constants. The terminal term is constant for a fixed forward schedule and fixed prior. The reconstruction term must be handled according to the chosen observation model; it is not an interior Gaussian KL with a nondegenerate posterior variance.

If reverse variance is learned, the variance terms also contribute to training, so the weighted mean loss alone no longer describes the complete objective.

## Train at a sampled timestep

The final remark on page 44 avoids evaluating every timestep for every data point. To estimate the interior sum, draw

$$
t\sim\operatorname{Uniform}\{2,\ldots,T\},
\qquad x_0\sim q_{\mathrm{data}},
\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),
$$

independently. Compute $x_t$ directly, rather than simulating the preceding $t$ transitions. An unbiased estimator of the sum is

$$
\widehat{\mathcal L}_{\mathrm{interior}}
=(T-1)w_t
\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\|_2^2.
$$

Without the factor $T-1$, the expectation is the average interior contribution. This constant scaling preserves the minimizers but changes the gradient magnitude. The timestep-dependent weights $w_t$ are a separate issue: removing them changes the relative emphasis on different noise levels.

<DdpmNoiseTraining />

The simplified DDPM objective instead uses

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{simple}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{\substack{x_0,\epsilon\\t\sim\operatorname{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,T\}}}
\left[
\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(
\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,t)
\|_2^2
\right].
}
$$

It samples the first timestep too and omits the $w_t$ weights. This is a useful reweighted training objective, not an exact evaluation of the complete negative ELBO. The paper treats the $t=1$ case through a simplified reconstruction loss. See its [simplified-objective discussion](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11239v2#S3.SS4).

The target noise is known because training creates the corruption. That does not make it uniquely recoverable from an arbitrary noisy input: many clean observations and noise draws can produce the same $x_t$. Squared-error training favors a conditional estimate of the noise. Predicting it provides a mean for the next reverse transition, rather than recovering an entire clean image in a single guaranteed step.

## Turn the prediction into a sampling step

Start generation from the model prior,

$$
x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I),
$$

then iterate $t=T,T-1,\ldots,1$:

$$
x_{t-1}
=
\underbrace{
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}
\left(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right)
}_{\mu_\theta(x_t,t)}
+\sigma_t z_t.
$$

For the sampler illustrated below, choose the fixed posterior variance for interior steps:

$$
\sigma_t^2=\tilde\beta_t
=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t,
\qquad t\geq2.
$$

Draw fresh, independent $z_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ for these steps. At $t=1$, set $z_1=0$ and return the predicted mean $x_0=\mu_\theta(x_1,1)$, following [Algorithm 2 of the original paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11239v2#alg2). This endpoint rule is a sampling convention; evaluating a discrete-data likelihood still uses the reconstruction decoder described in the reverse-process note.

Another fixed-variance choice is $\sigma_t^2=\beta_t$ for interior steps. It changes the stochastic transition. A sampling description must specify which variance it uses; the noise-prediction mean alone does not determine the complete sampler.
