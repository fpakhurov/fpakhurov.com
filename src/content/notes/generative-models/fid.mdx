The [Inception Score](/notes/generative-models/inception-score) looks only at generated images: it asks whether a classifier is confident on each image and whether the predicted classes are diverse. It never checks whether the images resemble the training data. The **Fréchet Inception Distance** (FID) of [Heusel et al. (2017)](#references) closes that gap. It passes real and generated images through the same pretrained Inception network, summarises each set of features by a mean vector and a covariance matrix, and measures how far apart the two summaries are.

Notation: the subscript $r$ marks real images and $g$ marks generated ones. $\varphi(x)$ is the Inception feature vector of an image $x$, and $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the Euclidean norm.

## The formula

$$
\mathrm{FID} = \|\mu_r - \mu_g\|_2^2 + \operatorname{Tr}\!\Big(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2\,(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g)^{1/2}\Big)
$$

- $\mu_r, \mu_g$ are the **average feature vectors** of the real and generated images.
- $\Sigma_r, \Sigma_g$ are their **covariance matrices**.

This is the definition of [Heusel et al. (2017)](#references), Eq. (6). With $N_r$ real images $x_1, \dots, x_{N_r}$ (and the same for the generated set), the two summaries are the usual sample estimates:

$$
\mu_r = \frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \varphi(x_i),
\qquad
\Sigma_r = \frac{1}{N_r - 1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \big(\varphi(x_i) - \mu_r\big)\big(\varphi(x_i) - \mu_r\big)^{\top}.
$$

In effect we approximate each feature distribution by a [Gaussian](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions), $\mathcal N(\mu_r, \Sigma_r)$ for real images and $\mathcal N(\mu_g, \Sigma_g)$ for generated ones, and compare the two Gaussians. Heusel et al. motivate this choice by maximum entropy: among all distributions with a given mean and covariance, the Gaussian has the largest entropy ([Heusel et al., 2017](#references), text before Eq. 6).

**Take the embeddings before the softmax.** The features are not the class probabilities that IS uses, but activations from inside the network. Heusel et al. use the last pooling layer of Inception v3 ([Szegedy et al., 2016](#references)) as the coding layer. In their [TTUR code](https://github.com/bioinf-jku/TTUR/blob/ec6561000644ef798b4a358846b1aee481332045/fid.py) this is the pool3 layer, which feeds the classification head: 2048 numbers per image. Class probabilities would discard most of what the network sees in an image, while the penultimate layer keeps a richer description that is not tied to the 1000 ImageNet labels.

## Reading the two terms

- The **mean term** $\|\mu_r - \mu_g\|_2^2$ measures how far apart the centres of the two feature clouds are.
- The **covariance term** $\operatorname{Tr}(\cdot)$ measures how differently the clouds are spread and oriented. It is never negative and vanishes only when $\Sigma_r = \Sigma_g$. Both facts follow from the [Wasserstein view below](#fid-is-a-wasserstein-distance-between-two-gaussians): with equal means the formula reduces to this term, so the term is the squared $W_2$ distance between $\mathcal N(0, \Sigma_r)$ and $\mathcal N(0, \Sigma_g)$. $W_2$ is a metric ([Peyré and Cuturi, 2019](#references), Proposition 2.3), so it is non-negative and zero only for equal distributions, here for equal covariances.

So **lower FID means closer Gaussian fits**, that is, closer means and covariances of the two feature clouds. **FID = 0 means equal means and equal covariances**, not identical feature distributions (see [Drawbacks](#drawbacks)).

### A one-dimensional check

For scalar features, $\Sigma$ becomes a variance $\sigma^2$, the matrix square root is an ordinary square root, and the trace disappears:

$$
\sigma_r^2 + \sigma_g^2 - 2\sqrt{\sigma_r^2\sigma_g^2} = \sigma_r^2 + \sigma_g^2 - 2\sigma_r\sigma_g = (\sigma_r - \sigma_g)^2 ,
\qquad
\mathrm{FID}_{1\text{D}} = (\mu_r - \mu_g)^2 + (\sigma_r - \sigma_g)^2 .
$$

The covariance term is the squared difference of the standard deviations. Take real features $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ and generated features $\mathcal N(0.5, 2^2)$:

$$
\mathrm{FID} = (0 - 0.5)^2 + \big(1 + 4 - 2\cdot 1\cdot 2\big) = 0.25 + 1 = 1.25 .
$$

A generator whose samples are too spread out is penalised just like a shifted one. So is a generator that is **too narrow**. A model that produces nearly the same image every time (mode collapse) has $\sigma_g \to 0$, and the covariance term tends to $\sigma_r^2$ even when the mean is right.

### The two-dimensional example in numbers

[Figure 2](#fig-fid-two-gaussians) uses

$$
\mu_r = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},\;
\Sigma_r = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0.6\\ 0.6 & 0.8\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mu_g = \begin{pmatrix}1.2\\0.6\end{pmatrix},\;
\Sigma_g = \begin{pmatrix}0.45 & -0.1\\ -0.1 & 0.35\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Both covariances are positive definite here ($\det\Sigma_r = 0.44$, $\det\Sigma_g = 0.1475$, positive diagonals). The only non-trivial step is the matrix square root. The product $M = \Sigma_r\Sigma_g$ is not symmetric. Because $\Sigma_r$ is invertible, it is similar to the symmetric positive-definite matrix $\Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g\Sigma_r^{1/2}$:

$$
M = \Sigma_r^{1/2}\big(\Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g\Sigma_r^{1/2}\big)\Sigma_r^{-1/2} .
$$

So $M$ is diagonalisable with positive eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$, and its principal square root $M^{1/2}$ has the same eigenvectors and eigenvalues $\sqrt{\lambda_1}, \sqrt{\lambda_2}$. For a $2\times 2$ matrix this gives a closed form:

$$
\operatorname{Tr} M^{1/2} = \sqrt{\lambda_1} + \sqrt{\lambda_2} = \sqrt{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + 2\sqrt{\lambda_1\lambda_2}} = \sqrt{\operatorname{Tr} M + 2\sqrt{\det M}} .
$$

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| $\lVert\mu_r - \mu_g\rVert_2^2 = 1.2^2 + 0.6^2$ | $1.80$ |
| $M = \Sigma_r\Sigma_g$ | $\begin{pmatrix}0.39 & 0.11\\ 0.19 & 0.22\end{pmatrix}$ |
| $\operatorname{Tr} M,\ \det M$ | $0.61,\ 0.0649$ |
| $\operatorname{Tr} M^{1/2} = \sqrt{0.61 + 2\sqrt{0.0649}}$ | $1.058$ |
| $\operatorname{Tr}\Sigma_r + \operatorname{Tr}\Sigma_g - 2\operatorname{Tr} M^{1/2} = 1.8 + 0.8 - 2.116$ | $0.484$ |
| FID | $2.284$ |

In practice the features are 2048-dimensional and the square root is computed numerically, for example with `scipy.linalg.sqrtm`. Unlike the symmetric root $\Sigma^{1/2}$ of the [Gaussian note](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions#sampling-from-standard-noise-to-any-gaussian), $(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}$ is the principal square root of a non-symmetric matrix. In floating point it can come back with tiny imaginary parts, which implementations discard, and when the product is singular they add a small $\varepsilon I$ to both covariances before taking the root (see `calculate_frechet_distance` in the [TTUR code](https://github.com/bioinf-jku/TTUR/blob/ec6561000644ef798b4a358846b1aee481332045/fid.py)).

## FID is a Wasserstein distance between two Gaussians

The 2-Wasserstein distance between distributions $P$ and $Q$ is

$$
W_2^2(P, Q) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(P, Q)} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y)\sim\gamma}\,\|x - y\|_2^2 ,
$$

where $\Pi(P, Q)$ is the set of transport plans with marginals $P$ and $Q$, as in the [WGAN note](/notes/generative-models/wgan#wasserstein-1-distance). It is the same family of transport distances as $W_1$, with squared rather than plain Euclidean cost. For two Gaussians the infimum has a closed form ([Dowson and Landau, 1982](#references)), and that closed form is the FID formula:

$$
\mathrm{FID} = W_2^2\big(\mathcal N(\mu_r, \Sigma_r),\ \mathcal N(\mu_g, \Sigma_g)\big).
$$

FID is the *squared* distance, so it has no outer square root. The name comes from Maurice Fréchet, who studied this distance between probability laws ([Fréchet, 1957](#references)); it is therefore also called the Fréchet distance. Heusel et al. use Dowson and Landau's closed form for Gaussians as the definition of FID ([Heusel et al., 2017](#references), Eq. 6).

<aside class="callout"><strong>Two forms of the trace.</strong> The closed form for $W_2$ between Gaussians is often written in the symmetric form $\operatorname{Tr}\big(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2(\Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g\Sigma_r^{1/2})^{1/2}\big)$. It equals the FID form. For positive definite $\Sigma_r$, the identity $\Sigma_r\Sigma_g = \Sigma_r^{1/2}\big(\Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g\Sigma_r^{1/2}\big)\Sigma_r^{-1/2}$ shows that the two matrices are similar, so their square roots have the same eigenvalues and the traces agree. No inverse is actually needed: with $X = \Sigma_r^{1/2}$ and $Y = \Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g$ the two matrices are $XY$ and $YX$, which always have the same characteristic polynomial and hence the same eigenvalues. So the traces also agree for singular sample covariances. The closed form itself extends to positive semidefinite covariances by continuity, since both sides are continuous in $\Sigma_r$ and $\Sigma_g$.</aside>

## Compared with the Inception Score

Unlike IS, FID compares the generated images with real data.

| | Inception Score | FID |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aim | confidence and diversity of generated images | closeness of generated images to real ones |
| Data used | generated images only | real and generated images |
| What is compared | each $p(y \mid x)$ with the marginal $p(y)$ | Gaussian fit to real features vs. Gaussian fit to generated features |
| Distance | KL divergence | Fréchet (2-Wasserstein) distance |
| Better is | higher | lower |

## Drawbacks

- **Gaussian approximation.** Inception features are not normally distributed: the embeddings of a typical image set have many modes ([Jayasumana et al., 2024](#references), §3.3). Two feature distributions with the same mean and covariance but different shapes get FID 0, because only the first two moments are compared. Jayasumana et al. build such a pair from a Gaussian and a mixture of Gaussians with the same overall mean and covariance (§3.2, Table 2).
- **Data unlike ImageNet.** The network was trained on ImageNet photographs. For faces, medical scans, sketches or spectrograms its features may be irrelevant to what makes an image good, and FID then measures the wrong thing. Even on natural images the features are strongly aligned with the ImageNet classes, so FID can be improved by matching class statistics without improving perceived quality ([Kynkäänniemi et al., 2023](#references)).
- **Needs thousands of images.** A 2048 × 2048 covariance matrix has $2048\cdot 2049/2 = 2\,098\,176$ free entries. With $N \le 2048$ images the sample covariance is singular (its rank is at most $N - 1$), and the matrix square root becomes numerically unstable. With a few thousand it is still noisy. The estimate is biased upward, and the bias depends on the generator, so a fixed sample size reduces the problem but does not remove it: two generators evaluated with the same $N$ can be ranked by their bias rather than by their quality ([Chong and Forsyth, 2020](#references), §3.1, Figure 2). Chong and Forsyth extrapolate in $1/N$ to an effectively unbiased $\mathrm{FID}_\infty$ (§3.2). Heusel et al. used 50 000 generated images; 10 000 or 50 000 are common.
- **Sensitive to preprocessing.** Resizing to the network's input size (the resizing filter and its antialiasing), 8-bit quantisation and JPEG compression all change the features, and FIDs computed with different pipelines are not comparable ([Parmar, Zhang and Zhu, 2022](#references), §3).

## Over which images is FID computed?

Standard FID is computed **once, over all generated and all real images**, not per class and not per pair of images. We pool every real image into one feature cloud and every generated image into another, fit one Gaussian to each, and compare the two Gaussians. FID needs no class labels, and no generated image is paired with a particular real image. (Class-conditional generators are sometimes scored with intra-FID, an FID computed separately within each class, between the generated and the real images of that class ([Miyato and Koyama, 2018](#references), §5.1), but that is a separate variant.) Because FID compares distributions rather than pairs, it needs the large samples described above. A pairwise, per-image comparison is what [LPIPS](/notes/generative-models/lpips) does.
