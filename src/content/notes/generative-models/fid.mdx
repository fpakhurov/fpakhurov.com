A generator that outputs three copied training images, one per class, earns the same [Inception Score](/notes/generative-models/inception-score#limitations) as a good generator. The score never looks at real data, so it cannot notice. The fix is to compare the generated images with real ones.

Comparing two sets of images directly is hopeless in pixel space, so the **Fréchet Inception Distance** (FID) of [Heusel et al. (2017)](#references) compares them in the feature space of the same Inception network. It passes both sets through the network, summarises each cloud of features by its mean and covariance, and measures how far apart the two summaries are.

## The formula

Let $\varphi(x)$ be the feature vector of an image $x$. For the real images $x_1, \dots, x_{N_r}$ the summaries are the sample mean and covariance,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol\mu_r &= \frac{1}{N_r} \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \varphi(x_i) , \\
\boldsymbol\Sigma_r &= \frac{1}{N_r - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \big(\varphi(x_i) - \boldsymbol\mu_r\big)\big(\varphi(x_i) - \boldsymbol\mu_r\big)^{\top} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and $\boldsymbol\mu_g$, $\boldsymbol\Sigma_g$ are the same for the generated images. As in the [Gaussian note](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions), vectors and matrices are bold. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{FID} ={}& \|\boldsymbol\mu_r - \boldsymbol\mu_g\|_2^2 \\
&+ \operatorname{Tr}\Big(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r + \boldsymbol\Sigma_g - 2\,(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r \boldsymbol\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\Big) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $(\cdot)^{1/2}$ is the principal matrix square root. Lower is better, and 0 is the minimum.

Why only a mean and a covariance? Two distributions are equal when the expectations of enough test functions agree. For bounded features all polynomials are enough: if every moment agrees, the distributions are the same. Inception features are bounded, because pixel values lie in a bounded range and a continuous network maps a closed bounded set to a bounded one. Heusel et al. keep only the first two moments, the mean and the covariance, and then treat each feature cloud as the Gaussian with those moments.

<details>
<summary>Why bounded features are fixed by their moments</summary>

Take a direction $\mathbf t$ and the projection $s = \mathbf t^\top \varphi(x)$, a bounded number, $\lvert s \rvert \le b$. Its moment generating function is a power series in its moments, because the series of $e^{\tau s}$ converges uniformly when $s$ is bounded and may be integrated term by term:

$$
\mathbb E\, e^{\tau s} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{\tau^k}{k!}\, \mathbb E\, s^k .
$$

Each $\mathbb E\, s^k$ is a combination of moments of $\varphi(x)$ of order $k$. If two feature distributions share all moments, every projection has the same moment generating function under both, finite for every $\tau$. A moment generating function that is finite near 0 determines the distribution ([Moment and characteristic functions](/notes/probability/expectation-and-variance#moment-and-characteristic-functions)), so every projection has the same distribution under both. A distribution on $\mathbb R^d$ is fixed by the distributions of all its one-dimensional projections, the Cramér–Wold theorem ([Billingsley, 1995](#references)), so the two feature distributions are equal.

Without a bound the argument fails at its first step: the moment generating function may be infinite for every $\tau > 0$, and then equal moments do not force equal distributions.

</details>

Among all distributions with a given mean and covariance the Gaussian has the largest entropy, so the fit adds no structure beyond the two moments.

<details>
<summary>Why the Gaussian has the largest entropy</summary>

Let $p$ be any density with mean $\boldsymbol\mu$ and covariance $\boldsymbol\Sigma$, and $g = \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)$. The cross-entropy of $g$ under $p$ needs only these two moments:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\mathbb E_p \log g
&= \tfrac12 \log\det(2\pi\boldsymbol\Sigma) \\
&\quad + \tfrac12\, \mathbb E_p (\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)^\top \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu) \\
&= \tfrac12 \log\det(2\pi\boldsymbol\Sigma) + \tfrac12 \operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} \boldsymbol\Sigma) \\
&= \tfrac12 \log\det(2\pi e\, \boldsymbol\Sigma) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second step writes the quadratic form as a trace, $\mathbf a^\top \mathbf B \mathbf a = \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf B \mathbf a \mathbf a^\top)$, and moves the expectation inside the trace by linearity. The result is the same for $p = g$, so it equals $H(g)$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
H(p) &= -\mathbb E_p \log g - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, g) \\
&= H(g) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, g) \le H(g) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with equality only for $p = g$, since a [KL divergence between densities](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#kullbackleibler-divergence) is non-negative and zero only for equal densities.

</details>

The features are internal activations: the class probabilities that IS uses would discard most of what the network sees. FID takes the last pooling layer of Inception v3 ([Szegedy et al., 2016](#references)), the 2048 numbers per image that feed the classification head. The real statistics are computed over the whole training set and the generated ones over 50,000 samples.

## Reading the two terms

The **mean term** $\|\boldsymbol\mu_r - \boldsymbol\mu_g\|_2^2$ measures how far apart the centres of the two clouds sit. The **covariance term** measures how differently they are spread and oriented; it is never negative and vanishes only when $\boldsymbol\Sigma_r = \boldsymbol\Sigma_g$, which follows from the Wasserstein view below.

So FID = 0 means equal means and equal covariances of the features, which is weaker than equal feature distributions.

<FidTwoGaussians />

### One dimension

For scalar features the covariances are variances, the matrix root is an ordinary root, $\sqrt{\sigma_r^2 \sigma_g^2} = \sigma_r \sigma_g$, and the trace disappears:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_r^2 + \sigma_g^2 - 2\sigma_r \sigma_g &= (\sigma_r - \sigma_g)^2 , \\
\mathrm{FID}_{1\mathrm D} &= (\mu_r - \mu_g)^2 + (\sigma_r - \sigma_g)^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The covariance term is the squared difference of the standard deviations. Real features $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ against generated features $\mathcal N(0.5, 2^2)$ give

$$
\mathrm{FID} = 0.5^2 + (1 - 2)^2 = 1.25 .
$$

A generator that is too spread out is penalised just like a shifted one, and so is one that is too narrow. A model that produces nearly the same image every time has $\sigma_g \to 0$, and the covariance term tends to $\sigma_r^2$ even when the mean is right.

For isotropic covariances $\sigma_r^2 \mathbf I$ and $\sigma_g^2 \mathbf I$ in $d$ dimensions the root is $\sigma_r \sigma_g \mathbf I$, and the covariance term is $d(\sigma_r - \sigma_g)^2$. The figure below takes $d = 2$, $\sigma_r = 1$, $\sigma_g = s$ and shifts the mean by $m$, so $\mathrm{FID} = m^2 + 2(1 - s)^2$.

### Two dimensions, in numbers

The two Gaussians of the earlier figure have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol\mu_r &= (0, 0)^\top , &
\boldsymbol\Sigma_r &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix} , \\
\boldsymbol\mu_g &= (1.2, 0.6)^\top , &
\boldsymbol\Sigma_g &= \begin{pmatrix} 0.45 & -0.1 \\ -0.1 & 0.35 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The only real work is the trace of the root of $\mathbf M = \boldsymbol\Sigma_r \boldsymbol\Sigma_g$. This product is not symmetric, but $\mathbf M = \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2} \big(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2} \boldsymbol\Sigma_g \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2}\big) \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{-1/2}$ is similar to the symmetric positive-definite matrix in the brackets, so its eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are positive and $\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf M^{1/2} = \sqrt{\lambda_1} + \sqrt{\lambda_2}$. Squaring that sum, and using that the eigenvalues add up to the [trace and multiply to the determinant](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#trace-and-determinant-from-the-eigenvalues),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf M^{1/2}
&= \sqrt{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + 2\sqrt{\lambda_1 \lambda_2}} \\
&= \sqrt{\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf M + 2\sqrt{\det \mathbf M}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| mean term $1.2^2 + 0.6^2$ | $1.80$ |
| $\mathbf M = \boldsymbol\Sigma_r \boldsymbol\Sigma_g$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 0.39 & 0.11 \\ 0.19 & 0.22 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| $\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf M$ and $\det \mathbf M$ | $0.61$ and $0.0649$ |
| $\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf M^{1/2} = \sqrt{0.61 + 2\sqrt{0.0649}}$ | $1.058$ |
| covariance term $1.8 + 0.8 - 2 \cdot 1.058$ | $0.484$ |
| FID | $2.284$ |

The shift of the means dominates: most of the distance comes from where the clouds sit, a fifth from their shapes.

With 2048 features the root is computed numerically, for example with `scipy.linalg.sqrtm`. In floating point it can come back with tiny imaginary parts, which implementations drop. When too few images make the covariances nearly singular, they add a small $\varepsilon \mathbf I$ to both and try again.

## FID is a Wasserstein distance between two Gaussians

The **2-Wasserstein distance** between distributions $P$ and $Q$ on $\mathbb R^d$ with finite second moments is the cheapest way to move the mass of one onto the other, when moving mass by a distance $t$ costs $t^2$:

$$
W_2^2(P, Q) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(P, Q)} \mathbb E_{(u, v) \sim \gamma} \|u - v\|_2^2 .
$$

Here $\Pi(P, Q)$ is the set of transport plans, joint distributions of $(u, v)$ whose marginals are $P$ and $Q$. For two Gaussians the infimum has a closed form, and it is the FID formula ([Isachenko, 2025](#references), lecture 6; [Конспект курса «Байесовские методы в анализе данных», 2026](#references), лекция 5):

$$
\mathrm{FID} = W_2^2\Big(\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_r, \boldsymbol\Sigma_r),\ \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_g, \boldsymbol\Sigma_g)\Big) .
$$

FID is the *squared* distance, with no outer root. The same distance is also named after Maurice Fréchet, hence the F. The derivation below needs only means, covariances and one matrix inequality. The idea: the cost of a plan depends on it only through the cross-covariance $\mathbf C$ of $u$ and $v$, so the best plan is the one that correlates $u$ and $v$ as strongly as the two covariances allow.

<details>
<summary>Derivation of the closed form</summary>

Assume both covariances are invertible. Write $u = \boldsymbol\mu_r + u_0$ and $v = \boldsymbol\mu_g + v_0$ with centred $u_0, v_0$, and let $\mathbf C = \mathbb E\, u_0 v_0^\top$. For any plan,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E \|u - v\|^2
&= \|\boldsymbol\mu_r - \boldsymbol\mu_g\|^2 + \mathbb E \|u_0 - v_0\|^2 \\
&= \|\boldsymbol\mu_r - \boldsymbol\mu_g\|^2 + \operatorname{Tr} \boldsymbol\Sigma_r \\
&\quad + \operatorname{Tr} \boldsymbol\Sigma_g - 2 \operatorname{Tr} \mathbf C .
\end{aligned}
$$

The cross term of the first line vanishes because $u_0 - v_0$ has mean zero; the second line expands the square, $\mathbb E\, u_0^\top v_0 = \operatorname{Tr} \mathbf C$. So the best plan maximises $\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf C$.

**Which $\mathbf C$ are possible.** The joint covariance of $(u_0, v_0)$ must be positive semidefinite. Conversely, any $\mathbf C$ that keeps it so is the cross-covariance of a jointly Gaussian pair, whose [marginals](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals) are the two Gaussians. Substitute $\mathbf C = \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2} \mathbf K \boldsymbol\Sigma_g^{1/2}$ and the vectors $\boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{-1/2} \mathbf a$, $\boldsymbol\Sigma_g^{-1/2} \mathbf b$ into the quadratic form of the joint covariance:

$$
\|\mathbf a\|^2 + 2\, \mathbf a^\top \mathbf K \mathbf b + \|\mathbf b\|^2 \ge 0
\quad \text{for all } \mathbf a, \mathbf b .
$$

With $\mathbf a = -\mathbf K \mathbf b$ this reads $\|\mathbf b\|^2 \ge \|\mathbf K \mathbf b\|^2$, so $\mathbf K$ stretches no vector: its largest singular value is at most 1. Conversely, if it is, Cauchy–Schwarz gives $2\, \mathbf a^\top \mathbf K \mathbf b \ge -2 \|\mathbf a\| \|\mathbf b\|$, and the form is at least $(\|\mathbf a\| - \|\mathbf b\|)^2 \ge 0$.

**The best $\mathbf K$.** Let $\mathbf B = \boldsymbol\Sigma_g^{1/2} \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2}$ with [singular value decomposition](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition#what-the-singular-values-measure) $\mathbf B = \mathbf U \mathbf S \mathbf V^\top$. With $\mathbf N = \mathbf V^\top \mathbf K \mathbf U$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf C
&= \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf K \mathbf B)
= \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf N \mathbf S) \\
&= \textstyle\sum_i s_i N_{ii}
\le \sum_i s_i .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first equality moves $\boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2}$ around the trace (cyclic property), the second does the same with $\mathbf V$. $\mathbf N$ stretches no vector either, since $\mathbf U$ and $\mathbf V$ are orthogonal, so $N_{ii} = \mathbf e_i^\top \mathbf N \mathbf e_i \le 1$. The choice $\mathbf K = \mathbf V \mathbf U^\top$ gives $\mathbf N = \mathbf I$ and reaches the bound.

**The value.** The singular values of $\mathbf B$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $\mathbf B^\top \mathbf B = \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2} \boldsymbol\Sigma_g \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2}$, so

$$
\max \operatorname{Tr} \mathbf C
= \operatorname{Tr}\big(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2} \boldsymbol\Sigma_g \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2}\big)^{1/2} ,
$$

which by the callout below equals $\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r \boldsymbol\Sigma_g)^{1/2}$. Putting it into the cost gives the FID formula.

</details>

Two facts used earlier follow. The covariance term is the smallest expected squared distance between centred $u_0$ and $v_0$, so it is never negative. It is zero only if the best plan has $u_0 = v_0$ with probability 1, which makes the two centred distributions equal and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_r = \boldsymbol\Sigma_g$. The [Wasserstein GAN](/notes/generative-models/wgan#wasserstein-1-distance), later in the course, trains with the cost $t$ instead of $t^2$.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Two forms of the trace.</strong> Many texts write the covariance term with $(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2} \boldsymbol\Sigma_g \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2})^{1/2}$. It gives the same trace: with $\mathbf X = \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2}$ and $\mathbf Y = \boldsymbol\Sigma_r^{1/2} \boldsymbol\Sigma_g$ the two matrices under the root are $\mathbf X \mathbf Y$ and $\mathbf Y \mathbf X$, which always have the same eigenvalues, even for singular covariances.</aside>

## Compared with the Inception Score

FID repairs the main blind spot of IS by using real data, and it reads richer features than class probabilities.

| | Inception Score | FID |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data used | generated images only | real and generated images |
| What is compared | each $p(y \mid x)$ with the marginal $p(y)$ | a Gaussian fit to real features with one fit to generated features |
| Distance | KL divergence | squared Fréchet ($W_2$) distance |
| Better is | higher | lower |
| Copies of three training images | score like a good generator | score badly: the features sit on three points |

Heusel et al. also checked that FID follows visible damage. They corrupted CelebA face images with increasing Gaussian noise, blur, black rectangles, swirls and salt-and-pepper noise, and replaced growing fractions of them with ImageNet images. FID rose steadily with every kind of damage, and it tracked the damage more consistently than IS.

## Over which images is FID computed?

Standard FID is computed once, over all generated and all real images: every real image goes into one feature cloud and every generated image into another. It needs no class labels, and no generated image is paired with a real one. Because it compares distributions, it needs many images, as the next section shows. A comparison of two individual images is a different tool, [LPIPS](/notes/generative-models/lpips).

## Where FID breaks \{#drawbacks\}

### Same moments, different shapes

FID sees only two moments. Take real one-dimensional features $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ and a generator whose features have two bumps, an equal mixture of $\mathcal N(-0.8, 0.6^2)$ and $\mathcal N(0.8, 0.6^2)$. The mixture has mean 0 and variance $0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 1$, so

$$
\mathrm{FID} = 0^2 + (1 - 1)^2 = 0 .
$$

The two distributions look nothing alike: one peak against two, with a dip where the real data are densest. The Code section confirms the zero on 20,000 samples. Real Inception features are far from Gaussian, with many modes ([Jayasumana et al., 2024](#references)) and, after the ReLU, about 2% of their components exactly zero ([Bińkowski et al., 2018](#references)), so two very different feature clouds can share their first two moments.

### The estimate is biased

The estimate of FID from samples is biased upward. Draw both sets from the *same* 64-dimensional Gaussian, so the true FID is 0:

| Images per set | Mean FID estimate |
| --- | --- |
| 200 | 10.98 |
| 1,000 | 2.19 |
| 5,000 | 0.44 |

Here the truth is 0 and every estimate is itself a squared distance between two fitted Gaussians, so no estimate can fall below the truth ([Bińkowski et al., 2018](#references), footnote 10). For a true FID above 0 a single estimate can land on either side; the bias says that the *average* over repeated samples lies above the truth. Two arguments, both folded below, show why: the noise in the estimated means and covariances can only raise the average, and in this test the excess is close to

$$
\frac{2d}{N} + \frac{d(d+1)}{2N} = \frac{d(d+5)}{2N} ,
$$

which for $d = 64$ gives $11.04$, $2.21$ and $0.44$ against the measured $10.98$, $2.19$ and $0.44$. With 2048 features the effect is larger: comparing the CIFAR-10 training and test sets, two samples of one distribution, Bińkowski et al. still get an FID of about 8.1 with all 10,000 test images.

<details>
<summary>Why the average estimate lies above the truth</summary>

In this fold a hat marks the sample statistics of the formula section, and plain symbols the true mean and covariance of each feature distribution.

**The mean term.** The difference of sample means is $\boldsymbol\mu_r - \boldsymbol\mu_g + \mathbf e$, where the noise $\mathbf e$ has mean zero and, since the two sets are independent, covariance $\boldsymbol\Sigma_r / N_r + \boldsymbol\Sigma_g / N_g$ (the variance of an average). The cross term has mean zero, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E \|\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_r - \hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_g\|^2
&= \|\boldsymbol\mu_r - \boldsymbol\mu_g\|^2 + \mathbb E \|\mathbf e\|^2 \\
&= \|\boldsymbol\mu_r - \boldsymbol\mu_g\|^2 \\
&\quad + \frac{\operatorname{Tr} \boldsymbol\Sigma_r}{N_r} + \frac{\operatorname{Tr} \boldsymbol\Sigma_g}{N_g} .
\end{aligned}
$$

**The covariance term.** The factor $1/(N - 1)$ makes each sample covariance unbiased, so $\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_r + \hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_g)$ is right on average. The root term $F(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r, \boldsymbol\Sigma_g) = \operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r \boldsymbol\Sigma_g)^{1/2}$ is the largest $\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf C$ over the cross-covariances allowed in the derivation above. If $\mathbf C_1$ is allowed for one pair of covariances and $\mathbf C_2$ for another, their average is allowed for the averaged pair, because an average of positive semidefinite joint covariances is positive semidefinite. So $F$ is jointly concave, and Jensen's inequality for a concave function gives

$$
\mathbb E\, F(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_r, \hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}_g)
\le F(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r, \boldsymbol\Sigma_g) .
$$

The term enters FID with the factor $-2$, so it too can only raise the average.

**The size in the test.** Take $\boldsymbol\Sigma_r = \boldsymbol\Sigma_g = \mathbf I$ and $N$ images per set. The mean term adds $2d/N$. Write the estimates as $\mathbf I + \mathbf X$ and $\mathbf I + \mathbf Y$ with small symmetric $\mathbf X, \mathbf Y$, and keep terms up to second order. The Taylor series $(\mathbf I + \mathbf Z)^{1/2} = \mathbf I + \mathbf Z/2 - \mathbf Z^2/8 + \dots$ gives $(\mathbf I + \mathbf X)^{1/2} = \mathbf I + \mathbf X/2 - \mathbf X^2/8$, and multiplying out,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(\mathbf I + \mathbf X)^{1/2} (\mathbf I + \mathbf Y) (\mathbf I + \mathbf X)^{1/2} = \mathbf I + \mathbf Z , \\
&\mathbf Z = \mathbf X + \mathbf Y + \tfrac12 (\mathbf X \mathbf Y + \mathbf Y \mathbf X) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the two $\mathbf X^2/8$ terms cancel the $\mathbf X^2/4$ from the product of the halves. The same series once more, with $\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf Z^2 \approx \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf X + \mathbf Y)^2$ to second order and $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf X\mathbf Y + \mathbf Y\mathbf X) = 2 \operatorname{Tr} \mathbf X \mathbf Y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{Tr}(2\mathbf I + \mathbf X + \mathbf Y) - 2 \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf I + \mathbf Z)^{1/2} \\
&\quad\approx -\operatorname{Tr} \mathbf X \mathbf Y + \tfrac14 \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf X + \mathbf Y)^2 \\
&\quad= \tfrac14 \operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf X - \mathbf Y)^2
= \tfrac14 \|\mathbf X - \mathbf Y\|_F^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first-order terms $\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf X + \mathbf Y)$ cancelled, which is why the bias is quadratic in the noise.

Ignoring the estimated mean, each entry of $\mathbf X$ is an average of $N$ independent products $z_i z_j - \delta_{ij}$ of standard normal coordinates. For $i \ne j$ the product has variance $\mathbb E z_i^2\, \mathbb E z_j^2 = 1$; for $i = j$, $\mathbb E z^4 - 1 = 3 - 1 = 2$. The fourth moment is integration by parts: the standard normal density $\phi$ has $z \phi(z) = -\phi'(z)$, so $\mathbb E z^4 = \int z^3 \cdot z \phi(z)\, dz = \int 3 z^2 \phi(z)\, dz = 3$. Summing over the $d^2$ entries of the independent $\mathbf X$ and $\mathbf Y$,

$$
\tfrac14\, \mathbb E \|\mathbf X - \mathbf Y\|_F^2
\approx \tfrac14 \cdot 2 \cdot \frac{d(d - 1) + 2d}{N}
= \frac{d(d + 1)}{2N} .
$$

Both terms fall like $1/N$, and the covariance term, of order $d^2/N$, dominates.

</details>

Evaluations therefore fix the number of generated images, typically 5,000 or 50,000 ([Конспект курса «Байесовские методы в анализе данных», 2026](#references), лекция 5), and an FID computed at one size cannot be compared with one computed at the other. A fixed size does not cure the problem, though, because the bias depends on the generator. Two generators evaluated with the same $N$ can be ranked by their bias instead of their quality ([Chong and Forsyth, 2020](#references)). Bińkowski et al. construct cases where the ranking flips, even for one-dimensional Gaussians, and one in 2048 dimensions where 50,000 samples still give the wrong order. Below 2049 images the 2048-dimensional covariance is singular outright: the $N$ centred feature vectors sum to zero, so they span at most $N - 1$ dimensions. Chong and Forsyth extrapolate the estimate in $1/N$ to an effectively unbiased $\mathrm{FID}_\infty$; [KID](#kernel-inception-distance-an-unbiased-alternative) avoids the bias by construction.

### What the features see

The network was trained to classify ImageNet photographs. Its features are so close to the class predictions that FID can be lowered by matching the generated images' ImageNet class statistics to the real ones, without any visible improvement ([Kynkäänniemi et al., 2023](#references)). For data far from ImageNet, such as medical images, sketches or spectrograms, nothing guarantees that the features capture what makes an image good. One remedy is a [custom encoder](#fid-with-a-custom-encoder).

### One number for two failures

A worse FID can come from unrealistic samples, which move and reshape the feature cloud, or from too little variety, which shrinks it. In the setting of the shift-and-shrink figure, a shift by $m = 0.85$ and a shrink to $s = 0.4$ both give $\mathrm{FID} \approx 0.72$, so two generators with equal scores can fail in opposite ways. [Precision and recall](/notes/generative-models/precision-recall) report the two separately.

### Copies and preprocessing

The real statistics come from the training set, so a generator that returns randomly drawn training images reproduces their mean and covariance and gets an FID near 0. Detecting copies needs a per-image comparison, such as a nearest-neighbour search under [LPIPS](/notes/generative-models/lpips#where-it-is-used).

Low-level preprocessing also moves the score. The resizing filter, 8-bit quantisation and JPEG compression all change the features, so FIDs computed with different pipelines are not comparable ([Parmar, Zhang and Zhu, 2022](#references)).

## FID with a custom encoder

Nothing in the formula depends on Inception: it needs one feature vector per image, and any encoder can supply it. For data unlike ImageNet, an encoder trained on the domain is the natural choice, for example the encoder of an [autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders#emergent-structure) trained on that data; Bińkowski et al. score MNIST models with the features of a small MNIST classifier. The price is comparability. Strictly, the result is a Fréchet distance in the features of the named encoder and layer. It can be compared only with values computed with the same encoder, layer and preprocessing, never with published FIDs.

Before trusting a new encoder, test it on real images only. Split a labelled dataset by class, fit a Gaussian to the features of each class, and compute the distance between every pair of classes. The $K \times K$ matrix should have a zero diagonal up to numerical noise and be symmetric, since the formula does not change when the two sets swap places (the callout above shows $\operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol\Sigma_r \boldsymbol\Sigma_g)^{1/2} = \operatorname{Tr}(\boldsymbol\Sigma_g \boldsymbol\Sigma_r)^{1/2}$ the same way). If the features carry meaning, similar classes should be closer than dissimilar ones: on CIFAR-10, airplane should be closer to ship than to frog, and truck closer to automobile than to dog.

The sample size bites harder here. CIFAR-10 has 1,000 test images per class, so each class covariance has rank at most 999, by the same count as above, and an encoder with a code of 1,000 numbers or more gives singular covariances. A small $\varepsilon \mathbf I$, such as $10^{-6} \mathbf I$, stabilises the root at the cost of a small bias; a smaller code avoids the problem.

## Kernel Inception Distance: an unbiased alternative

The **Kernel Inception Distance** (KID) of [Bińkowski et al. (2018)](#references) keeps the Inception features and drops the Gaussian fit. It is the squared **maximum mean discrepancy** (MMD) between the real and the generated features. For distributions $P$ and $Q$ and a kernel $k$,

$$
\mathrm{MMD}^2(P, Q) = \mathbb E\, k(u, u') + \mathbb E\, k(v, v') - 2\, \mathbb E\, k(u, v) ,
$$

with $u, u' \sim P$ and $v, v' \sim Q$ independent. The first two terms measure how similar points are within each set, the last how similar they are across the sets. KID uses the cubic polynomial kernel

$$
k(u, v) = \Big(\frac1d\, u^{\top} v + 1\Big)^3 ,
$$

where $d$ is the feature dimension. Expanding the cube writes $k$ as a positive combination of $(u^\top v)^j$ for $j = 0, \dots, 3$, with weights $c_j = \binom 3j d^{-j}$. Each power is an inner product of tensor powers, $(u^\top v)^j = \langle u^{\otimes j}, v^{\otimes j} \rangle$, so with the moment tensors $\mathbf m_j^P = \mathbb E_P\, u^{\otimes j}$, linearity of expectation and the independence of $u$ and $u'$, which turns $\mathbb E \langle u^{\otimes j}, u'^{\otimes j} \rangle$ into $\|\mathbf m_j^P\|^2$,

$$
\mathrm{MMD}^2(P, Q) = \sum_{j=1}^{3} c_j \big\|\mathbf m_j^P - \mathbf m_j^Q\big\|^2 .
$$

The $j = 0$ term cancels. So $\mathrm{MMD}^2$ is zero exactly when the moments up to order three agree: the mean, the covariance and the third moments, skewness among them. It assumes no parametric form.

With real features $u_1, \dots, u_m$ and generated features $v_1, \dots, v_n$, KID is estimated by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\mathrm{KID}} ={}& \frac{1}{m(m-1)} \sum_{i \ne j} k(u_i, u_j) \\
&+ \frac{1}{n(n-1)} \sum_{i \ne j} k(v_i, v_j) \\
&- \frac{2}{mn} \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n k(u_i, v_j) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Leaving out the terms $i = j$ makes each average an unbiased estimate of its expectation, because $u_i$ and $u_j$ are independent draws from $P$ when $i \ne j$, so $\widehat{\mathrm{KID}}$ is unbiased at every sample size ([Gretton et al., 2012](#references)). In the 64-dimensional test above it averages $+0.0018$, $-0.0009$ and $+0.00001$ at the three sizes: zero up to noise, and sometimes slightly negative, which an unbiased estimate of a value near 0 must be. No estimator of FID is unbiased for all distributions, at any sample size ([Bińkowski et al., 2018](#references), Appendix D.3). The reason: along a mixture $(1 - \alpha) P_0 + \alpha P_1$, the expected value of any estimator from $N$ samples is a polynomial in $\alpha$ of degree at most $N$, since each of the $N$ independent draws contributes one factor linear in $\alpha$. For one-dimensional Gaussians $P_0$, $P_1$ and a Gaussian target, FID along the family contains the standard deviation of the mixture, the square root of a quadratic in $\alpha$, and so is not a polynomial.

The double sums cost $O(n^2 d)$, against $O(n d^2 + d^3)$ for FID's covariances and root. Bińkowski et al. therefore average the estimate over many random subsets, 100 subsets of 1,000 images in their experiments. KID inherits FID's dependence on the ImageNet features, and its cubic kernel has its own blind spot: the two-bump example above is symmetric like the Gaussian, so their third moments agree too, and only a kernel that reaches the fourth moment would separate them.

## Code

The two-dimensional example, the two-bump failure and the bias of FID against KID. It uses NumPy and SciPy and runs in a few seconds.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import sqrtm

def fid(mu1, S1, mu2, S2):
    root = sqrtm(S1 @ S2).real                 # principal root; drop imaginary noise
    return float(((mu1 - mu2) ** 2).sum() + np.trace(S1 + S2 - 2 * root))

def fid_from_features(U, V):
    cov = lambda A: np.atleast_2d(np.cov(A, rowvar=False))
    return fid(U.mean(0), cov(U), V.mean(0), cov(V))

def kid(U, V):                                 # unbiased squared MMD, cubic kernel
    d, m, n = U.shape[1], len(U), len(V)
    k = lambda A, B: (A @ B.T / d + 1) ** 3
    Kuu, Kvv = k(U, U), k(V, V)
    return float((Kuu.sum() - np.trace(Kuu)) / (m * (m - 1))
                 + (Kvv.sum() - np.trace(Kvv)) / (n * (n - 1)) - 2 * k(U, V).mean())

Sr, Sg = np.array([[1, .6], [.6, .8]]), np.array([[.45, -.1], [-.1, .35]])
print('two Gaussians:', round(fid(np.zeros(2), Sr, np.array([1.2, .6]), Sg), 3))

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
real = rng.standard_normal((20_000, 1))        # one bump, N(0, 1)
bumps = rng.choice([-0.8, 0.8], (20_000, 1)) + 0.6 * rng.standard_normal((20_000, 1))
print('one bump vs two bumps:', round(fid_from_features(real, bumps), 4))

d = 64                                         # two samples of the SAME distribution
for n in (200, 1000, 5000):
    f = [fid_from_features(rng.standard_normal((n, d)), rng.standard_normal((n, d))) for _ in range(5)]
    k = [kid(rng.standard_normal((n, d)), rng.standard_normal((n, d))) for _ in range(5)]
    print(f'n = {n:5d}: FID {np.mean(f):.3f}   KID {np.mean(k):+.5f}')
```

```console
two Gaussians: 2.284
one bump vs two bumps: 0.0002
n =   200: FID 10.980   KID +0.00180
n =  1000: FID 2.188   KID -0.00092
n =  5000: FID 0.440   KID +0.00001
```

The first line is the table above. The second is the two-bump failure: zero up to sampling noise. The rest is the bias table, averaged over five pairs of samples per size, with KID beside it.
