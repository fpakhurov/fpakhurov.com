The [Inception Score](/notes/generative-models/inception-score) looks only at generated images: it asks whether a classifier is confident on each image and whether the predicted classes are diverse. It never checks whether the images resemble the training data. The **Fréchet Inception Distance** (FID) of [Heusel et al. (2017)](#references) closes that gap. It passes real and generated images through the same pretrained Inception network, summarises each set of features by a mean vector and a covariance matrix, and measures how far apart the two summaries are.

Notation: the subscript $r$ marks real images and $g$ marks generated ones. $\varphi(x)$ is the Inception feature vector of an image $x$, and $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the Euclidean norm.

## The formula

$$
\mathrm{FID} = \|\mu_r - \mu_g\|_2^2 + \operatorname{Tr}\!\Big(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2\,(\Sigma_r \Sigma_g)^{1/2}\Big)
$$

- $\mu_r, \mu_g$ are the **average feature vectors** of the real and generated images.
- $\Sigma_r, \Sigma_g$ are their **covariance matrices**.

With $N_r$ real images $x_1, \dots, x_{N_r}$ (and the same for the generated set), the two summaries are the usual sample estimates:

$$
\mu_r = \frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \varphi(x_i),
\qquad
\Sigma_r = \frac{1}{N_r - 1}\sum_{i=1}^{N_r} \big(\varphi(x_i) - \mu_r\big)\big(\varphi(x_i) - \mu_r\big)^{\top}.
$$

In effect we approximate each feature distribution by a [Gaussian](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions), $\mathcal N(\mu_r, \Sigma_r)$ for real images and $\mathcal N(\mu_g, \Sigma_g)$ for generated ones, and compare the two Gaussians.

**Take the embeddings before the softmax.** The features are not the class probabilities that IS uses, but activations from inside the network. In the reference implementation they come from the last pooling layer of Inception v3 (pool3), which feeds the classification head: 2048 numbers per image. Class probabilities would discard most of what the network sees in an image, while the penultimate layer keeps a richer description that is not tied to the 1000 ImageNet labels.

## Reading the two terms

- The **mean term** $\|\mu_r - \mu_g\|_2^2$ measures how far apart the centres of the two feature clouds are.
- The **covariance term** $\operatorname{Tr}(\cdot)$ measures how differently the clouds are spread and oriented. It is never negative and vanishes only when $\Sigma_r = \Sigma_g$.

So **lower FID means closer feature distributions**, and **0 means a perfect match**: equal means and equal covariances.

### A one-dimensional check

For scalar features, $\Sigma$ becomes a variance $\sigma^2$, the matrix square root is an ordinary square root, and the trace disappears:

$$
\sigma_r^2 + \sigma_g^2 - 2\sqrt{\sigma_r^2\sigma_g^2} = \sigma_r^2 + \sigma_g^2 - 2\sigma_r\sigma_g = (\sigma_r - \sigma_g)^2 ,
\qquad
\mathrm{FID}_{1\text{D}} = (\mu_r - \mu_g)^2 + (\sigma_r - \sigma_g)^2 .
$$

The covariance term is the squared difference of the standard deviations. Take real features $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ and generated features $\mathcal N(0.5, 2^2)$:

$$
\mathrm{FID} = (0 - 0.5)^2 + \big(1 + 4 - 2\cdot 1\cdot 2\big) = 0.25 + 1 = 1.25 .
$$

A generator whose samples are too spread out is penalised just like a shifted one. So is a generator that is **too narrow**. A model that produces nearly the same image every time (mode collapse) has $\sigma_g \to 0$, and the covariance term tends to $\sigma_r^2$ even when the mean is right.

### The two-dimensional example in numbers

Figure 2 uses

$$
\mu_r = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},\;
\Sigma_r = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0.6\\ 0.6 & 0.8\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mu_g = \begin{pmatrix}1.2\\0.6\end{pmatrix},\;
\Sigma_g = \begin{pmatrix}0.45 & -0.1\\ -0.1 & 0.35\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The only non-trivial step is the matrix square root. The product $M = \Sigma_r\Sigma_g$ is not symmetric, but it is similar to the symmetric positive-definite matrix $\Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g\Sigma_r^{1/2}$, so its eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are positive. For a $2\times 2$ matrix this gives a closed form:

$$
\operatorname{Tr} M^{1/2} = \sqrt{\lambda_1} + \sqrt{\lambda_2} = \sqrt{\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + 2\sqrt{\lambda_1\lambda_2}} = \sqrt{\operatorname{Tr} M + 2\sqrt{\det M}} .
$$

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| $\lVert\mu_r - \mu_g\rVert_2^2 = 1.2^2 + 0.6^2$ | $1.80$ |
| $M = \Sigma_r\Sigma_g$ | $\begin{pmatrix}0.39 & 0.11\\ 0.19 & 0.22\end{pmatrix}$ |
| $\operatorname{Tr} M,\ \det M$ | $0.61,\ 0.0649$ |
| $\operatorname{Tr} M^{1/2} = \sqrt{0.61 + 2\sqrt{0.0649}}$ | $1.058$ |
| $\operatorname{Tr}\Sigma_r + \operatorname{Tr}\Sigma_g - 2\operatorname{Tr} M^{1/2} = 1.8 + 0.8 - 2.116$ | $0.484$ |
| FID | $2.284$ |

In practice the features are 2048-dimensional and the square root is computed numerically, for example with `scipy.linalg.sqrtm`.

## FID is a Wasserstein distance between two Gaussians

$$
\mathrm{FID} = W_2^2\big(\mathcal N(\mu_r, \Sigma_r),\ \mathcal N(\mu_g, \Sigma_g)\big),
$$

the squared 2-Wasserstein distance between the two fitted Gaussians ([Dowson and Landau, 1982](#references)). For Gaussians this optimal-transport distance has the closed form above. It is the same family of transport distances as the $W_1$ of the [WGAN note](/notes/generative-models/wgan), with squared rather than plain Euclidean cost. That is where the name comes from: the 2-Wasserstein distance is also called the Fréchet distance. FID itself is the *squared* distance, so it has no outer square root.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Two forms of the trace.</strong> The general formula for $W_2$ between Gaussians is often written in the symmetric form $\operatorname{Tr}\big(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2(\Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g\Sigma_r^{1/2})^{1/2}\big)$. It equals the FID form: $\Sigma_r\Sigma_g = \Sigma_r^{1/2}\big(\Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g\Sigma_r^{1/2}\big)\Sigma_r^{-1/2}$, so the two matrices are similar, their square roots have the same eigenvalues, and the traces agree.</aside>

## Compared with the Inception Score

Unlike IS, FID really does compare with the "truth", the real data.

| | Inception Score | FID |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aim | confidence and diversity of generated images | closeness of generated images to real ones |
| Data used | generated images only | real and generated images |
| What is compared | each $p(y \mid x)$ with the marginal $p(y)$ | Gaussian fit to real features vs. Gaussian fit to generated features |
| Distance | KL divergence | Fréchet (2-Wasserstein) distance |
| Better is | higher | lower |

## Drawbacks

- **Gaussian approximation.** Inception features are not normally distributed. Two feature distributions with the same mean and covariance but different shapes get FID 0, because only the first two moments are compared.
- **Data unlike ImageNet.** The network was trained on ImageNet photographs. For faces, medical scans, sketches or spectrograms its features may be irrelevant to what makes an image good, and FID then measures the wrong thing. Even on natural images the features are strongly aligned with the ImageNet classes, so FID can be improved by matching class statistics without improving perceived quality ([Kynkäänniemi et al., 2023](#references)).
- **Needs thousands of images.** A 2048 × 2048 covariance matrix has $2048\cdot 2049/2 = 2\,098\,176$ free entries. With $N \le 2048$ images the sample covariance is singular (its rank is at most $N - 1$), and the matrix square root becomes numerically unstable. With a few thousand it is still noisy, and the FID estimate is biased upward for small samples ([Chong and Forsyth, 2020](#references)). Scores are only comparable at the same sample size; 10 000 or 50 000 images are common.
- **Sensitive to preprocessing.** Resizing to the network's input size, the interpolation method, compression and pixel normalisation all change the features. FIDs computed with different pipelines are not comparable ([Parmar, Zhang and Zhu, 2022](#references)).

## Over which images is FID computed?

A common question: is FID computed per class and accumulated, or per observation, comparing images in pairs? Neither. FID is computed **once, over all generated and all real images**. We pool every real image into one feature cloud and every generated image into another, fit one Gaussian to each, and compare the two Gaussians. FID needs no class labels, and no generated image is paired with a particular real image. Because it compares distributions rather than pairs, it needs the large samples described above. (A pairwise, per-image comparison is what [LPIPS](/notes/generative-models/lpips) does.)
