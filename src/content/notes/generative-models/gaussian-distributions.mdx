Suppose an optimizer trains the covariance matrix of a 2-D Gaussian directly. It starts at $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and one gradient step moves the off-diagonal entries to $1.2$. The new matrix has eigenvalues $2.2$ and $-0.2$: it would give one direction a negative variance, so it is no covariance at all, and the model breaks.

The fitting experiments of the next notes need a Gaussian that survives any gradient step, that we can sample from, and whose samples move smoothly when its parameters move. A triangular square root of the covariance gives the second and third. Storing its diagonal through an exponential gives the first: then every value of the trained numbers is a valid covariance. The density, the ellipses and the affine maps of the Gaussian are in the Probability course's [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian); here we use them.

## The covariance matrix

For a random vector $X = (X_1, X_2)^\top$ with mean $\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbb E[X]$, the covariance matrix is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol\Sigma &= \mathbb E\big[(X-\boldsymbol\mu)(X-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\big] \\
&= \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 \\ \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2 \end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\rho$ is the correlation of $X_1$ and $X_2$. It is symmetric, and positive semidefinite because $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf a^{\top}X) = \mathbf a^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf a \ge 0$ for every vector $\mathbf a$. We write $X \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)$, with the covariance, never the standard deviation, as the second argument. The running example has $\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf 0$, unit variances and $\rho = 0.5$.

Two variances alone cannot describe this cloud. Its axis variances are both 1, exactly those of the round $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$; only the off-diagonal entry records that the cloud leans along the diagonal $x_1 = x_2$.

## Sampling: from standard noise to any Gaussian

In one dimension, $X = \mu + \sigma Z$ with $Z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $\sigma > 0$ has distribution $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$: stretch standard noise by $\sigma$, then shift it by $\mu$. The [change-of-variables formula](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables#the-change-of-variables-formula) with $g(z) = \mu + \sigma z$ and $g' = \sigma$ gives the density $\varphi\big((x - \mu)/\sigma\big)/\sigma$, where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density; this is the $\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ density.

<Standardization1D />

In $d$ dimensions a matrix plays the role of $\sigma$. For any $\mathbf A$ with $\mathbf A\mathbf A^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$ and $Z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$,

$$
\boxed{\,X = \boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf A Z \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\,}
$$

The mean is $\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf A\,\mathbb E[Z] = \boldsymbol\mu$ by linearity of expectation. The covariance follows from the definition, with $X - \boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf A Z$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(X)
&= \mathbb E\big[\mathbf A Z (\mathbf A Z)^{\top}\big] \\
&= \mathbf A\, \mathbb E\big[Z Z^{\top}\big]\, \mathbf A^{\top} && \text{(linearity)} \\
&= \mathbf A\,\mathbf I\,\mathbf A^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Sigma .
\end{aligned}
$$

That the result is Gaussian at all is shown in the [affine maps section](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps) of the Probability course: by the change of variables for an invertible $\mathbf A$, and by the characteristic function for any $\mathbf A$.

Many matrices satisfy $\mathbf A\mathbf A^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$. Two are standard. The **symmetric square root** $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} = \mathbf U\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}\mathbf U^{\top}$ comes from the eigendecomposition $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf U\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf U^{\top}$, with $\mathbf U$ orthogonal by the [spectral theorem](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-spectral-theorem). It exists for every covariance, singular ones included: the eigenvalues of a positive semidefinite matrix are $\ge 0$, so their roots are real, and $\mathbf U^{\top}\mathbf U = \mathbf I$ gives $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} = \mathbf U\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf U^{\top}$. The **Cholesky factor** $\mathbf L$ is the unique lower-triangular matrix with positive diagonal and $\mathbf L\mathbf L^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$; it exists when $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is positive definite ([Golub and Van Loan, 2013](#references), §4.2; derived in [the Cholesky factorization](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-cholesky-factorization)).

For the running example the eigenvalues are $1.5$ and $0.5$, with eigenvectors along $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, and the two roots are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} &\approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.966 & 0.259 \\ 0.259 & 0.966 \end{pmatrix}, \\
\mathbf L &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & \sqrt{0.75} \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 0.866 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Both turn independent noise into the same correlated cloud. Code uses $\mathbf L$: a finite algorithm computes it in about $d^3/3$ operations ([the Cholesky factorization](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-cholesky-factorization)). An eigendecomposition needs an iterative method, because eigenvalues are roots of a polynomial of degree $d$, and for $d \ge 5$ such roots have no general formula in radicals (the Abel–Ruffini theorem). In PyTorch, `MultivariateNormal` stores the Cholesky factor of the covariance it receives and samples $\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf L Z$ ([PyTorch source](#references)).

## The reparameterization view

The map $x = \boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf L\varepsilon$ does more than sample. It moves all randomness into $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$, which does not depend on the parameters, and leaves a deterministic, differentiable function of $\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\mathbf L$. So the gradient of an expectation under the Gaussian becomes an expectation of a gradient:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\nabla_{\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf L}\, \mathbb E_{x \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf L\mathbf L^\top)}\big[f(x)\big] \\
&\quad= \nabla_{\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf L}\, \mathbb E_{\varepsilon}\big[f(\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf L\varepsilon)\big] \\
&\quad= \mathbb E_{\varepsilon}\big[\nabla_{\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf L}\, f(\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf L\varepsilon)\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first step uses only that $\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf L\varepsilon$ has the distribution $\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \mathbf L\mathbf L^\top)$, so both expectations are the same number. The second moves the gradient inside an expectation over a distribution that no longer depends on the parameters. This differentiation under the integral sign holds when $f$ is differentiable and its gradient is bounded by a function integrable against the noise density, as for any $f$ whose gradient grows at most polynomially.

The right side is estimated by averaging over a few draws of $\varepsilon$, and backpropagation handles it like any other layer. A sampler called as a black box would give no gradient at all ([Kingma and Welling, 2014](#references); [Isachenko, lecture 4](#references)).

A one-dimensional check: for $x = m + s\varepsilon$ and $f(x) = x^2$, the expectation is $\mathbb E[x]^2 + \operatorname{Var}(x) = m^2 + s^2$, so its derivative in $s$ is $2s$. The pathwise estimate averages $f'(x)\,\partial x/\partial s$, and $\mathbb E\varepsilon = 0$, $\mathbb E\varepsilon^2 = 1$ give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_\varepsilon\big[2(m + s\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon\big]
&= 2m\,\mathbb E\varepsilon + 2s\,\mathbb E\varepsilon^2 \\
&= 2s .
\end{aligned}
$$

With $m = 1$, $s = 0.5$ and 100,000 draws the average is $1.003$ against the exact $1$. The same move is the [reparameterization trick](/notes/generative-models/vae#the-reparameterization-trick) of the VAE and the [one-step noising formula](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process#the-objective-and-the-rescaled-step) of diffusion models.

## Gaussians in PyTorch

To let an optimizer change the covariance freely, we train the Cholesky factor through three unconstrained numbers $a, b, c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf L &= \begin{pmatrix} e^{a} & 0 \\ b & e^{c} \end{pmatrix}, \\
\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf L\mathbf L^{\top} &= \begin{pmatrix} e^{2a} & b\,e^{a} \\ b\,e^{a} & b^2 + e^{2c} \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The exponential keeps the diagonal positive, so $\mathbf L$ is invertible and $\mathbf v^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf v = \|\mathbf L^{\top}\mathbf v\|^2 > 0$ for every $\mathbf v \ne \mathbf 0$. Every gradient step on $a, b, c$ lands on a valid covariance, which repairs the opening example. The running example is $a = 0$, $b = 0.5$, $c = \ln\sqrt{0.75} \approx -0.144$, and $a = b = c = 0$ gives $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf I$.

The same factor gives the log-density without inverting $\boldsymbol\Sigma$. Start from the [Gaussian density](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#the-density), $\log p(\mathbf x) = -\tfrac12 (\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu) - \tfrac12\log\det(2\pi\boldsymbol\Sigma)$. Inverting the product $\mathbf L\mathbf L^{\top}$ gives $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} = \mathbf L^{-\top}\mathbf L^{-1}$, so the quadratic form is $\|\mathbf L^{-1}(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)\|^2$. The determinant of a product is the product of determinants, and that of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal, so $\det\boldsymbol\Sigma = \prod_i L_{ii}^2$. Together,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p(\mathbf x) ={}& -\tfrac12 \big\|\mathbf L^{-1}(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)\big\|^2 \\
&- \sum_{i=1}^d \log L_{ii} - \tfrac d2 \log 2\pi .
\end{aligned}
$$

The vector $\mathbf L^{-1}(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)$ comes from one triangular solve. This is how `MultivariateNormal.log_prob` computes it, and passing the factor as `scale_tril=L` skips the re-factorization that `covariance_matrix=L @ L.T` would trigger. In PyTorch the raw numbers are tensors created with `requires_grad=True`; the target distribution is built from fixed tensors.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_triangular
from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal

a, b, c = 0.0, 0.5, np.log(np.sqrt(0.75))      # raw parameters: any real numbers
L = np.array([[np.exp(a), 0.0],
              [b, np.exp(c)]])                 # lower triangular, positive diagonal
mu, Sigma = np.zeros(2), L @ L.T
print(L.round(3).ravel(), Sigma.round(3).ravel())   # Sigma = [[1, 0.5], [0.5, 1]]

def log_density(x, mu, L):
    w = solve_triangular(L, (x - mu).T, lower=True)  # L^{-1}(x - mu): no inverse of Sigma
    d = len(mu)
    return -0.5 * (w**2).sum(0) - np.log(np.diag(L)).sum() - 0.5 * d * np.log(2 * np.pi)

x = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [1.0, 1.0], [1.0, -1.0]])   # the centre, along the tilt, across it
print(log_density(x, mu, L).round(4))
print(multivariate_normal(mu, Sigma).logpdf(x).round(4))   # the same numbers

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
eps = rng.standard_normal((100_000, 2))
samples = mu + eps @ L.T                       # x = mu + L eps, row by row
print(np.cov(samples.T).round(2).ravel())

# reparameterization: x = m + s * eps, f(x) = x^2, so E f = m^2 + s^2 and dE/ds = 2s
m, s = 1.0, 0.5
e = rng.standard_normal(100_000)
print((2 * (m + s * e) * e).mean().round(3))   # pathwise estimate of 2s = 1
```

```text
[1.    0.    0.5   0.866] [1.  0.5 0.5 1. ]
[-1.694  -2.3607 -3.694 ]
[-1.694  -2.3607 -3.694 ]
[1.  0.5 0.5 1. ]
1.003
```

The points $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ are equally far from the centre, yet the one along the tilt has log-density $-2.36$ and the one across it $-3.69$. The fitting experiments start from a fixed target $P$ and a trainable model $Q$ built this way:

## Where the full factor breaks

A full covariance in $d$ dimensions has $d(d+1)/2$ free numbers. For a VAE with a 128-dimensional latent code that is 8,256 numbers per input, which an encoder would have to predict for every image. VAE encoders therefore output a diagonal covariance: $d$ variances, an $\mathbf L$ with nothing below the diagonal, and a cloud that can stretch along the axes but never tilt, like the left panel of the first figure.

A singular covariance has no Cholesky factor at all. If one coordinate copies another, $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and `np.linalg.cholesky` stops with "Matrix is not positive definite"; `torch.linalg.cholesky` fails the same way. Estimated covariances hit this when features are collinear or when there are fewer samples than dimensions. The usual repair adds a small jitter $\epsilon\mathbf I$. With $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ the factor is $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0.0014 \end{pmatrix}$: a valid Gaussian, flattened almost onto the diagonal line. Its log-density is $4.72$ at the origin, against $-1.84$ for $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$, and $-20.28$ at $(0, 0.01)$, a hundredth off the line.

```python
import numpy as np
S = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0]])          # X2 = X1: a singular covariance
try:
    np.linalg.cholesky(S)
except np.linalg.LinAlgError as err:
    print("LinAlgError:", err)
L = np.linalg.cholesky(S + 1e-6 * np.eye(2))    # a small jitter on the diagonal
print(L.round(4).ravel())
```

```text
LinAlgError: Matrix is not positive definite
[1.     0.     1.     0.0014]
```

The trained parametrization cannot produce a singular matrix, because $e^{a}$ and $e^{c}$ are never zero. It can still drift towards one. Write the second coordinate of $\mathbf x = \boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf L\varepsilon$ through the first: $\varepsilon_1 = (x_1 - \mu_1)e^{-a}$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_2 &= \mu_2 + b\,\varepsilon_1 + e^{c}\varepsilon_2 \\
&= \mu_2 + b\,e^{-a}(x_1 - \mu_1) + e^{c}\varepsilon_2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

If the training points lie on a line $x_2 = \alpha + \beta x_1$, choose $b\,e^{-a} = \beta$ and $\mu_2 - \beta\mu_1 = \alpha$. Then $\varepsilon_2 = 0$ at every point, the quadratic term of the log-density loses its second component, and the term $-\log L_{22} = -c$ grows without limit as $c \to -\infty$. Two points in the plane always lie on a line, so with no more points than dimensions maximum likelihood follows the same path: the memorizing fit of the [first note](/notes/generative-models/generative-modeling#the-problem-examples-of-an-unknown-distribution), in miniature.
