A generative model is a distribution we can sample from and, ideally, evaluate. Before neural networks enter, the course works with the simplest distribution that is still interesting in several dimensions: the multivariate Gaussian. It is the running example of the first notes, where it is both the **target** we draw data from and the **model** we fit to that data. It also comes back in almost every later model: the prior of a VAE, the noise of a diffusion model, the base distribution of a normalizing flow and the feature statistics behind FID.

The opening block of the course has three steps:

1. **generate data** from a known distribution (this note);
2. measure how far two distributions are from each other ([Entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences));
3. **fit** a parametrized 2-D distribution to the data by gradient descent on such a distance ([Fitting distributions with KL](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl)).

## The covariance matrix

Take a random column vector in two dimensions and its mean. Capital letters such as $X$ and $Z$ denote random vectors, bold lowercase $\mathbf x$ a point of the plane:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} X_1 \\ X_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \mathbb E[X] = \begin{pmatrix} \mu_{1} \\ \mu_{2} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its spread is described by the $2 \times 2$ variance–covariance matrix $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbb E\bigl[(X-\boldsymbol\mu)(X-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\bigr]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
&= \begin{pmatrix} \operatorname{Var}(X_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) \\ \operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) & \operatorname{Var}(X_2) \end{pmatrix} \\[4pt]
&= \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_{1}^2 & \rho\,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2} \\ \rho\,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2} & \sigma_{2}^2 \end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\rho$ is the correlation coefficient between $X_1$ and $X_2$. The matrix is symmetric, because $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_2, X_1)$. It is also positive semidefinite: for any fixed vector $\mathbf a$ the variance $\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf a^{\top}X) = \mathbf a^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf a$ cannot be negative. Three free numbers hold every variance and covariance of the vector.

The example used throughout this note is a Gaussian centred at the origin with

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}:
$$

unit variances and correlation $\rho = 0.5$, so the cloud leans along the diagonal $x_1 = x_2$.

### Why a full matrix and not just two variances?

Two variances describe how far the cloud spreads along each coordinate axis. That is enough only when the coordinates are uncorrelated, and then the cloud is stretched along the axes. The off-diagonal entry records what the variances cannot: how the coordinates move **together**. It tilts the cloud.

In Figure 1 the right cloud is the left one rotated by 45°. The two clouds have the same eigenvalues (1.5 and 0.5), but both axis variances of the right cloud equal 1. Those are exactly the axis variances of the round $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$ cloud, so two variances alone cannot tell the tilted cloud from a circle. Only $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1, X_2) = 0.5$ records the tilt.

## The bivariate density

When $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is invertible (positive definite, not just semidefinite), the joint density on the whole plane $\mathbb R^2$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(\mathbf x) &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{|\boldsymbol\Sigma|}} \\[4pt]
&\times \exp\!\left(-\tfrac12\, (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\,(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\right),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mathbf x = (x_1, x_2)^{\top}$, $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}$ is the inverse and $|\boldsymbol\Sigma|$ the determinant of the covariance matrix. The shapes in the quadratic form are

$$
\underbrace{(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}}_{1\times 2}\;
\underbrace{\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}}_{2\times 2}\;
\underbrace{(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)}_{2\times 1},
$$

so the exponent is a scalar. In $d$ dimensions the normalizing constant becomes $(2\pi)^{-d/2}|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{-1/2}$, and $d = 2$ gives the expression above ([Bishop, 2006](#references), §2.3). We write $X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ for a univariate normal and $X \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)$ for a multivariate one.

The level sets of the density, where $(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu) = k^2$, are ellipses; the figures draw them as the $1\sigma$ ($k = 1$) and $2\sigma$ ($k = 2$) contours. For a Gaussian vector in two dimensions this squared distance follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with two degrees of freedom, whose CDF is $1 - e^{-k^2/2}$. So the $1\sigma$ ellipse holds $1 - e^{-1/2} \approx 39\%$ of the probability and the $2\sigma$ ellipse $1 - e^{-2} \approx 86\%$, not the 68% and 95% of one-dimensional intervals.

For the running example

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\boldsymbol\Sigma| &= 1 - 0.5^2 = 0.75, \\[4pt]
\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} &= \frac{1}{0.75}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -0.5 \\ -0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \\[4pt]
&= \begin{pmatrix} 4/3 & -2/3 \\ -2/3 & 4/3 \end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
$$

and the peak density is $f(\boldsymbol\mu) = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{0.75}) \approx 0.184$.

## Sampling: from standard noise to any Gaussian

In one dimension, standardization and its inverse are

$$
\begin{aligned}
Z &= \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim \mathcal N(0, 1) \quad \text{for } X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2),\\[4pt]
X &= \mu + \sigma Z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2) \quad \text{for } Z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1).
\end{aligned}
$$

So to sample any normal distribution it is enough to sample the standard one, stretch by $\sigma$ and shift by $\mu$.

<Standardization1D />

The bivariate case needs a matrix that plays the role of $\sigma$.

**Spectral decomposition.** Every symmetric matrix, and in particular every covariance matrix, can be written as

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf U\,\boldsymbol\Lambda\,\mathbf U^{\top},
$$

where $\boldsymbol\Lambda$ is diagonal with the eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ and the columns of the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf U$ are the matching normalized eigenvectors. The eigenvalues are non-negative because $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is positive semidefinite, so we can define the matrix square root

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} = \mathbf U\,\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}\,\mathbf U^{\top},
$$

where $\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}$ holds the square roots of the eigenvalues. Since $\mathbf U^{\top}\mathbf U = \mathbf I$, we get $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} = \mathbf U\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf U^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$.

**Transformation.** If $Z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$, then

$$
X = \boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} Z \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma).
$$

More generally, any matrix $\mathbf A$ with $\mathbf A\mathbf A^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$ works. An affine map of a Gaussian vector is again Gaussian ([Bishop, 2006](#references), §2.3.3), so it is enough to check the first two moments. The mean is $\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf A\,\mathbb E[Z] = \boldsymbol\mu$, and the covariance is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf A Z) &= \mathbf A\,\operatorname{Cov}(Z)\,\mathbf A^{\top} \\
&= \mathbf A\,\mathbf I\,\mathbf A^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Sigma .
\end{aligned}
$$

The symmetric root $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2}$ is one choice. The lower-triangular **Cholesky factor** $\mathbf L$ is the usual choice in code, because it is cheaper to compute than an eigendecomposition. PyTorch's `MultivariateNormal` samples exactly this way: it stores the Cholesky factor of the covariance it receives and returns $\boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf L Z$ with $Z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$ ([PyTorch documentation](#references)). The same pattern, noise in and a deterministic transformation out, is the reparameterization trick of the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) and the one-step noising formula of [DDPM](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process).

### Worked example

For $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ the characteristic equation is $(1 - \lambda)^2 = 0.25$, so the eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 1.5$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.5$. The eigenvectors are $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)^{\top}$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)^{\top}$. The variance is largest along the diagonal $x_1 = x_2$: the $1\sigma$ ellipse has semi-axes $\sqrt{1.5} \approx 1.225$ along it and $\sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.707$ across it. Two valid square roots are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} &= \frac12\begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{1.5}+\sqrt{0.5} & \sqrt{1.5}-\sqrt{0.5} \\ \sqrt{1.5}-\sqrt{0.5} & \sqrt{1.5}+\sqrt{0.5} \end{pmatrix} \\[4pt]
&\approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.966 & 0.259 \\ 0.259 & 0.966 \end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\mathbf L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & \sqrt{0.75} \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 0.866 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Check: $0.966^2 + 0.259^2 \approx 1$ and $2 \cdot 0.966 \cdot 0.259 \approx 0.5$. For the Cholesky factor, $\mathbf L\mathbf L^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.25 + 0.75 \end{pmatrix} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$. Both matrices turn independent standard noise into samples of the same correlated cloud.

## Gaussians in PyTorch

`torch.distributions` contains parametrizable distributions with methods to evaluate their (log-)density and to draw samples. The 2-D Gaussian is `MultivariateNormal`. The fitting experiments of the next notes need a Gaussian whose parameters an optimizer can change freely. The covariance makes that non-trivial: an arbitrary $2\times 2$ matrix is generally not a valid covariance.

The fix is to learn the Cholesky factor instead of $\boldsymbol\Sigma$. With

$$
\mathbf L = \begin{pmatrix} e^{a} & 0 \\ b & e^{c} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf L\mathbf L^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{2a} & b\,e^{a} \\ b\,e^{a} & b^2 + e^{2c} \end{pmatrix},
$$

the three raw numbers $a, b, c$ can take any real values. The diagonal of $\mathbf L$ is stored through a logarithm, so it stays positive, $\mathbf L$ is invertible, and $\mathbf L\mathbf L^{\top}$ is symmetric and positive definite. The running example is reached with $a = 0$, $b = 0.5$ and $c = \ln\sqrt{0.75} \approx -0.144$; the initialization $a = b = c = 0$ gives $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf I$.

Parameters that should be learned are created with `requires_grad=True`, which tells PyTorch's autograd to record operations on them and compute their gradients. The model distribution $Q$ has a trainable mean and trainable raw entries of $\mathbf L$. The target distribution $P$ uses `requires_grad=False`, because it stays fixed. Figure 4 shows the starting point of the first fitting experiment, with each cloud coloured by its own density.

<NotebookScatter />

A minimal sketch of both ways to sample the running example:

```python
import torch
from torch import distributions as distrs

mu = torch.zeros(2)
cov = torch.tensor([[1.0, 0.5], [0.5, 1.0]])
target = distrs.MultivariateNormal(mu, covariance_matrix=cov)

x = target.sample(torch.Size([1000]))         # 1000 × 2 samples
L = torch.linalg.cholesky(cov)                # [[1, 0], [0.5, 0.866]]
x_manual = mu + torch.randn(1000, 2) @ L.T    # same distribution
```

Each row of `torch.randn(1000, 2)` is one draw $z^{\top}$, so `@ L.T` computes $(\mathbf L z)^{\top}$ row by row.
