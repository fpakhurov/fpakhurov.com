The course starts with mathematics and statistics before moving to images. The first goals are to generate data from a known 2-D distribution, fit a model to it, and meet the losses used to compare distributions: KL, JS and least-squares objectives. The multivariate Gaussian is the working example.

## Mean and covariance

Take a random $2 \times 1$ column vector and its mean:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} X_1 \\ X_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{pmatrix} \mu_{X_1} \\ \mu_{X_2} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its spread is described by the $2 \times 2$ variance–covariance matrix

$$
\boldsymbol{\Sigma} =
\begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{V}[X_1] & \mathrm{Cov}(X_1, X_2) \\ \mathrm{Cov}(X_1, X_2) & \mathrm{V}[X_2] \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} \sigma_{X_1}^2 & \rho\,\sigma_{X_1}\sigma_{X_2} \\ \rho\,\sigma_{X_1}\sigma_{X_2} & \sigma_{X_2}^2 \end{pmatrix},
$$

where $\rho$ is the correlation coefficient. A covariance matrix is always symmetric and positive semidefinite.

### Why a full matrix and not just two variances?

With only $\mathrm{Var}(X_1)$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_2)$ we can describe clouds that are stretched along the coordinate axes. The off-diagonal entry adds the one thing the variances cannot express: how the coordinates move **together**, which tilts the cloud. The matrix is a compact representation of all of this.

The seminar's running example is a Gaussian centred at the origin with

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}:
$$

unit variances and correlation $\rho = 0.5$, so the cloud leans along the diagonal $x_1 = x_2$.

## The bivariate density

When $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is invertible (positive definite), the joint density on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is

$$
f(x_1, x_2) = \left(2\pi\sqrt{|\boldsymbol\Sigma|}\right)^{-1}
\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12\, (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\,(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\right),
\qquad -\infty < x_1, x_2 < \infty,
$$

where $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}$ is the inverse and $|\boldsymbol\Sigma|$ the determinant of the covariance matrix. The shapes in the quadratic form are $(1\times 2)(2 \times 2)(2 \times 1)$, so the exponent is a scalar.

In $d$ dimensions the normalizing constant becomes $(2\pi)^{-d/2}|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{-1/2}$; for $d = 2$ this is the expression above. For the running example $|\boldsymbol\Sigma| = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75$.

Shorthand:

$$
X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2) \ \text{(univariate)}, \qquad
X \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma) \ \text{(multivariate)}.
$$

## Sampling: from standard noise to any Gaussian

In one dimension, standardization and its inverse are

$$
Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),
\qquad
X = \mu + \sigma Z \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2).
$$

The multivariate analogue needs a matrix that plays the role of $\sigma$.

**Spectral decomposition.** Every covariance matrix can be written as

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf Q\,\boldsymbol\Lambda\,\mathbf Q^{\top},
$$

where $\boldsymbol\Lambda$ is diagonal with the eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ and the columns of the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf Q$ are the corresponding normalized eigenvectors. (The slide writes this as $\boldsymbol\Gamma'\boldsymbol\Lambda\boldsymbol\Gamma$ with $\boldsymbol\Gamma' = \mathbf Q$.) Define the symmetric square root

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} = \mathbf Q\,\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}\,\mathbf Q^{\top},
$$

with $\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2}$ holding the square roots of the eigenvalues. Then $\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$.

**Transformation.** If $Z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$, then

$$
X = \boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} Z \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma).
$$

This answers the slide's question — yes, the bivariate case has the same transformation. More generally, any matrix $\mathbf A$ with $\mathbf A\mathbf A^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$ works, because $\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf A Z) = \mathbf A\,\mathbf I\,\mathbf A^{\top}$. The Cholesky factor is the usual choice in code.

### Worked example

For $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ the eigenvalues are $1.5$ and $0.5$, with eigenvectors $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)^{\top}$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)^{\top}$. The variance is largest along the diagonal, as expected. Two valid "square roots":

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.966 & 0.259 \\ 0.259 & 0.966 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf L_{\text{Cholesky}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & \sqrt{0.75} \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 0.866 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Both turn independent standard noise into samples of the correlated cloud.

## Practical notes from the notebook

- `torch.distributions` contains parametrizable distributions with density evaluation and sampling, including `MultivariateNormal`.
- The seminar notebook wraps a distribution in a small helper class for convenience and plots samples with a `plot_2d_dots` helper; their source is not part of the scan.
- `requires_grad=True` marks a tensor so that PyTorch computes gradients for it — the mechanism that later lets us fit the mean and covariance by gradient descent.
- `%matplotlib inline` embeds figures in the notebook. In modern Jupyter this is already the default and the magic is kept for compatibility.
- Code formatting: `black` (e.g. `python -m black <notebook>`) also has a Jupyter extra, and the same formatter can run from a `pre-commit` hook.

An illustrative sketch of the sampling step (not from the scan):

```python
import torch
from torch.distributions import MultivariateNormal

mu = torch.zeros(2)
cov = torch.tensor([[1.0, 0.5], [0.5, 1.0]])
target = MultivariateNormal(mu, covariance_matrix=cov)

x = target.sample((1000,))            # 1000 × 2 samples
L = torch.linalg.cholesky(cov)
x_manual = mu + torch.randn(1000, 2) @ L.T   # same distribution
```
