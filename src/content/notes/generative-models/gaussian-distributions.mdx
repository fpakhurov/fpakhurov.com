The first seminar starts with a small generative task: model a two-dimensional distribution and generate samples from it. A multivariate Gaussian provides a compact description of both the location of the data and how its coordinates vary together.

## Mean and covariance

For a random vector $X \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the mean is a vector and the covariance is a matrix:

$$
\mu = \mathbb{E}[X],
\qquad
\Sigma = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X-\mu)(X-\mu)^\top\right].
$$

In two dimensions,

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix}X_1\\X_2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\mu = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}[X_1]\\\mathbb{E}[X_2]\end{pmatrix},
$$

$$
\Sigma =
\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorname{Var}(X_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)\\
\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2) & \operatorname{Var}(X_2)
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\sigma_1^2 & \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2\\
\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 & \sigma_2^2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Here $\sigma_i$ is the standard deviation of coordinate $i$, and $\rho$ is the correlation when both standard deviations are nonzero. The diagonal entries describe each coordinate's variance. The off-diagonal entries describe how the coordinates vary together. Recording only $\operatorname{Var}(X_1)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X_2)$ loses that relationship.

The example in the notes is centered at the origin:

$$
\mu = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Both coordinates have variance $1$, and their correlation is $0.5$. The positive covariance makes samples tend to vary in the same direction, giving an elongated cloud along the $x_1=x_2$ direction. Two separate unit variances would not specify this orientation.

## Multivariate Gaussian density

We write

$$
X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma).
$$

When $\Sigma$ is positive definite, the density on $\mathbb{R}^d$ is

$$
p(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right].
$$

The quadratic form in the exponent is a scalar: a $1\times d$ row vector multiplies a $d\times d$ matrix and then a $d\times1$ column vector. It measures displacement from the mean while accounting for the covariance. In two dimensions, the normalizing factor becomes $1/(2\pi\sqrt{\det\Sigma})$.

A covariance matrix is always symmetric and positive semidefinite. Positive definiteness is the additional condition needed for the inverse and the ordinary density above. A singular covariance still defines a Gaussian distribution, but its samples lie in a lower-dimensional subspace and this density formula does not apply.

## Sampling by an affine transformation

For one variable, a standard normal sample can be shifted and scaled:

$$
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),
\qquad
X=\mu+\sigma Z
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2).
$$

The vector version replaces the scale $\sigma$ with a matrix factor. Choose $A$ such that $AA^\top=\Sigma$ and sample

$$
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d),
\qquad
X=\mu+AZ.
$$

The coordinates of $Z$ are independent standard normal variables. The transformation gives

$$
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu,
\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(X)=A I_d A^\top=\Sigma.
$$

<GaussianSampling />

One choice uses the spectral decomposition. With orthonormal eigenvectors as the columns of $Q$,

$$
\Sigma=Q\Lambda Q^\top,
\qquad
\Sigma^{1/2}=Q\Lambda^{1/2}Q^\top,
$$

where $\Lambda^{1/2}$ contains the square roots of the nonnegative eigenvalues. Taking $A=\Sigma^{1/2}$ produces the sampling transformation shown in the seminar. It extends the familiar univariate shift and scale to correlated coordinates.
