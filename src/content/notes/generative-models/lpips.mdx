**Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS)** compares two images using internal features of a neural network. A lower score means the pair is closer in the chosen perceptual feature space.

This answers a different question from [FID](/notes/generative-models/fid): LPIPS compares an image pair, while FID compares statistics of two image collections. For a reconstruction task, the pair can be a clean reference $x$ and the model's output $x'$.

## Why pixel error can be misleading

Pixelwise MSE compares values at matching coordinates. A small translation can move an edge to neighboring pixels and create a large error even when the image still looks similar. Conversely, a blurred image can have a relatively small pixel error while losing perceptually important detail.

The handwritten notes use these examples to motivate comparing **feature maps** instead of raw pixels. Features at several internal layers capture different aspects of image structure, so the comparison does not rely on a single layer's representation.

LPIPS remains sensitive to the features it uses. It is not a guarantee of invariance to shifts, noise or blur, and a large score does not establish that the images have different semantic content.

## From two images to one score

For the usual calibrated, pretrained variant, both images pass through the **same feature network**. At each selected layer $\ell$:

1. Extract feature maps $\phi_\ell(x)$ and $\phi_\ell(x')$.
2. Normalize each feature vector along its **channel dimension**, separately at every spatial location.
3. Subtract the normalized vectors and apply learned channel weights to their squared differences.
4. Average over the layer's spatial grid, then sum the contributions from the selected layers.

The shared network puts both images in the same feature space. Channel normalization concerns the features, not the RGB input's value range.

The normalized vector at location $(h,w)$ is denoted by $\hat{\phi}_\ell(x)_{hw}$. Conceptually,

$$
\hat{\phi}_\ell(x)_{hw}
=\frac{\phi_\ell(x)_{hw}}
{\|\phi_\ell(x)_{hw}\|_2}.
$$

Numerical implementations stabilize the denominator near zero. This normalization treats the channel activations at one location as a vector; it does not normalize each channel over all spatial positions.

## Layer distance and aggregation

The formula on page 19 agrees with the paper's distance definition:

$$
d_\ell(x,x')
=\frac{1}{H_\ell W_\ell}
\sum_{h,w}
\left\|
w_\ell\odot
\left(
\hat{\phi}_\ell(x)_{hw}
-\hat{\phi}_\ell(x')_{hw}
\right)
\right\|_2^2,
$$

$$
\operatorname{LPIPS}(x,x')=\sum_\ell d_\ell(x,x').
$$

Here $H_\ell$ and $W_\ell$ are the feature map's height and width; $w_\ell$ supplies one weight per channel; and $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication. The squared norm sums across channels. Dividing by $H_\ell W_\ell$ averages across positions, while the final sum combines layers.

In this notation, the coefficient of a channel's squared difference is $w_{\ell,c}^2$. The official implementation expresses this calibration as a learned linear layer applied **after** squaring the feature differences. This is a parameterization detail: do not multiply by an implementation's stored coefficient and then square it again.

## What is learned

The seminar says to learn $w_\ell$ from human perceptual judgments. In the paper's **linear calibration** variant, the pretrained feature network stays fixed while these channel weights are learned. The supervision asks which of two distorted patches looks closer to a reference. It teaches the comparison to emphasize differences that matter to those judgments.

This distinguishes calibrated LPIPS from a generic feature reconstruction loss with equal weights. The paper also studies variants that tune the feature network or train it from scratch; the pipeline above describes the fixed-feature calibration setting.

## Interpretation, uses and limitations

The notes list reconstruction, super-resolution, inpainting and style transfer as applications, along with comparisons of generated outputs and use as a perceptual training loss. For a paired task, lower LPIPS indicates that the output's features are closer to its reference's features. It can complement pixel error when these comparisons emphasize different distortions.

LPIPS alone does not establish realism, coverage of a dataset or correctness relative to a prompt. Comparing two generated samples can describe their pairwise variation, but does not by itself show that either sample is good.

The source lists three practical limitations: dependence on the feature network, more computation than pixelwise metrics, and imperfect agreement with human perception. Its claim of robustness to small shifts and noise is best read as a motivation for feature comparisons, not a universal property. The particular architecture and learned calibration affect the result.

For reproducible comparisons, specify the backbone, LPIPS version and preprocessing. The official package expects RGB tensors with shape $N\times3\times H\times W$ and inputs scaled to $[-1,1]$ by default. This input scaling is distinct from the internal channel normalization above.
