**LPIPS** (Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity; [Zhang et al., 2018](#references)) measures how different two images *look*. [Inception Score](/notes/generative-models/inception-score) and [FID](/notes/generative-models/fid) score a whole set of generated images. LPIPS instead compares a **pair** of images, usually a generated or distorted image $x'$ and a reference $x$.

The idea in one line: take outputs from **several internal layers** of a pretrained network, find the differences between the feature maps, then **normalise, weigh and average**.

## Why not compare pixels?

The obvious distance between two images is pixelwise: the $L_2$ distance or the mean squared error over all $C\times H\times W$ values,

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(x, x') = \frac{1}{CHW}\sum_{c,h,w}\big(x_{chw} - x'_{chw}\big)^2 .
$$

For perception it is a bad distance, and it fails in both directions:

- **Shift the image and MSE explodes.** Moving the picture by one pixel changes almost nothing for a human. Every pixel on an edge, however, now meets a different value, so the error is large.
- **MSE considers blur to be fine.** A blurred image replaces each pixel by an average of its neighbours. Each value moves only a little, so MSE stays small, even though the blur is clearly visible.

A small computed example makes this concrete. Take an $8\times 8$ image of vertical stripes with a period of 4 pixels, so every row reads `0 0 1 1 0 0 1 1`. Compare it with two copies:

- **shifted** right by 1 px (wrapping around), so every row becomes `1 0 0 1 1 0 0 1`;
- **blurred** with the kernel $\tfrac14[1\;2\;1]$, so every row becomes `.25 .25 .75 .75 .25 .25 .75 .75`.

| Copy | Per-pixel squared error | MSE | To a human |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shifted | $1$ on half the pixels, $0$ elsewhere | $0.5$ | looks the same |
| Blurred | $0.25^2 = 0.0625$ on every pixel | $0.0625$ | looks worse |

Pixel MSE calls the blurred copy $0.5 / 0.0625 = 8$ times closer to the original, which is the opposite of what we see.

A related effect explains why networks trained with a pure MSE loss tend to produce blurry outputs: when several sharp answers are plausible, their average (a blur) has the lowest expected squared error. This is the same reason [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) samples are blurry. A perceptual distance should instead be **robust to small shifts and noise** and **sensitive to the loss of structure**. Deep features get much closer to this. A convolutional network responds to edges, textures and parts, and its deeper layers summarise each neighbourhood on a coarse grid, so a one-pixel shift moves their responses only slightly. Early layers still register the shift, so LPIPS is more robust to small shifts than pixel MSE, but not invariant to them.

## The algorithm

1. **Extract features** $\varphi_l(x)$ and $\varphi_l(x')$ at several layers $l = 1,\dots,L$ of a pretrained network such as AlexNet or VGG. The same frozen network, with shared weights, processes both images.
2. **Normalise channel-wise.** At every spatial position $(h, w)$, divide the feature vector by its length across the channels.
3. **Subtract** the two normalised feature maps.
4. **Weigh the difference** channel by channel with a vector $w_l$, square it and **average spatially** over all positions. This gives one number $d_l$ per layer.
5. **Sum over layers** to get LPIPS.

The weights $w_l$ are **learned from human perceptual judgements**: they are fitted on the BAPPS dataset of two-alternative forced choices, in which people saw a reference patch and two distorted versions and picked the one that looked closer ([Zhang et al., 2018](#references)).

## The formula

Notation: layer $l$ produces a feature map $\varphi_l(x) \in \mathbb R^{C_l \times H_l \times W_l}$ with $C_l$ channels on an $H_l \times W_l$ grid. The feature vector at position $(h, w)$ is $\varphi_l(x)_{hw} \in \mathbb R^{C_l}$, and the hat marks its unit-normalised version:

$$
\hat\varphi_l(x)_{hw} = \frac{\varphi_l(x)_{hw}}{\big\|\varphi_l(x)_{hw}\big\|_2} .
$$

**Layer $l$:**

$$
d_l(x, x') = \frac{1}{H_l W_l}\sum_{h,w}\Big\|\, w_l \odot \big(\hat\varphi_l(x)_{hw} - \hat\varphi_l(x')_{hw}\big)\Big\|_2^2 ,
\qquad w_l \in \mathbb R^{C_l}.
$$

Here $\odot$ is the element-wise product, so $w_l$ scales each channel of the difference. The reference `lpips` package applies non-negative weights to the *squared* channel differences, $\sum_c w_{l,c}\,(\hat\varphi_{l,c} - \hat\varphi'_{l,c})^2$. This is the formula above with $w_l$ replaced by $\sqrt{w_l}$, so the two forms are equivalent.

**Final:**

$$
\mathrm{LPIPS}(x, x') = \sum_{l} d_l(x, x') .
$$

Each piece has a job:

- **Normalisation** removes the overall magnitude of the activations. Only the *direction* of the feature vector, meaning which channels respond and in what proportion, is compared. Layers or positions with large activations therefore do not dominate. Without the weights the term is a cosine distance in disguise: for unit vectors with angle $\theta$ between them, $\|\hat u - \hat v\|_2^2 = 2 - 2\cos\theta$.
- **The weights** $w_l$ decide which channels matter for perception and turn down the ones that do not.
- **The spatial average** $\frac{1}{H_l W_l}\sum_{h,w}$ makes layers with different grid sizes comparable. It also forgives a small local disagreement, provided the rest of the map agrees.

The toy layer below has $C_l = 2$ channels on a $3 \times 3$ grid, so each feature vector can be drawn as an arrow. The weights are $w_l = (1, 0.6)$. Two positions show the effect of each step:

- **Bottom right.** The feature vectors point along channel 1 and channel 2. They differ by $90^\circ$, the largest angle possible for real features, which come after a ReLU and are non-negative. Unweighted, the term is $2 - 2\cos 90^\circ = 2$. The difference is $(1, -1)$, and the weights turn it into $(1, -0.6)$, so the term is $1 + 0.36 = 1.36$.
- **Centre.** The directions differ by $40^\circ$. Unweighted, the term would be $2 - 2\cos 40^\circ \approx 0.47$. The weight $0.6$ on channel 2 lowers it to $0.34$.

All the other positions nearly agree. Averaging the nine terms gives $d_l \approx 0.20$.

## Interpretation

- **Low LPIPS:** the images look perceptually similar.
- **High LPIPS:** the images have a different structure or different semantics.

The course notebook makes this tangible. It takes the first FashionMNIST image, an ankle boot, and computes LPIPS (AlexNet backbone) between it and about a thousand random images from the dataset. It then plots a histogram of the distances for each class and shows the nine closest images. In the saved run all nine closest images are ankle boots, at distances from $0.17$ to $0.22$. All ankle boots in the sample lie at about $0.17$ to $0.44$, while almost all images of other classes lie above $0.3$. Small LPIPS distances therefore pick out images of the same kind of clothing. The notebook's checks require the smallest distance to be below $0.2$ and more than 90% of the nine nearest neighbours to share the query's label, which with nine neighbours means all nine. The notebook feeds raw $0$–$255$ grayscale values to the network without rescaling them to $[-1, 1]$, so these absolute distances are specific to that setup and not comparable with published LPIPS scores; only the ranking matters here.

## Pros and cons

**Pros**

- **Better correlation with humans** than pixelwise metrics and classic measures such as SSIM. Most of the gain comes from the deep features themselves, which already capture perceptual structure even without learned weights; fitting $w_l$ to human judgements improves the agreement further ([Zhang et al., 2018](#references)).
- **Robust to small shifts and noise**, much more than raw pixels: deeper features change little under them, although the metric is not fully shift-invariant.

**Cons**

- **Depends on the backbone CNN.** AlexNet and VGG give different values, so scores are comparable only when they use the same backbone and LPIPS version.
- **Slower than pixelwise metrics:** every comparison needs a forward pass through a deep network.
- **Still not perfect.** It is a learned approximation of human perception and can disagree with people, especially on distortions unlike those in its training judgements.

## Where it is used

- **Comparing the outputs of GANs and diffusion models.** It measures how close a generated image is to a reference, and averaged pairwise LPIPS between samples is often used as a measure of diversity.
- **Evaluating image restoration and editing:** super-resolution, inpainting and style transfer, where a pixel metric would reward blurry outputs.
- **As a "perceptual loss" in training networks.** LPIPS is differentiable, so it can replace or complement MSE as a training loss, in the spirit of earlier feature-space losses ([Johnson, Alahi and Fei-Fei, 2016](#references)). This targets exactly the blur problem above.

## Compared with IS and FID

This extends the [IS-versus-FID table](/notes/generative-models/fid#compared-with-the-inception-score) with a column for LPIPS.

| | Inception Score | FID | LPIPS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | confidence and diversity of generated images | closeness of generated images to real ones | perceptual similarity of two images |
| Input | generated images only | real and generated image sets | a pair of images, e.g. generated vs. reference |
| Compares | class distributions, each sample vs. the marginal | Gaussian fits to feature distributions | normalised deep features, position by position |
| Distance | KL divergence | Fréchet distance (squared $W_2$ between the Gaussians) | learned, weighted squared $L_2$ distance in feature space |
