## Batch normalization

Batch normalization ([Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015](#references)) standardizes the activations of a layer using statistics of the current mini-batch. Take one feature (in a convolutional network, one channel) and a mini-batch of $n$ values $x_1, \dots, x_n$ of it. The layer first computes the **batch mean** and **batch variance**

$$
\mu_B = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i,
\qquad
\sigma_B^2 = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(x_i - \mu_B\right)^2,
$$

and then standardizes every value:

$$
\hat x_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}} .
$$

The small constant $\varepsilon > 0$ (PyTorch uses $10^{-5}$) keeps the division safe when a batch has almost no spread. After this step the batch has mean $0$ and variance $\sigma_B^2/(\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon) \approx 1$.

<BatchNormHistogram />

**A small worked example.** For the batch $x = (1, 2, 3, 6)$ with $\varepsilon = 10^{-5}$:

| | first | second | third | fourth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x_i$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $6$ |
| $x_i - \mu_B$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $3$ |
| $\hat x_i$ | $-1.069$ | $-0.535$ | $0$ | $1.604$ |

Here $\mu_B = 12/4 = 3$ and $\sigma_B^2 = (4 + 1 + 0 + 9)/4 = 3.5$, so every centred value is divided by $\sqrt{3.5 + 10^{-5}} \approx 1.871$. The variance divides by $n$, not $n - 1$: it is the plain variance of the batch.

### The rest of the layer

A full batch-norm layer has two learnable parameters per feature, a scale $\gamma$ and a shift $\beta$, and outputs

$$
y_i = \gamma\,\hat x_i + \beta .
$$

The parameters $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are fixed numbers, while $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ change from batch to batch, so the identity cannot be matched batch by batch. It can be matched against the population statistics of the feature: with $\gamma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[x] + \varepsilon}$ and $\beta = \mathbb E[x]$ the layer reproduces its input at inference, where running averages that estimate these population statistics (described below) replace $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$. In training it does so only approximately, up to the batch-to-batch fluctuation of $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ ([Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015](#references), section 3). Standardization therefore does not restrict what the layer can express. It changes the parametrization, so that the scale of each feature is controlled by one parameter.

Training and inference behave differently. During training the layer uses the batch statistics $\mu_B, \sigma_B^2$ and also keeps running averages of them. In PyTorch, with the default momentum $0.1$, these are $\mu_{\text{run}} \leftarrow 0.9\,\mu_{\text{run}} + 0.1\,\mu_B$ and $\sigma^2_{\text{run}} \leftarrow 0.9\,\sigma^2_{\text{run}} + 0.1\,\tfrac{n}{n-1}\sigma_B^2$; the running variance stores the unbiased estimate. At inference, when a "batch" may be a single image, the running averages are used instead, so the output of one example no longer depends on the other examples in the batch. In PyTorch the switch is `model.eval()`. Turning off gradients with `torch.no_grad()` does *not* switch batch norm to its running statistics. The Deep Learning course draws both modes on the batch above, derives the gradient through the batch statistics and shows where small batches fail ([batch normalization in training and in evaluation](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#batch-normalization-in-training-and-in-evaluation)).

In a convolutional network, `BatchNorm2d` normalizes each channel over all values of that channel in the batch: batch size × height × width of them. A convolution followed by batch norm is usually created with `bias=False`, because subtracting $\mu_B$ cancels any constant bias, and $\beta$ takes over its role.

For a similar reason, batch norm does not see the scale of what comes before it. If a bias-free convolution with weights $W$ computes the channel values $x_i$ from an input $u$, then scaling $u$ or $W$ by a factor $r > 0$ turns every $x_i$ into $r\,x_i$, because the convolution is linear. Then $\mu_B$ becomes $r\,\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ becomes $r^2\sigma_B^2$, and

$$
\frac{r x_i - r\mu_B}{\sqrt{r^2\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}} = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon/r^2}} .
$$

Up to the small change of $\varepsilon$ to $\varepsilon/r^2$, the output is unchanged ([Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015](#references), section 3.3). A bias-free convolution followed by batch norm is therefore insensitive to the overall scale of its input and of its weights.

Why batch norm helps is still debated; the explanations (internal covariate shift, a smoother loss, scale invariance, noise) are weighed in [the debated explanations](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#why-does-it-help-the-debated-explanations). Either way, it keeps the scale of activations under control, which is exactly the issue of the next section.

### Instance normalization

Batch norm pools each channel over the whole batch, so the output for one image depends on the other images in it. **Instance normalization** ([Ulyanov, Vedaldi and Lempitsky, 2016](#references), Eq. 3) standardizes each image on its own. For image $n$ and channel $c$ of an $H \times W$ feature map $x$,

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mu_{nc} = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} x_{nchw}, \qquad
\sigma_{nc}^2 = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} \left(x_{nchw} - \mu_{nc}\right)^2, \\
y_{nchw} = \gamma_c\,\frac{x_{nchw} - \mu_{nc}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{nc}^2 + \varepsilon}} + \beta_c ,
\end{gathered}
$$

with a learnable scale $\gamma_c$ and shift $\beta_c$ per channel, as in batch norm (PyTorch's `InstanceNorm2d` includes them only with `affine=True`). Batch, layer ([Ba, Kiros and Hinton, 2016](#references)), instance and group norm differ only in which values share a mean and a variance; [normalizing over other axes](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#normalizing-over-other-axes) draws the four sets side by side.

If the four values $(1, 2, 3, 6)$ of the worked example above are the $2 \times 2$ map of one channel of one image, instance norm (with $\gamma_c = 1$, $\beta_c = 0$) turns them into $(-1.069, -0.535, 0, 1.604)$, whatever the other images in the batch contain. This has three consequences:

- **No dependence on the batch.** Training and inference compute the same function, no running averages are needed, and a batch of a single image works.
- **Per-image contrast is removed.** The mean and the spread of every feature map of every image are discarded; $\gamma_c$ and $\beta_c$ are the same for all images and cannot restore them. This is what fast style transfer needs: the stylized image should not depend on the contrast of the content image (Ulyanov et al., section 2). Where the overall brightness or contrast of an image carries information, the layer throws it away.
- **It needs spatial extent.** On a $1 \times 1$ map each channel has a single value, its variance is zero, and the output is $\beta_c$ whatever the input.

Three uses of instance normalization:

- **Style transfer.** Replacing batch norm by instance norm in a feed-forward stylization network, and keeping it at test time, improved the stylized images markedly (Ulyanov et al., section 3).
- **CycleGAN.** Both the generators and the discriminators use it, and training runs with batches of one image ([Zhu et al., 2017](#references), section 4 and appendix 7.2; see [the networks of CycleGAN](/notes/generative-models/cyclegan#the-networks)).
- **The critic of WGAN-GP.** The [gradient penalty](/notes/generative-models/wgan#wgan-gp-gradient-penalty) constrains the gradient of the critic's output for each input separately, which is meaningful only if each output depends on its own input alone. Batch norm breaks this, so WGAN-GP omits it from the critic. Any normalization without interactions between examples is allowed; [Gulrajani et al. (2017)](#references), section 4, recommend [layer normalization](/notes/nlp/transformers#normalization), and instance normalization qualifies as well.

## Vanishing and exploding gradients

We train by computing the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight (backpropagation) and stepping against it. By the chain rule, the gradient that reaches an early layer is a **product** of one factor per layer, or per time step, that it passed through. With one unit per layer, $h_i = \varphi(a_i)$ with pre-activation $a_i = w_i h_{i-1} + b_i$ and activation function $\varphi$, each factor is

$$
\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial h_{i-1}} = w_i\,\varphi'(a_i),
$$

a weight times the slope of the activation. For layers with many units the factors are Jacobian matrices, and what matters is their size: the product of many Jacobians tends to grow or shrink geometrically with depth, at a rate set by their largest singular values ([Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2013](#references), section 2.1; [Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016](#references), sections 8.2.5 and 10.7). So if the per-layer factors are mostly

- larger than $1$ in size, the product grows geometrically: **exploding gradients**;
- smaller than $1$ in size, the product shrinks geometrically: **vanishing gradients**.

The further the factors are from $1$, the faster this happens, but the departure need not be large (see the table below). The problem was first analysed for recurrent networks ([Hochreiter, 1991](#references); [Bengio, Simard and Frasconi, 1994](#references)), where it is easiest to see. For layers of many units, the Deep Learning course derives the factor by which each layer multiplies the variance of the signal and of the gradient ([why the scale is multiplied at every layer](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#why-the-scale-is-multiplied-at-every-layer)); here we follow one unit.

### Example: an RNN

A recurrent network applies the *same* weight at every time step. Take a single recurrent unit with a ReLU, $h_t = \operatorname{ReLU}(w\,h_{t-1} + u\,x_t + b)$, and recurrent weight $w = 2$.

<RnnUnrolled />

Every step along the recurrent path multiplies by the same $w = 2$, so the sensitivity of a later state to an earlier one is $2$ after one step, $4$ after two, $8$ after three, and so on. Formally, let the loss be computed only from the last state $h_{k+\tau}$, $\tau$ steps after $h_k$ (as in the unrolled RNN above, where only the last output is used). While the ReLU is in its active region ($\operatorname{ReLU}' = 1$), each per-step factor $w\,\varphi'(a_t)$ equals $w$, and the chain rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}} &= w \quad \text{for every } t, \\
\frac{\partial h_{k+\tau}}{\partial h_k} &= \prod_{s=k+1}^{k+\tau} \frac{\partial h_s}{\partial h_{s-1}} = w^{\tau}, \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_k} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_{k+\tau}}\, w^{\tau} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The gradient sent back $\tau$ steps carries the factor $w^\tau$:

| weight | after 3 steps | after 10 steps | after 50 steps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $w = 2$ | $8$ | $1024$ | $\approx 1.1\cdot 10^{15}$ |
| $w = 1.1$ | $1.33$ | $2.59$ | $117$ |
| $w = 1$ | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $w = 0.9$ | $0.73$ | $0.35$ | $0.0052$ |
| $w = 0.5$ | $0.125$ | $\approx 9.8\cdot 10^{-4}$ | $\approx 8.9\cdot 10^{-16}$ |

Even a weight only 10 % away from $1$ changes the gradient by two orders of magnitude over 50 steps. Nothing in the table is specific to recurrence: a stack of $\tau$ layers with the same factor $w$ each gives the same $w^\tau$, and that depth-wise reading is all the autoencoders of the next notes need. Recurrent units and their gates are developed in the NLP course ([recurrent networks](/notes/nlp/recurrent-networks), [LSTM and GRU](/notes/nlp/lstm-and-gru)).

### What this does to training

A gradient-descent step is $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta L$, so the size of the step is proportional to the gradient.

- **Exploding:** the gradient arrives multiplied by a huge factor, so the update takes a very large step in the loss landscape. Each update overshoots, and the loss jumps around or diverges.
- **Vanishing:** the gradient that reaches the early layers of a deep stack carries one factor per layer. If the factors are small, the product goes to zero and the steps become tiny: the early weights barely move, and the training budget runs out long before the minimum is reached.

### Common remedies

The rest of the course relies on the standard fixes. The Deep Learning course derives them; here is what each does and where it is worked out.

- **Initialization** that keeps the scale of signals near 1: Xavier/Glorot initialization for saturating activations ([Glorot and Bengio, 2010](#references)) and He initialization for ReLUs ([He et al., 2015](#references)), derived in [initialization that keeps the factor at one](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#initialization-that-keeps-the-factor-at-one); the [convolution section](#a-convolution-layer) below gives their fan-in for a convolution and PyTorch's default.
- **Normalization layers** such as batch norm above.
- **Non-saturating activations** instead of sigmoids. The sigmoid's derivative is at most $\tfrac14$ (reached at $0$), so the factor $w_i\,\varphi'(a_i)$ of a sigmoid layer is at most $|w_i|/4$ in size: each sigmoid layer shrinks the gradient by at least a factor of 4 even when $|w_i| = 1$. **ReLU**, $\operatorname{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$ ([Nair and Hinton, 2010](#references)), has slope exactly $1$ wherever the unit is active. **LeakyReLU**, $\max(\alpha x,\ x)$ with a small slope $\alpha$ such as $0.2$ ([Maas, Hannun and Ng, 2013](#references)), also keeps slope $\alpha$ instead of $0$ for negative inputs, so an inactive unit still passes some gradient.
- **Gradient clipping** against explosions: rescale the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold ([Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2013](#references), section 3.2); [gradient clipping](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#gradient-clipping) compares clipping by norm and by value.
- **Gated recurrent cells.** The LSTM ([Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997](#references)) and the GRU ([Cho et al., 2014](#references)) give the state an additive path whose factor per step is a gate the network can keep near $1$ ([LSTM and GRU](/notes/nlp/lstm-and-gru)).
- **Residual connections** ([He et al., 2016a](#references)), $x_{l+1} = x_l + F(x_l)$. With a pure identity skip, as in pre-activation ResNets ([He et al., 2016b](#references), eq. 5), the Jacobian of a block is $I + \partial F/\partial x_l$, so the gradient always has a path whose factor is exactly $1$. What the identity path does to the forward scale is in [residual connections](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#residual-connections-an-identity-path-and-the-variance-it-adds), and the networks built on it in [residual networks](/notes/deep-learning/cnn-architectures#residual-networks-making-depth-trainable).

## A convolution layer

The convolution layer (`nn.Conv2d`) is built in the Deep Learning course: the sliding dot product and why libraries compute a cross-correlation ([the convolution is a cross-correlation](/notes/deep-learning/convolution#the-convolution-is-a-cross-correlation)), the weight tensor $\text{out}_c \times \text{in}_c \times k_H \times k_W$ with one bias per output channel, the parameter count and the cost ([channels and the weight shape](/notes/deep-learning/convolution#channels-and-the-weight-shape)), and padding, stride and dilation with the output size they give ([padding, stride and dilation](/notes/deep-learning/convolution#padding-stride-and-dilation)). In the notation of the figure below, $\text{in}[c]$ is input channel $c$, $W_{j,c}$ the $k_H \times k_W$ kernel that output channel $j$ applies to it, and $\star$ the cross-correlation:

$$
\text{out}[j] = \sum_{c=1}^{\text{in}_c} W_{j,c} \star \text{in}[c] + b_j .
$$

<ConvShapes />

The networks of the next notes need three more facts about the layer.

- **How the encoder halves the image.** The output height is $H_{\text{out}} = \lfloor (H_{\text{in}} + 2p - d\,(k_H - 1) - 1)/s \rfloor + 1$, and the same for the width ([Dumoulin and Visin, 2016](#references)). In the autoencoder of the next note each of the four encoder blocks has $k = 3,\ p = 1,\ s = 2,\ d = 1$, so $H_{\text{out}} = \lfloor (H_{\text{in}} - 1)/2 \rfloor + 1$ halves even sizes: $64 \to 32 \to 16 \to 8 \to 4$. Decoders go back up with a transposed convolution, which the Deep Learning course derives together with its checkerboard artifacts ([transposed convolution](/notes/deep-learning/convolution#transposed-convolution-going-up-in-resolution)), or with upsampling followed by a convolution; the [autoencoder note](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders#transposed-convolution-or-upsample--conv) compares the two.
- **Initialization.** For a convolution, $\text{fan}_{\text{in}} = \text{in}_c\, k_H k_W$ and $\text{fan}_{\text{out}} = \text{out}_c\, k_H k_W$ take the place of the widths in the Glorot and He variances ([Glorot and Bengio, 2010](#references); [He et al., 2015](#references), section 2.2; derived in [initialization that keeps the factor at one](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#initialization-that-keeps-the-factor-at-one)). PyTorch's default for `Conv2d` is a simpler fan-in rule, a uniform distribution on $\pm 1/\sqrt{\text{fan}_{\text{in}}}$ (implemented as `kaiming_uniform_` with $a = \sqrt5$). A uniform distribution on $\pm b$ has variance $b^2/3$, so this default has variance $1/(3\,\text{fan}_{\text{in}})$, six times smaller than the He variance. Without normalization, the signal variance of a deep ReLU stack initialized this way shrinks by about a factor of 6 per layer. In the networks of the next notes, batch norm after the convolutions restores the scale.
- **What the weights learn.** Kernels in the first layers typically become small detectors for edges, colour blobs and simple textures. Deeper layers combine them into parts and objects over larger receptive fields ([Zeiler and Fergus, 2014](#references), section 4 and figure 2). This is why pretrained convolutional networks are useful as feature extractors ([transfer learning](/notes/deep-learning/cnn-architectures#transfer-learning-from-imagenet-backbones)), and why the [evaluation metrics](/notes/generative-models/inception-score) of the course use them.

## A training run in practice

Around the model and its loss, every training run in the course follows the same routine. The Deep Learning course develops it in general ([the training pipeline](/notes/deep-learning/pytorch-and-autograd#the-training-pipeline), [reading the curves](/notes/deep-learning/training-in-practice#read-the-curves-underfitting-or-overfitting), [reproducible runs](/notes/deep-learning/training-in-practice#make-runs-reproducible)); these are the pieces the notebooks below rely on.

- **Optimizer.** Most models use Adam ([Kingma and Ba, 2015](#references)), which divides each coordinate's step by a running estimate of its gradient's magnitude (see the [fitting note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl#how-the-parameters-are-trained)). A common variant is **AdamW** ([Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019](#references), section 2), which shrinks the weights directly, outside the adaptive step, instead of adding an L2 penalty to the loss; why the two differ under Adam is worked out in [AdamW: decoupled weight decay](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#adamw-decoupled-weight-decay). PyTorch's `AdamW` defaults to $\eta = 10^{-3}$ and $\lambda = 10^{-2}$.
- **Validation.** After each epoch the loss is also computed on held-out data, with the model in evaluation mode (`model.eval()`, which switches batch norm to its running averages and turns dropout off) and without gradient tracking (`torch.no_grad()`, which saves memory and time and changes nothing else). Plotting both losses per epoch shows overfitting as a validation curve that levels off or rises while the training curve keeps falling.
- **Checkpoints.** The trained parameters (the model's `state_dict`) are saved to disk, so that the model can be reloaded and evaluated later without training it again.
- **Reproducibility.** Every random number generator involved is seeded: Python's `random`, NumPy, and PyTorch, whose `torch.manual_seed` covers the CPU and all GPUs. On GPUs, cuDNN can time several convolution algorithms and keep the fastest, which may differ between runs, and some algorithms are not deterministic; `torch.backends.cudnn.benchmark = False` and `torch.backends.cudnn.deterministic = True` turn both off, at some cost in speed. Even then, results are reproducible only on the same hardware and software versions ([PyTorch documentation](#references), "Reproducibility").
