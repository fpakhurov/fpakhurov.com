A VAE generates 64 faces with its batch-norm layers still in training mode. Each face is then normalized with the statistics of the other 63, so the same latent code decodes to a slightly different face in another batch. The critic of WGAN-GP, the gradient-penalty variant of the Wasserstein GAN, must contain no batch norm at all, and CycleGAN trains on one image at a time. The models of the [previous note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl) were too small to meet such details; every network from here on meets them.

The layers themselves are built in the Deep Learning course: [Normalization and initialization](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization), [Optimizing deep networks](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks) and [Convolution](/notes/deep-learning/convolution).

## Batch normalization

Batch normalization ([Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015](#references)) standardizes each channel with the statistics of the current mini-batch. For the $n$ values $x_1, \dots, x_n$ of one channel in a batch,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat x_i &= \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}}, \\
y_i &= \gamma\, \hat x_i + \beta ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ are the batch mean and the plain variance (divided by $n$), $\varepsilon = 10^{-5}$ in PyTorch, and $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are learned per channel. `BatchNorm2d` pools each channel over the batch and all positions. The batch $(1, 2, 3, 6)$ has $\mu_B = 3$ and $\sigma_B^2 = 3.5$ and becomes $(-1.069, -0.535, 0, 1.604)$.

<BatchNormHistogram />

In training the layer uses the batch statistics and updates running averages of them; in evaluation it uses the running averages, so an output no longer depends on the rest of the batch ([both modes worked through](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#batch-normalization-in-training-and-in-evaluation)). `model.eval()` switches the mode; `torch.no_grad()` does not, since it only stops autograd from recording the graph. A generator sampled in training mode therefore couples its samples to each other and drags the running averages toward the statistics of generated images.

### Scale invariance and bias-free convolutions \{#the-rest-of-the-layer\}

Subtracting $\mu_B$ cancels any constant bias, so a convolution followed by batch norm is created with `bias=False`. The layer also ignores the scale of what feeds it. Scaling the input or the weights of a bias-free convolution by $r > 0$ scales every $x_i$ and $\mu_B$ by $r$ and $\sigma_B^2$ by $r^2$:

$$
\frac{r x_i - r\mu_B}{\sqrt{r^2\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}} = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon/r^2}} .
$$

Up to the change of $\varepsilon$ to $\varepsilon/r^2$ the output is the same ([Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015](#references), section 3.3). A penalty on the size of whatever feeds such a block can be dodged by shrinking it, as the [sparse autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders#sparse-autoencoder) of the next note shows.

### Instance normalization

**Instance normalization** ([Ulyanov, Vedaldi and Lempitsky, 2016](#references)) standardizes every image on its own. For image $n$ and channel $c$ of an $H \times W$ feature map,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu_{nc} &= \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} x_{nchw}, \\
\sigma_{nc}^2 &= \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{h,w} \left(x_{nchw} - \mu_{nc}\right)^2, \\
y_{nchw} &= \gamma_c\, \frac{x_{nchw} - \mu_{nc}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{nc}^2 + \varepsilon}} + \beta_c .
\end{aligned}
$$

(`InstanceNorm2d` learns $\gamma_c$ and $\beta_c$ only with `affine=True`.) If $(1, 2, 3, 6)$ is the $2 \times 2$ map of one channel of one image, it becomes $(-1.069, -0.535, 0, 1.604)$ whatever the other images contain. Training and evaluation compute the same function, and a batch of one image works ([other axes](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#normalizing-over-other-axes)).

The price is that each feature map loses its own mean and spread, which the shared $\gamma_c$ and $\beta_c$ cannot restore. Fast style transfer wants that: the result should not depend on the contrast of the content image (Ulyanov et al., section 2).

Two later models depend on it. [CycleGAN](/notes/generative-models/cyclegan#the-networks) uses instance norm in its generators and discriminators and trains with batches of one image ([Zhu et al., 2017](#references), section 4). The [gradient penalty of WGAN-GP](/notes/generative-models/wgan#wgan-gp-gradient-penalty) constrains the critic's gradient for each input separately, which needs each output to depend on its own input alone; [Gulrajani et al. (2017)](#references), section 4, drop batch norm from the critic, and instance norm qualifies.

## Vanishing and exploding gradients

Backpropagation multiplies one factor per layer. With one unit per layer, $h_i = \varphi(a_i)$ and $a_i = w_i h_{i-1} + b_i$, the chain rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial h_{i-1}} &= w_i\, \varphi'(a_i), \\
\frac{\partial h_L}{\partial h_0} &= \prod_{i=1}^{L} w_i\, \varphi'(a_i) .
\end{aligned}
$$

With the same weight $w$ in every layer and ReLUs in their active region, the factor after $\tau$ layers is $w^\tau$. A recurrent network applies the same weight at every time step, so it meets the same power along time ([Bengio, Simard and Frasconi, 1994](#references); [recurrent networks](/notes/nlp/recurrent-networks)).

| weight | 3 layers | 10 layers | 50 layers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $w = 2$ | $8$ | $1024$ | $1.1\cdot 10^{15}$ |
| $w = 1.1$ | $1.33$ | $2.59$ | $117$ |
| $w = 0.9$ | $0.73$ | $0.35$ | $0.0052$ |
| $w = 0.5$ | $0.125$ | $9.8\cdot 10^{-4}$ | $8.9\cdot 10^{-16}$ |

A weight only 10% away from 1 changes the gradient by two orders of magnitude over 50 layers: **exploding** gradients above 1, **vanishing** below. With many units per layer the factors are Jacobians $J_i$. The norm of a product is at most the product of the norms, $\lVert J_L \cdots J_1\rVert \le \prod_i \lVert J_i\rVert$, and the norm of a matrix is its largest singular value, which therefore plays the role of $w$: if every one stays below 1, the gradient must vanish, and an explosion needs at least one above 1 ([Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2013](#references), section 2.1); the Deep Learning course derives the [factor per layer](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#why-the-scale-is-multiplied-at-every-layer).

<GradientFactor />

A step of plain gradient descent, $-\eta\, g$, is proportional to the gradient. An exploded gradient overshoots the minimum and the loss diverges; a vanished one leaves the early layers almost still.

<StepSizes />

Adam and AdamW behave differently. They divide a running mean $m$ of each coordinate's gradient by the root of a running mean $v$ of its square ([Adam](/notes/ml/stochastic-and-adaptive-optimizers#adam)). Multiplying every gradient by $c$ multiplies $m$ by $c$ and $v$ by $c^2$, so $m/\sqrt v$ and the step do not change: a consistently large or small gradient moves each weight by about $\eta$. A single spike $g$ far larger than the gradients before it gives $m \approx (1 - \beta_1)\,g = 0.1\,g$ and $v \approx (1 - \beta_2)\,g^2 = 0.001\,g^2$ late in training, a step of about $0.1/\sqrt{0.001} \approx 3.16\,\eta$ whatever the size of $g$. The failures above are therefore those of SGD. Adam absorbs a scale shared by all steps, though not a gradient below its $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$, where the division stops rescaling.

### Common remedies

The rest of the course relies on the standard fixes:

- [initialization](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#initialization-that-keeps-the-factor-at-one) that keeps the factor near 1, Glorot's or He's ([Glorot and Bengio, 2010](#references); [He et al., 2015](#references));
- normalization layers, as above;
- non-saturating activations. The sigmoid's derivative $\sigma(1 - \sigma)$ is at most $\tfrac14$, its value at $\sigma = \tfrac12$, so a sigmoid layer with $|w_i| \le 1$ shrinks the gradient at least fourfold. LeakyReLU, $\max(0.2x, x)$ in most networks of this course ([Maas, Hannun and Ng, 2013](#references)), has slope 1 for positive inputs and 0.2 for negative ones;
- [gradient clipping](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#gradient-clipping), $g \leftarrow g \cdot \min(1, c/\lVert g\rVert)$ for a threshold $c$ ([Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2013](#references), section 3.2);
- [residual connections](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#residual-connections-an-identity-path-and-the-variance-it-adds) $x_{l+1} = x_l + F(x_l)$, whose Jacobian $I + \partial F/\partial x_l$ always holds a path with factor 1 ([He et al., 2016](#references));
- gated recurrent cells ([LSTM and GRU](/notes/nlp/lstm-and-gru)).

## A convolution layer

A convolution layer holds one $k_H \times k_W$ kernel $W_{j,c}$ for every pair of input channel $c$ and output channel $j$. With $\star$ the cross-correlation,

$$
\text{out}[j] = \sum_{c=1}^{\text{in}_c} W_{j,c} \star \text{in}[c] + b_j .
$$

The [Deep Learning course](/notes/deep-learning/convolution#channels-and-the-weight-shape) derives the weight shape and the cost.

<ConvShapes />

With padding $p$, stride $s$ and dilation $d$, the output height is

$$
H_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor \frac{H_{\text{in}} + 2p - d\,(k_H - 1) - 1}{s} \right\rfloor + 1 ,
$$

and the same holds for the width ([Dumoulin and Visin, 2016](#references)). It counts window positions: the kernel spans $d(k_H - 1) + 1$ rows of the $H_{\text{in}} + 2p$ padded ones, and a window may start at every $s$-th row up to the last start that fits ([derivation](/notes/deep-learning/convolution#padding-stride-and-dilation)). The encoder of the next note uses $k = 3$, $p = 1$, $s = 2$ and $d = 1$, which halves an even side: $64 \to 32 \to 16 \to 8 \to 4$. Decoders go back up with a [transposed convolution](/notes/deep-learning/convolution#transposed-convolution-going-up-in-resolution) or with upsampling followed by a convolution.

A convolution has $\text{fan}_{\text{in}} = \text{in}_c\, k_H k_W$. PyTorch's default for `Conv2d` draws weights uniformly on $\pm 1/\sqrt{\text{fan}_{\text{in}}}$, with variance $1/(3\,\text{fan}_{\text{in}})$ (a uniform on $\pm b$ has variance $b^2/3$), a sixth of He's $2/\text{fan}_{\text{in}}$. A ReLU layer multiplies the signal variance by $\text{fan}_{\text{in}}\operatorname{Var}(w)/2$ ([the factor per layer](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#why-the-scale-is-multiplied-at-every-layer)), which He's choice sets to 1, so a plain ReLU stack at this default loses a factor of 6 per layer; here batch norm after each convolution restores the scale.

Trained first-layer kernels become detectors of edges, colour blobs and textures, and deeper layers combine them into parts and objects ([Zeiler and Fergus, 2014](#references)). That is why the [evaluation metrics](/notes/generative-models/inception-score) of the course compare images through pretrained convolutional features.

## A training run in practice

Runs follow the routine of [training in practice](/notes/deep-learning/training-in-practice): [AdamW](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#adamw-decoupled-weight-decay) at PyTorch's defaults, $\eta = 10^{-3}$ and weight decay $10^{-2}$ ([Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019](#references)), validation in `eval()` mode under `torch.no_grad()`, and a saved `state_dict`. Seeds are set for Python, NumPy and PyTorch. On GPUs cuDNN may pick the fastest of several convolution algorithms, which can differ between runs, and some are not deterministic; `cudnn.benchmark = False` and `cudnn.deterministic = True` turn both off at some cost in speed. Results then repeat only on the same hardware and software ([PyTorch documentation](#references), "Reproducibility").
