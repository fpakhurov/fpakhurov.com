The [previous note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl) fitted a model with a handful of parameters. From here on the models are neural networks. The [autoencoders](/notes/generative-models/autoencoders) of the next note are built from blocks of *convolution → batch normalization → activation*, and every later model is trained by backpropagating gradients through many such layers. The evaluation metrics later in the course also run images through pretrained convolutional networks. This note collects that background: what batch normalization computes, why gradients can vanish or explode, and how a convolution layer is shaped.

## Batch normalization

Batch normalization ([Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015](#references)) standardizes the activations of a layer using statistics of the current mini-batch. Take one feature (in a convolutional network, one channel) and a mini-batch of $n$ values $x_1, \dots, x_n$ of it. The layer first computes the **batch mean** and **batch variance**

$$
\mu_B = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i,
\qquad
\sigma_B^2 = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(x_i - \mu_B\right)^2,
$$

and then standardizes every value:

$$
\hat x_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}} .
$$

The small constant $\varepsilon > 0$ (PyTorch uses $10^{-5}$) keeps the division safe when a batch has almost no spread. After this step the batch has mean $0$ and variance $\sigma_B^2/(\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon) \approx 1$.

<BatchNormHistogram />

**A small worked example.** For the batch $x = (1, 2, 3, 6)$ with $\varepsilon = 10^{-5}$:

| | first | second | third | fourth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x_i$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $6$ |
| $x_i - \mu_B$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $3$ |
| $\hat x_i$ | $-1.069$ | $-0.535$ | $0$ | $1.604$ |

Here $\mu_B = 12/4 = 3$ and $\sigma_B^2 = (4 + 1 + 0 + 9)/4 = 3.5$, so every centred value is divided by $\sqrt{3.5 + 10^{-5}} \approx 1.871$. The variance divides by $n$, not $n - 1$: it is the plain variance of the batch.

### The rest of the layer

A full batch-norm layer has two learnable parameters per feature, a scale $\gamma$ and a shift $\beta$, and outputs

$$
y_i = \gamma\,\hat x_i + \beta .
$$

The parameters $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are fixed numbers, while $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ change from batch to batch, so the identity cannot be matched batch by batch. It can be matched against the population statistics of the feature: with $\gamma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[x] + \varepsilon}$ and $\beta = \mathbb E[x]$ the layer reproduces its input at inference, where running averages that estimate these population statistics (described below) replace $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$. In training it does so only approximately, up to the batch-to-batch fluctuation of $\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ ([Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015](#references), section 3). Standardization therefore does not restrict what the layer can express. It changes the parametrization, so that the scale of each feature is controlled by one parameter.

Training and inference behave differently. During training the layer uses the batch statistics $\mu_B, \sigma_B^2$ and also keeps running averages of them. In PyTorch, with the default momentum $0.1$, these are $\mu_{\text{run}} \leftarrow 0.9\,\mu_{\text{run}} + 0.1\,\mu_B$ and $\sigma^2_{\text{run}} \leftarrow 0.9\,\sigma^2_{\text{run}} + 0.1\,\tfrac{n}{n-1}\sigma_B^2$; the running variance stores the unbiased estimate. At inference, when a "batch" may be a single image, the running averages are used instead, so the output of one example no longer depends on the other examples in the batch. In PyTorch the switch is `model.eval()`. Turning off gradients with `torch.no_grad()` does *not* switch batch norm to its running statistics.

In a convolutional network, `BatchNorm2d` normalizes each channel over all values of that channel in the batch: batch size × height × width of them. A convolution followed by batch norm is usually created with `bias=False`, because subtracting $\mu_B$ cancels any constant bias, and $\beta$ takes over its role.

For a similar reason, batch norm does not see the scale of what comes before it. If a bias-free convolution with weights $W$ computes the channel values $x_i$ from an input $u$, then scaling $u$ or $W$ by a factor $r > 0$ turns every $x_i$ into $r\,x_i$, because the convolution is linear. Then $\mu_B$ becomes $r\,\mu_B$ and $\sigma_B^2$ becomes $r^2\sigma_B^2$, and

$$
\frac{r x_i - r\mu_B}{\sqrt{r^2\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon}} = \frac{x_i - \mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2 + \varepsilon/r^2}} .
$$

Up to the small change of $\varepsilon$ to $\varepsilon/r^2$, the output is unchanged ([Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015](#references), section 3.3). A bias-free convolution followed by batch norm is therefore insensitive to the overall scale of its input and of its weights.

Why does it help? The original paper motivated batch norm by reducing "internal covariate shift", the drift of each layer's input distribution during training. [Santurkar et al. (2018)](#references), sections 2–3, argue that the gain does not come from reducing internal covariate shift. In their analysis batch norm makes the loss and its gradients smoother, which tolerates larger learning rates; why batch norm works is still debated. Either way, it keeps the scale of activations under control, which is exactly the issue of the next section.

## Vanishing and exploding gradients

We train by computing the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight (backpropagation) and stepping against it. By the chain rule, the gradient that reaches an early layer is a **product** of one factor per layer, or per time step, that it passed through. With one unit per layer, $h_i = \varphi(a_i)$ with pre-activation $a_i = w_i h_{i-1} + b_i$ and activation function $\varphi$, each factor is

$$
\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial h_{i-1}} = w_i\,\varphi'(a_i),
$$

a weight times the slope of the activation. For layers with many units the factors are Jacobian matrices, and what matters is their size: the product of many Jacobians tends to grow or shrink geometrically with depth, at a rate set by their largest singular values ([Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2013](#references), section 2.1; [Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016](#references), sections 8.2.5 and 10.7). So if the per-layer factors are mostly

- larger than $1$ in size, the product grows geometrically: **exploding gradients**;
- smaller than $1$ in size, the product shrinks geometrically: **vanishing gradients**.

The further the factors are from $1$, the faster this happens, but the departure need not be large (see the table below). The problem was first analysed for recurrent networks ([Hochreiter, 1991](#references); [Bengio, Simard and Frasconi, 1994](#references)), where it is easiest to see.

### Example: an RNN

A recurrent network applies the *same* weight at every time step. Take a single recurrent unit with a ReLU, $h_t = \operatorname{ReLU}(w\,h_{t-1} + u\,x_t + b)$, and recurrent weight $w = 2$.

<RnnUnrolled />

Every step along the recurrent path multiplies by the same $w = 2$, so the sensitivity of a later state to an earlier one is $2$ after one step, $4$ after two, $8$ after three, and so on. Formally, let the loss be computed only from the last state $h_{k+\tau}$, $\tau$ steps after $h_k$ (as in the unrolled RNN above, where only the last output is used). While the ReLU is in its active region ($\operatorname{ReLU}' = 1$), each per-step factor $w\,\varphi'(a_t)$ equals $w$, and the chain rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}} &= w \quad \text{for every } t, \\
\frac{\partial h_{k+\tau}}{\partial h_k} &= \prod_{s=k+1}^{k+\tau} \frac{\partial h_s}{\partial h_{s-1}} = w^{\tau}, \\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_k} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_{k+\tau}}\, w^{\tau} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The gradient sent back $\tau$ steps carries the factor $w^\tau$:

| weight | after 3 steps | after 10 steps | after 50 steps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $w = 2$ | $8$ | $1024$ | $\approx 1.1\cdot 10^{15}$ |
| $w = 1.1$ | $1.33$ | $2.59$ | $117$ |
| $w = 1$ | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $w = 0.9$ | $0.73$ | $0.35$ | $0.0052$ |
| $w = 0.5$ | $0.125$ | $\approx 9.8\cdot 10^{-4}$ | $\approx 8.9\cdot 10^{-16}$ |

Even a weight only 10 % away from $1$ changes the gradient by two orders of magnitude over 50 steps.

### What this does to training

A gradient-descent step is $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta L$, so the size of the step is proportional to the gradient.

- **Exploding:** the gradient arrives multiplied by a huge factor, so the update takes a very large step in the loss landscape. Each update overshoots, and the loss jumps around or diverges.
- **Vanishing:** the gradient that reaches the early layers of a deep stack carries one factor per layer. If the factors are small, the product goes to zero and the steps become tiny: the early weights barely move, and the training budget runs out long before the minimum is reached.

### Common remedies

The rest of the course relies on the standard fixes:

- **Initialization** that keeps the scale of signals near 1: Xavier/Glorot initialization for saturating activations ([Glorot and Bengio, 2010](#references)) and He initialization for ReLUs ([He et al., 2015](#references)), described in the next section.
- **Normalization layers** such as batch norm above.
- **Non-saturating activations** instead of sigmoids. The sigmoid's derivative is at most $\tfrac14$ (reached at $0$), so the factor $w_i\,\varphi'(a_i)$ of a sigmoid layer is at most $|w_i|/4$ in size: each sigmoid layer shrinks the gradient by at least a factor of 4 even when $|w_i| = 1$. **ReLU**, $\operatorname{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$ ([Nair and Hinton, 2010](#references)), has slope exactly $1$ wherever the unit is active. **LeakyReLU**, $\max(\alpha x,\ x)$ with a small slope $\alpha$ such as $0.2$ ([Maas, Hannun and Ng, 2013](#references)), also keeps slope $\alpha$ instead of $0$ for negative inputs, so an inactive unit still passes some gradient.
- **Gradient clipping** against explosions: rescale the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold ([Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio, 2013](#references), section 3.2).
- **Gated recurrent cells.** In the LSTM ([Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997](#references)) the cell state is updated additively. In the original cell its factor per step is exactly $1$. The cell used today adds a forget gate ([Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins, 2000](#references)), and the factor equals that gate: a number in $(0, 1)$ that the network can keep near $1$. The GRU ([Cho et al., 2014](#references)) works the same way: it interpolates between the old state and a candidate, so the direct path carries the factor of its update gate.
- **Residual connections** ([He et al., 2016a](#references)), $x_{l+1} = x_l + F(x_l)$. With a pure identity skip, as in pre-activation ResNets ([He et al., 2016b](#references), eq. 5), the Jacobian of a block is $I + \partial F/\partial x_l$, so the gradient always has a path whose factor is exactly $1$.

## A convolution layer

How a 2-D convolution layer (`nn.Conv2d`) is parametrized and trained ([Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016](#references), chapter 9; [Dumoulin and Visin, 2016](#references)):

- **The kernels are trained.** All entries of the weight tensor and the bias are learned by gradient descent:

  $$
  W \in \mathbb R^{\text{out}_c \times \text{in}_c \times k_H \times k_W},
  \qquad
  b \in \mathbb R^{\text{out}_c} .
  $$

- **Notation.** $\text{in}[c]$ is input channel number $c$, an $H_{\text{in}} \times W_{\text{in}}$ plane (subscripted $W_{\text{in}}, W_{\text{out}}$ are widths; $W$, $W_j$, $W_{j,c}$ are always weights). $W_{j,c}$ is the $k_H \times k_W$ kernel that output channel $j$ applies to input channel $c$. Collected over $c$, $W_j \in \mathbb R^{\text{in}_c \times k_H \times k_W}$ is the **filter** for output channel $j$.
- **Output channel $j$**, for $j = 1, \dots, \text{out}_c$, sums the per-channel results and adds one scalar bias:

  $$
  \text{out}[j] = \sum_{c=1}^{\text{in}_c} W_{j,c} \star \text{in}[c] + b_j .
  $$

  Each $\text{out}[j]$ is an $H_{\text{out}} \times W_{\text{out}}$ plane. There is one bias per output channel, so the bias vector has $\text{out}_c$ entries.

<ConvShapes />

Written out entry by entry for stride $s$ and dilation $d$, the operation $\star$ is a sliding dot product. Deep-learning libraries compute a cross-correlation and do not flip the kernel, unlike the convolution of signal processing; since the kernel is learned, the difference does not matter.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{out}[j]_{y,x} = b_j
&+ \sum_{c=1}^{\text{in}_c}\sum_{m=0}^{k_H-1}\sum_{l=0}^{k_W-1} W_{j,c,m,l} \\
&\quad\cdot\ \text{in}[c]_{\,s y + d m,\; s x + d l} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $y = 0, \dots, H_{\text{out}} - 1$, $x = 0, \dots, W_{\text{out}} - 1$, and $\text{in}[c]$ denotes the input after zero-padding by $p$ on each side, indexed from $0$. The formula assumes one group, so every output channel sees every input channel; grouped convolutions restrict these connections.

- **Hyperparameters, not trained:** stride, padding, kernel size and dilation are fixed when the layer is built. They determine the output size ([PyTorch documentation](#references)):

  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  H_{\text{out}} &= \left\lfloor \frac{N_H}{s} \right\rfloor + 1, \\
  N_H &= H_{\text{in}} + 2p - d\,(k_H - 1) - 1
  \end{aligned}
  $$

  for the height, where $N_H$ is the padded height minus the extent $d\,(k_H - 1) + 1$ of the dilated kernel. The same holds for $W_{\text{out}}$ with $W_{\text{in}}$ and $k_W$. In the autoencoder of the next note each of the four encoder blocks has $k = 3,\ p = 1,\ s = 2,\ d = 1$, so $H_{\text{out}} = \lfloor (H_{\text{in}} - 1)/2 \rfloor + 1$ halves even sizes: $64 \to 32 \to 16 \to 8 \to 4$.
- **Initialization.** The kernels start random. With $\text{fan}_{\text{in}} = \text{in}_c\, k_H k_W$ and $\text{fan}_{\text{out}} = \text{out}_c\, k_H k_W$, Xavier/Glorot initialization draws weights with variance $2/(\text{fan}_{\text{in}} + \text{fan}_{\text{out}})$ ([Glorot and Bengio, 2010](#references)), and He initialization with variance $2/\text{fan}_{\text{in}}$ for layers followed by a ReLU ([He et al., 2015](#references), section 2.2). Both are designed for the goal of the previous section: keep signals and gradients at a constant scale from layer to layer. PyTorch's default for `Conv2d` is a simpler fan-in rule, a uniform distribution on $\pm 1/\sqrt{\text{fan}_{\text{in}}}$ (implemented as `kaiming_uniform_` with $a = \sqrt5$). A uniform distribution on $\pm b$ has variance $b^2/3$, so this default has variance $1/(3\,\text{fan}_{\text{in}})$, six times smaller than the He variance. Without normalization, the signal variance of a deep ReLU stack initialized this way shrinks by about a factor of 6 per layer. In the networks of the next notes, batch norm after the convolutions restores the scale.
- **What the weights learn.** Kernels in the first layers typically become small detectors for edges, colour blobs and simple textures. Deeper layers combine them into parts and objects over larger receptive fields ([Zeiler and Fergus, 2014](#references), section 4 and figure 2). This is why pretrained convolutional networks are useful as feature extractors for the [evaluation metrics](/notes/generative-models/inception-score) of the course.

For scale: a layer with $\text{in}_c = 16$, $\text{out}_c = 32$ and $3 \times 3$ kernels has $32 \cdot 16 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 4608$ weights plus $32$ biases.
