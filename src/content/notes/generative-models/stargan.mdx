A face dataset labels every photo with its hair colour, gender and age. You want one tool that takes any face and makes it blond, or male, or old, or all three at once. [CycleGAN](/notes/generative-models/cyclegan) translates between two domains with two generators. Here every request is a different pair of domains, and a separate model per pair does not scale.

**StarGAN** ([Choi et al., 2018](#references)) makes the target domain an input of a single generator, and a single discriminator also says which domain an image belongs to. **StarGAN v2** ([Choi et al., 2020](#references)) then removes CycleGAN's other limit, one output per input, by replacing the label with a style code drawn from a learned space of styles of that domain.

## Why not one CycleGAN per pair?

A **domain** is a set of images that share an attribute value: blond hair, male, a happy expression. A translator between $k$ domains must handle every ordered pair of them, $k(k-1)$ directions. CycleGAN learns the two directions of a pair together, so it needs $k(k-1)/2$ models and $k(k-1)$ generators: 12 for four domains, 56 for the eight facial expressions of the RaFD dataset.

Each of these generators also learns from the images of two domains only, although the shape of a face or the texture of skin is shared by all of them.

StarGAN makes the target an input. Its generator takes an image $x$ and a target label $c$ and returns $G(x, c)$. In training, $c$ is drawn at random, so one network learns every direction, and at test time any label can be requested. This is the [conditional GAN](/notes/generative-models/gans#conditional-gan) with an image in place of the noise.

## How the generator reads the label

A label is a vector with $n_c$ entries: binary for attributes that can occur together (on CelebA, black, blond and brown hair, male, young), one-hot for categories that exclude each other (the eight expressions of RaFD). The generator copies each entry over a constant $h \times w$ map and concatenates these maps with the three colour channels, so its first layer sees an $h \times w \times (3 + n_c)$ tensor.

The rest is the [CycleGAN generator](/notes/generative-models/cyclegan#the-networks) at $128 \times 128$ with six residual blocks and instance normalization. Each label entry adds only the weights of one input channel of the first convolution; nothing else depends on the number of domains.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Why StarGAN pads with zeros.</strong> A convolution adds the responses to the image and label maps. Reflection padding keeps a constant label map constant, so its convolution contributes a constant per channel. During training, instance normalization using that image's own mean removes this additive constant. Zero padding makes the label contribution spatially varying within 3 pixels of the border of a $7\times7$ convolution: $1-(122/128)^2\approx9.2\%$ of a $128\times128$ map. After normalization the effect is not confined to that border: the channel mean and variance depend on it, affecting interior values too. The official implementation documents the reflection-padding problem; the local verifier checks the mechanism on an explicitly constructed numeric map, without importing model code.</aside>

## The discriminator: real or fake, and which domain

The discriminator has one convolutional trunk and two heads: $D_{\mathrm{src}}$ scores realism, and $D_{\mathrm{cls}}$ outputs one logit per label entry. The second head is the auxiliary classifier of AC-GAN ([Odena, Olah and Shlens, 2017](#references)). Let $c'$ be the original label of a real image $x$. The classifier enters two losses:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L^{r}_{\mathrm{cls}} &= \mathbb E_{x, c'}\big[-\log D_{\mathrm{cls}}(c' \mid x)\big], \\
\mathcal L^{f}_{\mathrm{cls}} &= \mathbb E_{x, c}\big[-\log D_{\mathrm{cls}}\big(c \mid G(x, c)\big)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

$D$ minimizes the first: on real images it learns their true labels. $G$ minimizes the second: a translated image must be classified as the target domain. The classifier is trained on real images only, so it encodes what the domains look like in the data, and the generator is rewarded for producing exactly that.

For a one-hot label, $D_{\mathrm{cls}}(c \mid x)$ is a softmax probability and $-\log$ of it a cross-entropy. The entries of a binary attribute vector can co-occur, so each gets its own sigmoid, and the attributes are treated as independent given the image. With logits $\ell_i$,

$$
D_{\mathrm{cls}}(c \mid x) = \prod_i \sigma(\ell_i)^{c_i}\big(1 - \sigma(\ell_i)\big)^{1 - c_i},
$$

so $-\log D_{\mathrm{cls}}(c \mid x)$ is a sum of binary cross-entropies, which is what the authors' implementation computes for CelebA.

### Worked example: classifying a fake

Use an original synthetic tile with four independent binary attributes: striped, bright, wide and tilted. The requested label is $c=(1,0,1,0)$ and the example logits are $(0.8,-0.3,1.1,0.4)$. No image dataset or trained classifier supplies these values. Each attribute contributes $-\big[c_i\log\sigma(\ell_i)+(1-c_i)\log(1-\sigma(\ell_i))\big]$:

| | striped | bright | wide | tilted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| target $c_i$ | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| logit $\ell_i$ | 0.8 | −0.3 | 1.1 | 0.4 |
| $\sigma(\ell_i)$ | 0.690 | 0.426 | 0.750 | 0.599 |
| loss term | 0.371 | 0.554 | 0.287 | 0.913 |

The sum is approximately $2.126=-\log 0.119$. The largest contribution is “tilted”: the target is zero, but its predicted probability exceeds one half. Changing the striped and wide logits to $-0.8$ and $-1.1$ raises the loss by $0.8+1.1=1.9$, to about $4.026$. The logit gradient is $\sigma(\ell_i)-c_i$; during a generator update it propagates through the fixed classifier into $G$. It rewards matching the target label, but says nothing by itself about preserving the input's content.

## Adversarial loss and the way back

For the realism head, StarGAN uses the [WGAN-GP](/notes/generative-models/wgan#wgan-gp-gradient-penalty) objective, with $D_{\mathrm{src}}$ a critic with a real-valued output:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{adv}} = {}& \mathbb E_x\big[D_{\mathrm{src}}(x)\big] - \mathbb E_{x, c}\big[D_{\mathrm{src}}(G(x, c))\big] \\
&- \lambda_{\mathrm{gp}}\, \mathbb E_{\hat x}\Big[\big(\|\nabla_{\hat x} D_{\mathrm{src}}(\hat x)\|_2 - 1\big)^2\Big],
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\hat x$ is drawn on segments between real and translated images and $\lambda_{\mathrm{gp}} = 10$. The discriminator maximizes $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{adv}}$, with the generated images detached for that update. The generator minimizes only $-\mathbb E_{x,c}D_{\mathrm{src}}(G(x,c))$; the gradient penalty is a discriminator regularizer and is not differentiated through $G$.

Neither the adversarial nor the classification objective explicitly compares the translated image with its input: any realistic image of domain $c$ satisfies both, whatever $x$ was. This is CycleGAN's problem with [the adversarial loss alone](/notes/generative-models/cyclegan#without-pairs-the-adversarial-loss-is-not-enough), and StarGAN uses the same remedy, a [cycle-consistency](/notes/generative-models/cyclegan#cycle-consistency) loss:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rec}} = \mathbb E_{x, c, c'}\, \big\|x - G\big(G(x, c),\, c'\big)\big\|_1 .
$$

The way back is the same generator with the original label $c'$. Inverting a translation is just another translation, so no second network is needed.

## The full objective

Each player has its own classification term, so the two minimize different objectives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_D &= -\mathcal L_{\mathrm{adv}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{cls}}\, \mathcal L^{r}_{\mathrm{cls}}, \\
\mathcal L_G &= -\mathbb E_{x,c}D_{\mathrm{src}}(G(x,c)) + \lambda_{\mathrm{cls}}\, \mathcal L^{f}_{\mathrm{cls}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}\, \mathcal L_{\mathrm{rec}},
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\lambda_{\mathrm{cls}} = 1$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}} = 10$ in all experiments. Training uses Adam with an initial learning rate $10^{-4}$, five critic updates per generator update as in WGAN-GP, and batches of 16. The authors' implementation draws target labels by shuffling the labels of the batch, so every requested label is a combination that occurs in the data.

## Training on several datasets: the mask vector

CelebA labels hair colour, gender and age; RaFD (4,824 images of 67 people) labels expressions. A model trained on both could give CelebA faces the expressions learned from RaFD. The obstacle is the reconstruction loss: it needs the full original label $c'$, and each dataset knows only its own part of it.

StarGAN appends a **mask vector** $m$, a one-hot vector over the $n$ datasets that says which part of the label is known, and sets the unknown parts to zero:

$$
\tilde c = [\,c_1, \dots, c_n, m\,].
$$

For CelebA and RaFD the unified label has $5 + 8 + 2 = 15$ entries. A blond young woman from CelebA is $(0, 1, 0, 0, 1 \mid 0, \dots, 0 \mid 1, 0)$; the request "make her happy" is $(0, 0, 0, 0, 0 \mid$ one-hot for happy $\mid 0, 1)$. The classifier predicts all $5 + 8 = 13$ labels, but on a batch from one dataset only the outputs for that dataset's labels enter $\mathcal L^{r}_{\mathrm{cls}}$ and $\mathcal L^{f}_{\mathrm{cls}}$, and training alternates between the datasets.

The model learns to read the mask. Given an expression label with the wrong mask $[1, 0]$, it ignores the expression, reads the all-zero CelebA part as "not young", and ages the face ([Choi et al., 2018](#references), Figure 7).

## One output per image and domain

Sharing helps: a model for two RaFD expressions sees about 1,000 images, StarGAN about 4,000. An expression classifier that errs on 0.45% of real test images errs on 2.12% of StarGAN's translations and 5.99% of CycleGAN's ([Choi et al., 2018](#references), Table 3).

But $G(x, c)$ is a function: the same image and label always give the same translation. A domain contains many appearances. "Female" covers every hairstyle and every kind of makeup, and StarGAN picks one of them for each input. On CelebA-HQ it changes the gender of a face only locally, mostly by applying makeup ([Choi et al., 2020](#references)).

The obvious fix is to give the generator noise as well. In the reported StarGAN v2 ablation, the authors tried it: feed a latent code $z$ to $G$ next to the domain and ask an encoder to recover it from the output. The outputs became varied, but the codes had no way to separate domains, the authors conjecture that this favoured domain-shared variations, such as colour, over domain-specific variations, such as hairstyle. The fix that worked maps the noise to a style of a particular domain first.

## StarGAN v2: a style code instead of a label

The v2 explanation and local schematic below adapt the method described by Yunjey Choi, Youngjung Uh, Jaejun Yoo and Jung-Woo Ha in [StarGAN v2, arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01865v2), licensed [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). Wording, organization and diagram layout are changed; see the [scoped attribution](/licenses/stargan-v2-paper.txt). The article license does not license the separately distributed code, models or images used as training data.

### Four networks

From here on, $y$ is the original domain of $x$, $\tilde y$ a target domain, $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ a latent code in $\mathbb R^{16}$ and $s \in \mathbb R^{64}$ a style code. StarGAN v2 has four networks.

The **generator** $G(x, s)$ translates $x$ to an image with the style $s$. It receives no domain label, because the style code already belongs to a domain. The **mapping network** $F$ turns a latent code into a style code of a chosen domain, $s = F_{\tilde y}(z)$: an MLP with four shared layers and a branch of four more for each domain. The **style encoder** $E$ reads the style of an image of domain $y$, $s = E_y(x)$, with a shared trunk and one output head per domain. The **discriminator** $D$ has one output head per domain.

Latent-guided translation samples $z$ and uses $s = F_{\tilde y}(z)$, so varying $z$ can produce different outputs; the losses below encourage the model to use that variation. Reference-guided translation uses $s = E_{\tilde y}(x_{\mathrm{ref}})$ for a real image $x_{\mathrm{ref}}$ of the target domain, and the output takes on its style.

### How the style enters \{#how-the-style-enters-adain\}

The style enters through [adaptive instance normalization](/notes/generative-models/stylegan#styles-through-adain), as in StyleGAN: each channel is normalized over its positions, then scaled and shifted by numbers computed from $s$. The official implementation writes the scale as $1 + \gamma(s)$, so a zero style map starts as plain instance normalization ([Clova AI Research, 2020](#references)). The downsampling blocks use plain instance normalization; the last two bottleneck blocks and all upsampling blocks use AdaIN.

This is where StarGAN's padding problem disappears. StarGAN's label entered before normalization and relied on a boundary-induced spatial variation. The style acts after the normalization, which therefore cannot remove it.

### One discriminator head per domain

StarGAN v2's discriminator asks one question per domain: is this a real image of domain $y$? Each head $D_y$ is a binary real-versus-fake classifier, and there is no classification loss. The authors report that this **multi-task discriminator** ([Mescheder, Geiger and Nowozin, 2018](#references); [Liu et al., 2019](#references)) lets the generator change the global structure of an image, where the auxiliary classifier led to local edits.

### Losses

Draw a target domain $\tilde y$ and a latent code $z$, and let $s = F_{\tilde y}(z)$. The head of the target domain judges the translation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{adv}} = {}& \mathbb E_{x, y}\big[\log D_y(x)\big] \\
&+ \mathbb E_{x, \tilde y, z}\big[\log\big(1 - D_{\tilde y}(G(x, s))\big)\big],
\end{aligned}
$$

where $D_y$ is a probability, a sigmoid of the head's logit. In practice the generator uses the [non-saturating loss](/notes/generative-models/gans#which-generator-loss-is-used-in-practice) $-\log D_{\tilde y}(G(x, s))$.

Three more terms tie the output to the style and to the input. Style reconstruction asks the style encoder to read $s$ back from the output, penalizing $G$ when it ignores distinct styles. This term alone does not prevent the mapping network from collapsing different latent codes to one style. Style diversification draws a second code $s_2 = F_{\tilde y}(z_2)$ and rewards the generator for a second output that differs from the first. The cycle returns to $x$ with the input's own estimated style $\hat s = E_y(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{sty}} &= \mathbb E\, \big\|s - E_{\tilde y}\big(G(x, s)\big)\big\|_1, \\
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ds}} &= \mathbb E\, \big\|G(x, s) - G(x, s_2)\big\|_1, \\
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{cyc}} &= \mathbb E\, \big\|x - G\big(G(x, s),\, \hat s\big)\big\|_1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

StarGAN's way back used the original label $c'$, which says only which domain to return to. Here the return must restore the particular appearance of the input. The generator, mapping network and style encoder play against the discriminator:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min_{G, F, E}\; \max_D\;\; & \mathcal L_{\mathrm{adv}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{sty}}\, \mathcal L_{\mathrm{sty}} \\
& - \lambda_{\mathrm{ds}}\, \mathcal L_{\mathrm{ds}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}}\, \mathcal L_{\mathrm{cyc}},
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\lambda_{\mathrm{sty}} = \lambda_{\mathrm{cyc}} = 1$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{ds}} = 1$ on CelebA-HQ, 2 on the animal-face dataset AFHQ. The diversity term is maximized. On an unconstrained output space it can grow without bound; even with bounded pixels it can reward unrealistic differences. The authors linearly decay its weight to zero during training to stabilize the competing objectives; this is a training choice, not a proof of stability. The same objective is also trained with reference-guided styles in place of $F_{\tilde y}(z)$.

### R1: a penalty on real images only

StarGAN used WGAN-GP. StarGAN v2 uses the non-saturating loss and adds the **R1 penalty** of [Mescheder, Geiger and Nowozin (2018)](#references) to the discriminator's loss:

$$
R_1 = \frac{\gamma}{2}\, \mathbb E_{x \sim P}\, \big\|\nabla_x D(x)\big\|_2^2 ,
$$

with $\gamma = 1$, where $D(x)$ is the discriminator's logit and $P$ the data. The penalty acts on real images only and pulls the gradient towards zero, where WGAN-GP pulls it towards norm 1 on interpolates. A linear logit $D(x) = w^\top x$ with $w = (0.6, -0.8, 0)$ shows the difference: $\|w\|_2 = 1$, so the WGAN-GP penalty is 0, while R1 is $\tfrac12\|w\|_2^2 = 0.5$. R1 asks for a discriminator that is flat on the data.

Why flat? Mescheder et al. answer with the smallest possible GAN, the Dirac-GAN. The data are a point mass at 0, the generator is a point mass at $\theta$, and the discriminator's logit is $D(x) = \psi x$. With $f(t) = \log \sigma(t)$, the value is $V = f(0) + f(-\psi\theta)$: the real point scores $f(D(0))$ and the fake one $f(-D(\theta))$. The discriminator maximizes $V - \tfrac\gamma2 \psi^2$, the R1 penalty being $\tfrac\gamma2\psi^2$ because $\nabla_x D = \psi$; the generator minimizes $V$. Gradient ascent and descent in continuous time, linearized at the equilibrium $\theta = \psi = 0$ with $a = f'(0) = \tfrac12$, give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot\theta &= -\partial_\theta V = a\,\psi, \\
\dot\psi &= \partial_\psi V - \gamma\psi = -a\,\theta - \gamma\psi, \\
\tfrac{d}{dt}\big(\theta^2 + \psi^2\big) &= 2\theta\dot\theta + 2\psi\dot\psi = -2\gamma\psi^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Without the penalty, $\gamma = 0$, the distance to the equilibrium is conserved: the two players circle it forever. With $\gamma > 0$ it shrinks. The eigenvalues of this linear system solve $\lambda^2 + \gamma\lambda + a^2 = 0$, so for $\gamma = 1$ both equal $-\tfrac12$ and the players approach the equilibrium at the rate $e^{-t/2}$, up to a linear factor. This calculation concerns the local linearized continuous-time system, not a general neural training trajectory. Their Theorem 4.1 proves local convergence of simultaneous and alternating updates under assumptions I–III: a realizable equilibrium, a discriminator zero in a neighbourhood of the data support, regular parameterization manifolds and nondegeneracy/expressivity conditions, with sufficiently small learning rates. It is not a global convergence guarantee for StarGAN v2.

## How translation models are measured

The comparisons of StarGAN v2 follow a fixed protocol ([Choi et al., 2020](#references), Appendix C). For realism, every test image of a source domain is translated to a target domain with 10 random latent codes, and the [FID](/notes/generative-models/fid) between these translations and the training images of the target domain is computed and averaged over pairs of domains. For diversity, the [LPIPS](/notes/generative-models/lpips#where-it-is-used) distances between the $\binom{10}{2} = 45$ pairs of outputs of each test image are averaged.

Selected rows from the authors' Table 1, with abbreviated labels, under CC BY 4.0: the CelebA-HQ ablation compares successive changes. These are published research measurements, not results of our synthetic practice.

| Configuration | FID | LPIPS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. StarGAN | 98.4 | – |
| C. + multi-task $D$, R1, AdaIN | 80.5 | – |
| D. + latent code fed to $G$ | 32.3 | 0.312 |
| E. + mapping network and style code | 17.1 | 0.405 |
| F. + style diversification: StarGAN v2 | 13.7 | 0.452 |

The authors attribute the C-to-D gap to the number of output samples: D supplies ten outputs per input where C supplies one. That comparison mixes a model change with [sample-size bias in FID](/notes/generative-models/fid#drawbacks). Equal output counts in D, E and F make the comparison more informative, but a lower FID is still a distributional score, not proof of better input-preserving translations; empirical FID is not guaranteed to decrease in every larger finite sample. For reference, the FID between the training and the test images of CelebA-HQ is 14.8, and StarGAN v2 lies below it; with a small test set, that number is no floor.

Neither score looks at the input: a generator that ignores $x$ and draws good faces scores well. A fair report adds domain accuracy, as in StarGAN's RaFD experiment, and a check that pose and identity survive.

## Where it breaks

Diversity scores count artifacts as diversity. Against two-domain models trained once per pair of domains, StarGAN v2 has the lowest FID on AFHQ, 16.2 against 41.5 for MUNIT. Yet MUNIT's LPIPS, 0.511, is higher than StarGAN v2's 0.450 (Table 2). The authors conjecture that spurious artifacts in MUNIT's outputs explain this: two outputs that differ by artifacts are far apart in LPIPS, and the score reads that as variety.

The published StarGAN v2 setup assigns each training image one domain index, because $F_y$, $E_y$ and $D_y$ are heads indexed by it. Overlapping attributes are not interchangeable with that single categorical index: one CelebA face is male, young and smiling at once, so directly treating its 40 attributes as 40 mutually exclusive domains would discard this overlap. A usable partition takes one binary attribute, mutually exclusive values of one attribute, or the products of a few attributes, such as male or female times young or old. The mask vector, which joined datasets with different label sets, has no counterpart.

## What changed

| | CycleGAN | StarGAN | StarGAN v2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Networks for $k$ domains | $k(k-1)$ generators and discriminators | one $G$, one $D$ | one each of $G$, $F$, $E$, $D$ |
| Condition | none | target label, tiled and concatenated | style code through AdaIN |
| Discriminator | one per domain | critic and domain classifier | one real/fake head per domain |
| Tie to the input | two cycles, identity loss | cycle with the original label | cycle with the input's style |
| Outputs per input and target | one | one | many |
| Labels | two unlabelled collections | any combination of attributes | one domain per image |

## What you should remember

- One $G(x, c)$ replaces $k(k-1)$ pairwise generators and learns from all domains.
- $D$ classifies real images; $G$ must make fakes it assigns to the target; the way back is $G(G(x, c), c')$.
- A mask vector marks which dataset's labels are known, so one model trains on several datasets.
- StarGAN v2: a style $F_{\tilde y}(z)$ or $E_{\tilde y}(x_{\mathrm{ref}})$ enters through AdaIN; style, diversity and cycle losses.
- R1 penalizes $\|\nabla_x D\|^2$ on real data; local convergence results require the stated equilibrium and regularity assumptions.
- FID depends on sample count; diversity scores can reward artifacts.

The [synthetic diagnostic](https://github.com/fpakhurov/fpakhurov.com/blob/main/scripts/notes/generative-practice.py) represents an object by three coordinates: two preserve its content, and the third stores a domain offset plus style. Domain offsets are −2 and 2; style lies between −0.4 and 0.4. Three content points are authored directly in the script. There are no faces, overlapping attribute labels or model checkpoints.

- Translate from the negative to the positive domain while retaining the first two coordinates; restore the original domain and style. The [saved output](https://github.com/fpakhurov/fpakhurov.com/blob/main/docs/reports/2026-11-13-licensed-example-renewal/generative-practice-results.json) has **zero content and cycle error**, with **100% target-domain accuracy** on these three constructed points.
- Apply the identity map instead. It also has zero cycle error, but **0% target-domain accuracy**. Cycle consistency alone cannot tell whether a translation happened.
- Supply styles −0.4 and 0.4 to the same target domain. The style coordinate changes by **0.8**. A map that ignores the supplied style has separation zero. These checks distinguish content preservation, domain control and style diversity. The transformation is analytic; no StarGAN networks or perceptual-quality metric are trained or evaluated.
