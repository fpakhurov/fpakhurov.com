An autoencoder learns to reconstruct an input through an intermediate representation. The seminar's central point is that reconstruction is a training objective, while the learned representation is also an important result.

## Encoder, bottleneck and decoder

The basic path is

$$
x \xrightarrow{\text{encoder}} z
\xrightarrow{\text{decoder}} \hat{x},
\qquad
z=f_\phi(x),\quad \hat{x}=g_\theta(z).
$$

The encoder maps the input to a latent representation $z$. A bottleneck constrains the information available to the decoder, which maps that representation back to a reconstruction $\hat{x}$. In the image architecture discussed in the notes, the encoder downsamples and the decoder upsamples.

The notes give a latent shape of $32\times4\times4$: this contains $512$ scalar entries. Flattening the tensor would produce a vector of length $512$.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial clarification.</strong> Under the usual convention for convolutional features, this shape means $32$ channels on a $4\times4$ spatial grid.</aside>

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial clarification.</strong> A bottleneck can encourage useful structure, but reconstruction alone does not guarantee an interpretable or well-organized latent space. The constraints on the representation matter.</aside>

## Reconstruction with mean squared error

The reconstruction loss compares the original input with the decoder's output. The notes use mean squared error (MSE):

$$
\operatorname{MSE}(x,\hat{x})
=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\hat{x}_i)^2,
$$

where $n$ is the number of scalar input values. For an image, the sum can run over its pixels and channels. Minimizing this loss trains the encoder and decoder together to reduce reconstruction error.

The notes also mention “emergent structure”: representations can capture regularities in the data as the network learns to reconstruct it. This is a motivation for studying the latent space, rather than evaluating only the reconstructed image.

## Learned feature maps and resizing

The seminar distinguishes a learned encoder from a direct image resize. Its layers learn filters and produce feature maps; a smaller spatial grid can therefore encode features rather than simply store a smaller copy of the input pixels.

The [neural training background note](/notes/generative-models/neural-training-background#a-convolution-learns-kernels-across-channels) shows how kernels combine input channels and reuse their weights across the image.

Likewise, the decoder is described as upsampling combined with convolution. Increasing spatial dimensions makes room for a larger output, while learned convolutions transform features into a reconstruction.

The notes mention resizing a $28\times28$ input to $64\times64$ before encoding, giving the model more spatial “room” for successive downsampling steps.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial clarification.</strong> Resizing to a larger grid does not add information to the original image. It can make an input fit a chosen architecture. The handwritten scan leaves the layer details unspecified; the related notebook uses stride-2 convolutions in the encoder and bilinear upsampling followed by convolution in the simple AE decoder.</aside>

## Denoising autoencoders

A denoising autoencoder (DAE) receives a corrupted input and learns to reconstruct the clean original:

$$
\tilde{x}=\operatorname{corrupt}(x),
\qquad
\hat{x}=g_\theta(f_\phi(\tilde{x})).
$$

The target remains $x$, so the model must learn useful structure that survives the corruption. The notes summarize this as “add noise; reconstruct; increase robustness.”

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial clarification.</strong> The later page calls this “more stable.” Here that is interpreted as the motivation to improve robustness to input corruption, rather than a guarantee about optimization or every kind of noise. The scan does not name a corruption distribution; the related notebook adds Gaussian noise and retains the clean reconstruction target.</aside>

## Sparse autoencoders

A sparse autoencoder adds a sparsity objective to reconstruction. The notes describe the intended effect as rare activations: units are encouraged to remain inactive much of the time.

This places another constraint on the representation and encourages the model to express an input through selected features. The scan does not provide a particular penalty; the related notebook explores KL regularization of intermediate activations and an L1 penalty on the bottleneck.

The Q&A also asks about stride and upsampling in a sparse AE. These serve the architecture's spatial transformations: stride can reduce feature-grid resolution, and upsampling can increase it. Sparsity instead constrains the activation values; it does not replace those encoder and decoder operations.

The presenter cautions that the sparse-AE notebook is something to experiment with, rather than evidence that its particular penalty produces a useful sparse representation. The distinction between a chosen objective and its observed effect matters when inspecting the notebook outputs.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial clarification.</strong> Sparse activations refer to the values produced by units. They do not, by themselves, imply sparse model weights.</aside>

## Activation functions

The first page introduces two common nonlinearities.

The rectified linear unit (ReLU) passes positive values through and sets negative values to zero:

$$
\operatorname{ReLU}(a)=\max(0,a).
$$

The sigmoid maps a real value into $(0,1)$:

$$
\sigma(a)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-a}},
\qquad \sigma(0)=\frac12.
$$

The scan sketches these shapes without assigning them to particular layers. In the related notebook's simple AE, the hidden convolutional blocks use LeakyReLU, and the output uses sigmoid.

## Seminar notebook

The [Autoencoders notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/42dd64b535f5bedf1d2b3db28f2f21ce96c9f09f/seminars/00_Autoencoders/autoencoders.ipynb) supplies practical context for these remarks. Cell numbers below count all cells from 1 in this linked snapshot; it is a reference version, whose exact correspondence to the version shown during the seminar is unconfirmed.

- **Architecture and latent structure:** cells 5 and 11 resize MNIST to $64\times64$ and encode it as $32\times4\times4$ features. Cells 9, 15 and 20 project flattened features with t-SNE before and after training; cells 23-24 interpolate between two encoded digits. These are concrete ways to inspect the “emergent structure” mentioned in the notes.
- **Denoising:** cells 27 and 31 form $\tilde{x}=x+0.2\epsilon$ with standard-normal $\epsilon$, then minimize MSE against clean $x$. Cell 35 places original, noisy and reconstructed images side by side. The model also uses dropout in its convolutional blocks.
- **Sparsity and sampling:** cells 39-43 regularize sigmoid intermediate activations toward a mean of $0.05$ with coefficient $0.001$; cells 51-53 instead add $0.001\,\operatorname{mean}(|z|)$. Cell 25 samples each latent entry from a Gaussian fitted separately for each digit label, then decodes it. This exercise uses a fitted latent sampling rule; reconstruction training alone does not impose a standard-normal prior.
