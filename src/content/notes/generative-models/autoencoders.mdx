With the blocks of the [previous note](/notes/generative-models/training-background), convolutions, batch norm and LeakyReLU, we can build the first neural model of the course. It does not generate anything yet. An autoencoder is trained to reproduce its own input; training a network by back-propagation to pass its input through a narrow hidden layer and back already appears as the "encoding problem" of [Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (1986)](#references). The reconstruction is only the pretext. What we actually want is the **representation**: the compact code the network is forced to build on the way ([Hinton and Salakhutdinov, 2006](#references); [Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016](#references), chapter 14). This note trains a plain, a denoising and two sparse (KL and L1) convolutional autoencoders on MNIST, looks at what their codes capture, and ends with the gap that the [variational autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/vae) will close.

**Notation.** $x$ is an input image and $P$ the data distribution it comes from. The encoder $f_\varphi$, with parameters $\varphi$, maps $x$ to the latent code $z = f_\varphi(x)$. The decoder $g_\theta$, with parameters $\theta$, maps the code back to the reconstruction $\hat x = g_\theta(z)$. As in the [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae), $\varphi$ belongs to the encoder and $\theta$ to the decoder. $\beta$ and $\lambda$ are the weights of the sparsity penalties below; they are unrelated to the noise schedule $\beta_t$ of the diffusion notes.

## Architecture: downsample, bottleneck, upsample

An **encoder** $f_\varphi$ maps an input $x$ to a latent code $z$, and a **decoder** $g_\theta$ maps the code back to input space:

$$
z = f_\varphi(x), \qquad \hat{x} = g_\theta(z) = g_\theta\big(f_\varphi(x)\big).
$$

The data path has a simple shape, from the input through the bottleneck $z$ to the reconstruction:

$$
x \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{downsample}\ }\; z \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{upsample}\ }\; \hat{x}.
$$

The bottleneck is smaller than the input, so the network cannot pass the pixels through unchanged. It has to keep what it needs to rebuild the input and drop the rest. This is the **latent space** idea: every input becomes a point in a lower-dimensional space that is learned from the data.

**Input resolution: $28 \times 28 \to 64 \times 64$.** The MNIST digits are resized to $64 \times 64$ (`transforms.Resize((64, 64))`, then `ToTensor()`, which already gives pixel values in $[0, 1]$). The larger image lets the model downsample step by step. The encoder halves the spatial size four times: a stride-2 convolution with kernel 3 and padding 1 maps $H$ to $\lfloor (H-1)/2 \rfloor + 1$ ([output-size formula](/notes/generative-models/training-background#a-convolution-layer)). The decoder doubles the size four times:

| Side | Encoder, 4 halvings | Decoder, 4 doublings | Restores input? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $64$ | $64 \to 32 \to 16 \to 8 \to 4$ | $4 \to 8 \to 16 \to 32 \to 64$ | yes |
| $28$ | $28 \to 14 \to 7 \to 4 \to 2$ | $2 \to 4 \to 8 \to 16 \to 32$ | no ($32 \ne 28$) |

With a side divisible by $2^4 = 16$, such as $64$, every stage halves exactly and the mirrored decoder returns to the input size. At $28 \times 28$ the same four stages would leave a $2 \times 2$ map and a $32 \times 32$ output.

<AEPipeline />

**The model.** Every encoder stage is a block of a $3\times 3$ convolution with stride 2, batch normalization and LeakyReLU with slope $0.2$ (all explained in the [previous note](/notes/generative-models/training-background)). A last $3 \times 3$ convolution with stride 1 produces the code. Each decoder stage upsamples by a factor of 2 with bilinear interpolation and then applies a $3\times 3$ convolution, batch norm and LeakyReLU. A final convolution maps 16 channels to 1, followed by a sigmoid.

| Tensor | Shape (channels × height × width) | Numbers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| input $x$ | $1 \times 64 \times 64$ | $4096$ |
| after encoder block 1 | $16 \times 32 \times 32$ | $16384$ |
| after encoder block 2 | $32 \times 16 \times 16$ | $8192$ |
| after encoder block 3 | $32 \times 8 \times 8$ | $2048$ |
| after encoder block 4 and the last conv: code $z$ | $32 \times 4 \times 4$ | $512$ |
| after decoder block 1 | $32 \times 8 \times 8$ | $2048$ |
| after decoder block 2 | $32 \times 16 \times 16$ | $8192$ |
| after decoder block 3 | $32 \times 32 \times 32$ | $32768$ |
| after decoder block 4 | $16 \times 64 \times 64$ | $65536$ |
| output $\hat x$ | $1 \times 64 \times 64$ | $4096$ |

**Latent size.** The bottleneck has shape $32 \times 4 \times 4$, so it holds $512$ numbers: the latent code is 512-dimensional when flattened. That is $4096 / 512 = 8$ times fewer numbers than the resized input, but only about $1.5$ times fewer than the original $28 \times 28 = 784$ pixels. The compression is mild; the point is the structure of the code, not its size.

## How to read the encoder and decoder

- **The encoder works as a learned filter, not a fixed compression.** Every downsampling step is a learned $3\times3$ convolution with stride 2. The filters extract features while the stride reduces the resolution, so the network itself decides what survives each halving, which a fixed resize could not do.
- **The decoder is not an upscaler.** It works as *upsample + conv*. The bilinear upsampling is a fixed operation with no parameters; the learned convolution after it fills in the detail.
- **Compression comes from the architecture, not from an explicit squeeze.** No layer is told to compress. The first encoder block even expands $4096$ input values to $16384$ activations. The code becomes small only because the shapes narrow towards the bottleneck.
- **Layers learn features, not pixels.** Early layers respond to local patterns such as edges and stroke fragments. Deeper layers see a larger part of the image through their receptive field and combine these into larger parts of a digit, so the channels encode features rather than individual pixel values.

## Reconstruction loss

The loss compares the output with the input. The standard choice is the mean squared error over all $n$ scalar components,

$$
\mathrm{MSE}(\hat y, y) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - \hat{y}_i\right)^2,
$$

averaged over the data:

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{recon}}(\varphi, \theta) = \mathbb E_{x \sim P}\Big[\mathrm{MSE}\big(g_\theta(f_\varphi(x)),\ x\big)\Big].
$$

For an autoencoder $y = x$ and $\hat y = \hat x$. PyTorch's `nn.MSELoss()` averages over every pixel of the batch: $n = 64 \cdot 64 \cdot 64 = 262144$ for a batch of 64 images.

**MSE or BCE.** Pixel values are in $[0, 1]$, so binary cross-entropy is a natural alternative:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{BCE}(\hat x, x) &= -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[x_i \log \hat x_i \\
&\qquad\quad + (1 - x_i)\log(1 - \hat x_i)\Big],
\end{aligned}
$$

averaged over pixels as `nn.BCELoss` does by default. It treats each pixel as the probability of being "on", which suits nearly binary MNIST digits.

The two losses correspond to two decoder likelihoods. A Gaussian decoder with fixed variance $s^2$ on $D$ pixels has $-\log \mathcal N(x;\, \hat x,\, s^2 I) = \frac{1}{2s^2}\|x - \hat x\|^2 + \frac D2 \log(2\pi s^2)$, so MSE equals its negative log-likelihood up to a positive factor and an additive constant. BCE is the negative log-likelihood of a decoder that makes each pixel an independent Bernoulli variable with mean $\hat x_i$. Strictly, that holds only for binary pixels. The resized MNIST images have many grey values; for those, BCE is still minimized at $\hat x_i = x_i$, but it is no longer the negative log of a normalized density over $[0, 1]$ ([Loaiza-Ganem and Cunningham, 2019](#references)). The VAE note builds on this [reading of the loss as a likelihood](/notes/generative-models/vae#variational-approximation-and-the-elbo).

With a sigmoid already applied in `forward`, BCE must be `nn.BCELoss`; `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` expects raw logits and applies the sigmoid itself, so combining it with a sigmoid output would apply the sigmoid twice. The numerically more stable option is to drop the sigmoid and use `nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss` on the logits.

The input range must suit both the output activation and the loss. MNIST is already in $[0, 1]$ after `ToTensor()`, so no extra normalization is needed here; with other data it usually is.

## Emergent structure

Nothing in the loss asks for an organized latent space, and no labels are used in training. Still, structure emerges. Projecting the 512-dimensional codes of the test set to 2-D with t-SNE ([van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008](#references)) and colouring the points by digit shows clusters by digit that are tighter and better separated than before training. Most digits form one compact cluster; the 2s split into two groups and the 1s form an elongated band. The codes are organized by content, not by individual pixel values.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Read the before/after plots with care.</strong> The t-SNE of the <em>randomly initialized</em> encoder already shows clear clusters for most digits; the main overlap is between 4 and 9. Random convolutional architectures are known to give surprisingly useful features (<a href="#references">Saxe et al., 2011</a>, for networks of convolution and pooling). This encoder uses strided convolutions instead of pooling, so that result is an analogy here, not a direct explanation. Training makes the clusters tighter and better separated, but it does not create them from nothing.</aside>

Two experiments probe the latent space directly:

- **Interpolation.** Encode a "7" and a "2" and decode $z_\alpha = (1 - \alpha) z_1 + \alpha z_2$ for 8 frames, $\alpha = 0, \tfrac17, \dots, 1$. The endpoints are clean digits, but the middle frames look like overlays of the two strokes rather than digits that morph into each other.
- **Sampling.** Fit an axis-aligned (diagonal) Gaussian per digit class to the codes of the training images, using the per-coordinate mean and standard deviation of $z$, so that the 512 coordinates are sampled independently. Draw one code per class and decode it. In the stored run six of the ten samples are recognisable digits, while the 2, 4, 5 and 8 are badly distorted. This is a single draw per class.

Both experiments show why a plain autoencoder is a representation learner but **not yet a generative model**. Nothing in training makes an arbitrary point of the latent space decode to a plausible image, and the codes do not follow any distribution we know how to sample from: a diagonal Gaussian ignores the correlations between coordinates, the class-wise sampling needed labels and per-class statistics, and the regions between clusters were never trained to decode to anything. [Variational autoencoders](/notes/generative-models/vae#from-autoencoders-to-generation) close exactly this gap by making the code distribution part of the training objective.

## Variants

### Denoising autoencoder

A denoising autoencoder ([Vincent et al., 2008](#references)) changes the task rather than the architecture:

1. **Corrupt** the input with a random corruption $q(\tilde x \mid x)$. The original paper sets a random fraction of the input components to zero; additive Gaussian noise is another common choice ([Vincent et al., 2010](#references)).
2. **Reconstruct the clean input** from the corrupted one, $\hat x = g_\theta(f_\varphi(\tilde x))$, and compute the loss against $x$, not $\tilde x$.
3. The result is a **robust** representation: its features stay stable when part of the input is corrupted.

The network cannot succeed by learning the identity map, so it has to learn which structure in the data is signal and which is noise. Later this idea becomes central. With Gaussian corruption, training a denoiser is equivalent to score matching on a smoothed version of the data density ([Vincent, 2011](#references)), and a diffusion model's [noise-prediction objective](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective#from-means-to-noise) trains a denoiser at many noise levels.

<DenoisingPipeline />

Here the corruption is additive Gaussian noise,

$$
\tilde x = x + 0.2\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I),
$$

without clipping, so corrupted pixels can leave $[0, 1]$. Every block of the encoder and decoder also applies `Dropout2d(0.3)`, which drops whole feature maps at random during training: a second, internal kind of corruption. The loss is the same MSE against the clean image. After 5 epochs the denoising model's training loss is $0.0102$, compared with $0.0021$ for the plain autoencoder. The numbers are not directly comparable: the denoising model solves a harder task, with noisy input and dropout active.

### Sparse autoencoder

A sparse autoencoder keeps the reconstruction loss and adds a **sparsity penalty** that encourages **rare activations**: each hidden unit should be active for only a small fraction of the inputs ([Ng, 2011](#references)). Together with the reconstruction loss, this is meant to make each input use only a few strongly active units. The hidden layer can then be large, even larger than the input, without the network simply copying its input through.

Two variants are common.

**KL penalty on intermediate layers.** Hidden units use sigmoid activations after batch norm, so every activation lies in $(0, 1)$ and can be read as a firing rate. For each unit $j$, let $\hat\rho_j$ be its mean activation over the batch; [Ng (2011)](#references) averages over the whole training set, and the batch mean is an estimate of that average. The penalty compares $\hat\rho_j$ with a target rate $\rho$ through the KL divergence between two Bernoulli distributions:

$$
\mathcal L = \mathrm{MSE}(\hat x, x) + \beta \sum_{j} D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho \,\|\, \hat\rho_j),
$$

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho \,\|\, \hat\rho_j) = \rho \log\frac{\rho}{\hat\rho_j} + (1-\rho)\log\frac{1-\rho}{1-\hat\rho_j},
$$

with $\rho = 0.05$ and $\beta = 0.001$ here.

<SparseActivations />

This is the [KL divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#kullbackleibler-divergence) between a "fires with probability $\rho$" coin and a "fires with probability $\hat\rho_j$" coin. It is zero only when the average firing rate equals $\rho$. The penalty constrains only the average rate of each unit: a unit firing weakly at $0.05$ for every input satisfies it too. Sparsity for each input has to come from the interplay with the reconstruction loss. The sum runs over every channel and position of the four intermediate encoder layers: $16\cdot 32^2 + 32 \cdot 16^2 + 32 \cdot 8^2 + 32 \cdot 4^2 = 27136$ terms. This model's decoder also differs from the others: it uses transposed convolutions (kernel 4, stride 2, padding 1) instead of upsample + conv. With dilation 1 and no output padding, the [`ConvTranspose2d` output-size formula](https://pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.ConvTranspose2d.html) gives a doubled side:

$$
H_{\text{out}} = (H - 1)\cdot 2 - 2\cdot 1 + 4 = 2H.
$$

**L1 penalty on the code.** This is the plain architecture plus a penalty with weight $\lambda = 0.001$ on the mean absolute value of the 512 code entries, averaged over the batch as well:

$$
\mathcal L = \mathrm{MSE}(\hat x, x) + \lambda \cdot \frac{1}{512}\sum_{k=1}^{512} |z_k|.
$$

The (sub)gradient of $|z_k|$ is $\operatorname{sign}(z_k)$, of constant size, so the L1 penalty keeps pulling small entries towards zero, whereas the pull of a squared penalty vanishes near zero. The minimizer of an L1-penalized objective therefore typically has some entries exactly at zero: it soft-thresholds them ([Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, 2016](#references), section 7.1.2). Plain gradient training with Adam, as here, only drives such entries close to zero, where they keep oscillating.

**Neither variant works well here, and the reasons are instructive.** If the hidden sigmoids start with batch means near $0.5$, as batch norm centres their inputs at initialization, the KL term starts at roughly $27136 \cdot 0.001 \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(0.05\,\|\,0.5) \approx 13$. This is an order-of-magnitude estimate, since the per-unit means vary; because $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho\,\|\,\cdot)$ is convex, a spread of the $\hat\rho_j$ around an average of $0.5$ only increases it (Jensen's inequality).

The notebook logs only the sum of the two terms. It falls from $8.85$ in the first epoch to $0.214$ in the fifth, and it is still falling steeply ($0.788$ in the fourth epoch), so training has not converged. An all-black output would score an MSE equal to the mean squared pixel value, about $0.10$ for the resized MNIST images (computed from the MNIST test set with the same bilinear resize, not taken from the notebook). The stored reconstructions of the KL model are worse than the plain model's but far from blank. So more than half of the fifth-epoch average, at least $0.214 - 0.10 \approx 0.11$, is penalty: about 50 times the plain autoencoder's whole reconstruction error of $0.0021$. With 27136 terms, $\beta = 0.001$ is too large a weight. The KL model's reconstructions are visibly worse and its t-SNE plot is fragmented.

The L1 variant has the opposite problem. With $\lambda = 0.001$ the penalty does not visibly change training: the loss ends at $0.0022$, essentially the plain model's $0.0021$, and with one run each the gap is within noise. The penalty can also be bypassed. The first decoder block applies bilinear upsampling and a bias-free convolution, both linear, and then batch norm. Replacing $z$ by $r z$ for any $r > 0$ scales the convolution's outputs by $r$, and batch norm divides this factor out again, as long as $r^2\sigma_B^2$ stays well above its $\varepsilon$ ([batch norm ignores the scale of its input](/notes/generative-models/training-background#the-rest-of-the-layer)). The encoder could therefore lower the penalty by shrinking the overall scale of $z$, without making the code sparse. The notebook does not measure the scale of $z$, so this is a possible bypass, not an observed one; printing `z.abs().mean()` before and after training would show it. Sparsity penalties need their weight tuned against the reconstruction loss, and they need an architecture in which they cannot be bypassed.

### Transposed convolution or upsample + conv?

Both double the spatial size. A transposed convolution learns its own upsampling filter, but when the kernel size is not divisible by the stride, the kernel footprints overlap unevenly and produce checkerboard artifacts. Resizing followed by a convolution avoids the uneven overlap and is the usual safer default ([Odena, Dumoulin and Olah, 2016](#references)). The same study adds two caveats. Its best results came with nearest-neighbour resizing, and the authors had difficulty making bilinear resizing work, which is what the plain model here uses. A kernel divisible by the stride, such as kernel 4 with stride 2 in the KL model, removes the uneven overlap, but the learned kernels can still produce artifacts. The two decoders here are not compared under equal conditions, so the choice is a design option rather than a measured result.

## Why a sigmoid at the output?

The convolutional blocks use **LeakyReLU** with slope $0.2$, $\max(0.2x,\ x)$ ([Maas, Hannun and Ng, 2013](#references)). Why such non-saturating activations replace sigmoids inside deep networks is explained in the [previous note](/notes/generative-models/training-background#common-remedies). The sigmoid has a different job here. It squashes any real number into $(0, 1)$, the range of the pixel intensities, so it maps the unbounded output of the decoder's last convolution onto the scale of the targets. It passes through $0.5$ at $x = 0$:

$$
\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}, \qquad \sigma(0) = \tfrac12 .
$$

<Activations />

The sigmoid is the output activation of every model here. In the KL-sparse model it is also the hidden activation, because the Bernoulli penalty needs activations that can be read as firing rates in $(0, 1)$.

## Next

A plain autoencoder learns useful codes, but we cannot sample new images from it: nothing tells us where in latent space the codes lie or what lies between them, and the class-wise Gaussians above needed labels and still gave distorted digits. The [next note](/notes/generative-models/vae) makes the distribution of the codes part of the model and trains it with a lower bound on the log-likelihood, which turns the autoencoder into a generator.
