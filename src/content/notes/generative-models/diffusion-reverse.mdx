The [forward process](/notes/generative-models/diffusion) turns data into noise using a known schedule. Generation starts at the noisy end and learns how to move back toward the data. The useful training target is a reverse step whose distribution we can calculate **when the original example is known**.

We keep $x_0$ for data, $x_1,\ldots,x_T$ for increasingly noisy states, $\alpha_t=1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^t\alpha_s$. The empty product is $\bar\alpha_0=1$.

## Generation runs from noise to data

Start with a simple prior and compose learned transitions:

$$
p(x_T)=\mathcal N(0,I),
\qquad
p_\theta(x_{0:T})=p(x_T)\prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t).
$$

At an interior step, a common DDPM choice is

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)
=\mathcal N\!\left(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\sigma_t^2 I\right),
\qquad t\ge2.
$$

The network receives the current noisy state and its timestep. It predicts a mean; the variance can be a fixed positive value chosen for that timestep. More general models can learn the covariance, but that is not needed for the derivation below. The last transition $p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)$ is an observation decoder and will receive its own loss term.

Sampling moves through $T,T-1,\ldots,1$. The model is not given the original image during generation: producing a plausible $x_0$ is its task.

## A training posterior knows the original example

During training, we do know $x_0$. Combining the forward transition with the previous forward marginal gives, for $t\ge2$,

$$
q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)
\propto q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\,q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0).
$$

Both factors are Gaussian functions of $x_{t-1}$, so completing the square gives

$$
q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)
=\mathcal N\!\left(\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0),\tilde\beta_t I\right),
$$

with

$$
\tilde\beta_t
=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t,
$$

$$
\tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0)
=\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0
+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t.
$$

The posterior mean is a schedule-dependent combination of the clean example and its noisy version. Its coefficients need not sum to one; this is a Gaussian-conditioning formula, rather than a simple interpolation rule.

This answers the handwritten question, “Why need NN?” A closed-form target requiring $x_0$ helps during training, but cannot be used directly when generating an unknown $x_0$. The network learns a transition conditioned on $x_t$ and $t$ instead.

## The reverse conditional is not always Gaussian

The posterior above includes a crucial extra condition: $x_0$. If we remove it, the exact reverse conditional is

$$
q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)
=\int q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,q(x_0\mid x_t)\,dx_0.
$$

This averages over possible clean examples. Even though every fixed-$x_0$ posterior is Gaussian, their mixture need not be Gaussian. For example, separated plausible clean examples can produce distinct modes in a reverse step.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial correction.</strong> Page 43 broadly describes the reverse of a Gaussian Markov chain as Gaussian. Gaussian forward transitions do not make the whole chain jointly Gaussian when its initial data distribution is non-Gaussian. The posterior conditioned on both $x_t$ and $x_0$ is exactly Gaussian here. A Gaussian model conditioned only on $x_t$ is a modeling choice, supported by sufficiently small noising steps, rather than an exact statement for every data distribution and finite step size.</aside>

## The likelihood sums over latent trajectories

The forward chain is fixed:

$$
q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)=\prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}).
$$

The likelihood of an observed example marginalizes the model's unobserved trajectory:

$$
p_\theta(x_0)=\int p_\theta(x_{0:T})\,dx_{1:T}.
$$

Many trajectories can lead to the same endpoint. Computing this integral directly is generally impractical for a learned reverse chain. The forward distribution supplies trajectories that we can sample and use to form a training bound.

For a fixed $x_0$, rewrite the integral as an expectation:

$$
p_\theta(x_0)
=\mathbb E_{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}
\left[\frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\right].
$$

## Jensen gives an upper bound on negative log likelihood

Since $\log$ is concave,

$$
\log\mathbb E_q[R]\ge\mathbb E_q[\log R],
\qquad R=\frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}.
$$

Multiplying by $-1$ reverses the inequality:

$$
-\log p_\theta(x_0)
\le
\mathbb E_q\!\left[
\log\frac{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}
\right]
\equiv L(x_0).
$$

The **ELBO** is $-L(x_0)$, a lower bound on the log likelihood. $L(x_0)$ is the corresponding upper bound on negative log likelihood. Keeping the sign explicit prevents the two statements from being confused.

Its gap is itself a KL divergence:

$$
L(x_0)
=-\log p_\theta(x_0)
+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(
q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)
\right).
$$

The fixed forward chain plays the role of an approximate posterior over the model's latent trajectory. Minimizing the bound gives a tractable objective, while equality would additionally require those trajectory posteriors to agree.

## The bound contains three different kinds of terms

Conditioned on $x_0$, factor the forward trajectory in reverse order:

$$
q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)
=q(x_T\mid x_0)\prod_{t=2}^Tq(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0).
$$

Substituting this factorization and the model's reverse joint into $L(x_0)$ yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(x_0)
={}&D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\|\,p(x_T)\right)\\
&+\sum_{t=2}^T\mathbb E_{q(x_t\mid x_0)}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(
q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)
\right)\\
&-\mathbb E_{q(x_1\mid x_0)}\log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1).
\end{aligned}
$$

The **terminal KL** compares the final noisy marginal with the sampling prior. For a fixed schedule and fixed prior it does not depend on $\theta$. It need not be exactly zero at a finite $T$, even when the final state is close to standard Gaussian noise.

The **interior KL terms** compare a tractable training posterior with a learned denoising transition. For each sampled $x_t$, the target uses the known $x_0$ and the model uses $x_t$ alone, together with $t$.

The **reconstruction term** asks the final decoder to assign likelihood to the observed $x_0$ from $x_1$. It is not an interior Gaussian KL. At $t=1$, the posterior conditioned on $x_0$ is the point mass at $x_0$: the formula gives $\tilde\beta_1=0$. Treating that point mass as an ordinary nondegenerate Gaussian would introduce invalid variance ratios or logarithms of zero.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial correction.</strong> Page 42 focuses on the interior sum after simplifying the bound. The terminal term can be omitted from gradients when it is fixed, but the reconstruction term cannot be declared zero merely because $x_1$ has little noise. It depends on the chosen decoder and its prediction. For discrete image likelihoods, the original DDPM paper uses Gaussian probability mass integrated over pixel bins at this boundary.</aside>

The complete training objective averages $L(x_0)$ over the data distribution. Sampling timesteps allows stochastic estimates of the interior sum without evaluating every reverse step for every training example. The algebraic decomposition and closed-form posterior are given in the [DDPM paper, equations 5–7 and appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2006.11239v2#S2).

## Fixed variance turns the learned part into mean matching

Let the interior target be $\mathcal N(\tilde\mu_t,\tilde\beta_t I)$ and the model be $\mathcal N(\mu_\theta,\sigma_t^2 I)$ in $d$ dimensions. For positive variances,

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p_\theta)
={}&\frac{\|\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta(x_t,t)\|_2^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\\
&+\frac d2\left(
\frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2}-1
+\log\frac{\sigma_t^2}{\tilde\beta_t}
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

If the schedule and model variance are fixed, the second line is independent of $\theta$. Thus the learned part of each interior term is a weighted squared error between the posterior mean and the network's mean. When $\sigma_t^2=\tilde\beta_t$, the second line is exactly zero. Fixed unequal variances leave a constant term but the same mean-matching objective.

This reconstructs page 43's drawing of two Gaussians and an arrow bringing their means together. It does not require predicting a covariance, and it does not make the missing clean example an input to the generation network.

The next note, [noise prediction](/notes/generative-models/diffusion-noise-prediction), rewrites this mean target in terms of the Gaussian noise that created $x_t$. That parameterization leads to the familiar denoising loss and reverse sampling update.
