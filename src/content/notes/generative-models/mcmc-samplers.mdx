Take a network that maps an image $x \in \mathbb R^d$ to one number $U(x)$, and declare the density of images to be $\pi(x) = e^{-U(x)}/Z$. The [energy-based models](/notes/generative-models/energy-based-models) of the next note define densities this way, with no architectural restriction of the kind [flows](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures) need. The normalizer $Z = \int e^{-U(x)}\,dx$ is an integral over all images, and nobody can compute it. Two other quantities are cheap:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)} &= e^{U(x) - U(y)}, \\
\nabla \log \pi(x) &= -\nabla U(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

In the ratio $Z$ cancels, and the gradient with respect to $x$ never sees it, because $\log Z$ does not depend on $x$. $U$ is called the **potential energy** and $\nabla \log \pi$ the **score**. How do we draw samples of $\pi$ with nothing but these two quantities?

## The starting point: rejection and Metropolis–Hastings \{#the-starting-point\}

Two samplers of the probability course already work with ratios. **Rejection sampling** ([Monte Carlo methods](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#rejection-sampling)) keeps a draw from a proposal $q$ with probability $\pi(x)/(M q(x))$, which needs a bound $\pi \le M q$. It is exact, and it dies with dimension. Take the target $\mathcal N(0, I_d)$ and the proposal $\mathcal N(0, 4 I_d)$. The ratio $\pi/q$ is a product of $d$ factors $2e^{-3x_i^2/8}$, each at most 2, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
M &= \sup_x \frac{\pi(x)}{q(x)} = 2^d, \\
P(\text{accept}) &= 2^{-d} .
\end{aligned}
$$

That is one proposal in 1,024 kept at $d = 10$ and one in $10^{30}$ at $d = 100$.

**Metropolis–Hastings** ([Markov chain Monte Carlo](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#metropolis-hastings)) gives up independent draws. From the current state $x$ it proposes $y \sim q(\cdot \mid x)$ and moves there with probability

$$
\alpha(x, y) = \min\Big(1, \frac{\pi(y)\, q(x \mid y)}{\pi(x)\, q(y \mid x)}\Big) ;
$$

otherwise it stays at $x$. With the random-walk proposal $y = x + \sigma z$, where $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ is fresh noise, $q$ is symmetric and cancels.

The random walk knows nothing about the target. Its step $\sigma$ must be about the width of the narrowest direction, or proposals land in low density and are rejected. Independent steps add in variance, so $n$ steps travel about $\sigma\sqrt n$, and crossing the widest direction takes about $(\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min})^2$ steps. For a bivariate normal with unit variances and correlation $0.95$ the axes of the ellipse have standard deviations $\sqrt{1.95} = 1.40$ and $\sqrt{0.05} = 0.22$, so a crossing takes about 39 steps even if every proposal were accepted. Dimension adds a second cost, worked out [later](#how-the-step-must-shrink-with-dimension).

## MALA: let the gradient propose \{#mala\}

### The Langevin equation

Picture a particle that drifts uphill on $\log \pi$ and is jostled by noise. The **Langevin diffusion** writes this as

$$
dX_t = \tfrac12 \nabla \log \pi(X_t)\, dt + dW_t ,
$$

where $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion: its increment over a time $\varepsilon$ is $\mathcal N(0, \varepsilon I)$, independent of the past. The drift alone would climb to a mode and stop; the noise alone would spread without limit. With the factor $\tfrac12$ they balance.

The **Fokker–Planck equation**, derived from one Euler step and a Taylor expansion in the [continuous-time diffusion note](/notes/generative-models/continuous-time-diffusion#how-densities-move-the-fokkerplanck-equation), makes this balance exact. For $dX_t = f(X_t)\,dt + dW_t$ (noise scale $g = 1$ there) the density $p_t$ of $X_t$ obeys the first line below. The second line writes it as minus the divergence of the flux $f p_t - \frac12 \nabla p_t$, drift minus diffusion, and the drift $f = \tfrac12 \nabla \log \pi$ makes that flux zero at $p_t = \pi$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t p_t &= -\nabla \cdot (f\, p_t) + \tfrac12 \Delta p_t \\
&= -\nabla \cdot \big(f\, p_t - \tfrac12 \nabla p_t\big) , \\
f\, \pi &= \tfrac12\, \pi \nabla \log \pi = \tfrac12 \nabla \pi , \\
f\, \pi - \tfrac12 \nabla \pi &= 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\Delta = \nabla \cdot \nabla$ is the Laplacian; [Isachenko (2025](#references), lecture 10) finds the same drift by setting $\partial_t p_t = 0$. Stationarity does not yet say that chains reach $\pi$, and the discretized chain below does not even keep $\pi$ exactly. For a potential $U$ that is $m$-strongly convex with an $M$-Lipschitz gradient, the unadjusted chain forgets its start geometrically and ends within a distance of order $\sqrt{\varepsilon d}$ of $\pi$ ([Dalalyan, 2017](#references), Theorem 1; the bound is below).

<details>
<summary>The bound</summary>

In the notation of the theorem the Euler step below has $h = \varepsilon/2$, and for $h \le 2/(m + M)$ the distribution $\nu_K$ of the state after $K$ steps satisfies

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_2(\nu_K, \pi) \le{}& (1 - m h)^K\, W_2(\nu_0, \pi) \\
&+ 1.82\, \frac{M}{m} \sqrt{h d} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $W_2$ is the Wasserstein-2 distance. The first term is the start being forgotten: two copies of the chain driven by the same noise approach each other by the factor $1 - mh$ per step, because a strongly convex $U$ pulls them together. The second is the price of the frozen drift; it does not shrink with $K$ but vanishes as $h \to 0$.

</details>

### One Euler step: unadjusted Langevin

To simulate the diffusion for a time $\varepsilon$, hold the drift at its value at the start and draw the Brownian increment exactly:

$$
y = x + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \nabla \log \pi(x) + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\, z .
$$

Used as a chain, this is the **unadjusted Langevin algorithm** (ULA) of the [probability course](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#langevin-dynamics), and the frozen drift makes it slightly wrong. On $\pi = \mathcal N(0, 1)$ the score is $-x$, one step is $y = a x + \sqrt\varepsilon\, z$ with $a = 1 - \varepsilon/2$, and a stationary variance $v$ must reproduce itself, since $x$ and $z$ are independent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= a^2 v + \varepsilon \\
v &= \frac{\varepsilon}{1 - a^2} \\
&= \frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon - \varepsilon^2/4} \\
&= \frac{1}{1 - \varepsilon/4} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The variance is $1.14$ at $\varepsilon = 0.5$ and $1.33$ at $\varepsilon = 1$, against the target's 1; for $\varepsilon \ge 4$, where $|a| \ge 1$, the chain diverges.

### The Metropolis correction

The **Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm** (MALA) keeps the Euler step as a proposal and adds the Metropolis–Hastings test ([Neal, 2011](#references), Section 5.2). The proposal density is

$$
q(y \mid x) = \mathcal N\Big(y;\ x + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \nabla \log \pi(x),\ \varepsilon I\Big) .
$$

It is not symmetric: the reverse move from $y$ is centred at $y + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla\log\pi(y)$, with the drift at $y$, so both $q(y \mid x)$ and $q(x \mid y)$ enter $\alpha$. Each step costs one new gradient, at $y$.

Why is the chain exact for every $\varepsilon$? The [probability course](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#metropolis-hastings) proves the general case; here is the core step. A chain leaves $\pi$ invariant if it satisfies [detailed balance](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#detailed-balance), $\pi(x) P(x \to y) = \pi(y) P(y \to x)$. For $y \ne x$ the chain reaches $y$ only by proposing and accepting it, so $P(x \to y) = q(y \mid x)\, \alpha(x, y)$. Moving the positive factor $\pi(x) q(y \mid x)$ inside the minimum gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi(x)\, P(x \to y)
&= \pi(x)\, q(y \mid x)\, \alpha(x, y) \\
&= \min\big(\pi(x)\, q(y \mid x),\ \pi(y)\, q(x \mid y)\big) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

which is symmetric in $x$ and $y$ and therefore equals $\pi(y) P(y \to x)$. Rejections form a point mass at $x$, where detailed balance holds trivially (the full kernel is written out in the probability course and in «Байесовские методы в анализе данных», [2026](#references), lecture 4). So $\pi$ is stationary for any step size; a larger $\varepsilon$ costs rejections, never bias.

### Worked example: MALA on a standard normal

Take $\pi = \mathcal N(0, 1)$, so $\nabla \log \pi(x) = -x$, and write $a = 1 - \varepsilon/2$ for the factor that maps $x$ to the proposal mean $a x$. The log-ratio of the two proposal densities is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log \frac{q(x \mid y)}{q(y \mid x)} &= \frac{(y - a x)^2 - (x - a y)^2}{2\varepsilon} \\
&= \frac{(1 - a^2)(y^2 - x^2)}{2\varepsilon} \\
&= \Big(1 - \frac{\varepsilon}{4}\Big)\frac{y^2 - x^2}{2} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the second line expands both squares (the cross terms $-2axy$ cancel) and the third uses $1 - a^2 = \varepsilon - \varepsilon^2/4$. Adding $\log \pi(y) - \log \pi(x) = (x^2 - y^2)/2$ leaves

$$
\log \frac{\pi(y)\, q(x \mid y)}{\pi(x)\, q(y \mid x)} = \frac{\varepsilon}{8}\,(x^2 - y^2) .
$$

The random walk's log-ratio for the same pair is $(x^2 - y^2)/2$; MALA's is that times $\varepsilon/4$, because the proposal already carries most of the change in density. With $x = 2$ and $\varepsilon = 1$ the proposal is $\mathcal N(1, 1)$. A draw that lands farther out, at $y = 2.8$, has $\pi(y)/\pi(x) = e^{-1.92} = 0.15$, so a random walk would accept it with probability $0.15$. MALA accepts it with $e^{-0.48} = 0.62$: the reverse proposal from $2.8$ has mean $1.4$, only $0.6$ from $x = 2$, so the move is easy to undo.

The example also suggests why ULA's bias is small for small steps: a proposal moves $x$ by order $\sqrt\varepsilon$, so the log-ratio $\frac{\varepsilon}{8}(x - y)(x + y)$ is of order $\varepsilon^{3/2}$, and ULA differs from the exact MALA only in the rare rejections. This is an intuition; the bound is the second term of Dalalyan's inequality above.

## Hamiltonian Monte Carlo: momentum and energy \{#hamiltonian-monte-carlo\}

MALA forgets its direction after every step. **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo** (HMC) adds a velocity that persists. Introduce a **momentum** $p \in \mathbb R^d$ and a **mass matrix** $M$ (the identity unless said otherwise), and sample the pair $(x, p)$ from

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi(x, p) &\propto e^{-H(x, p)} , \\
H(x, p) &= U(x) + K(p) , \\
K(p) &= \tfrac12\, p^\top M^{-1} p .
\end{aligned}
$$

The density factorizes as $e^{-U(x)} e^{-K(p)}$, so $p \sim \mathcal N(0, M)$ independently of $x$, and the marginal of $x$ is $\pi$: sample pairs and drop $p$. Think of $x$ as the position of a particle with potential energy $U$, kinetic energy $K$ and total energy $H$ ([Neal, 2011](#references), Section 3.1). The particle moves by **Hamilton's equations**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dx}{dt} &= \frac{\partial H}{\partial p} = M^{-1} p , \\
\frac{dp}{dt} &= -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} = -\nabla U(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The exact flow has three properties, and HMC rests on all three ([Neal, 2011](#references), Section 2.2). First, it conserves $H$, by the chain rule and then Hamilton's equations, with $\nabla K = M^{-1} p$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dH}{dt} &= \nabla U \cdot \frac{dx}{dt} + \nabla K \cdot \frac{dp}{dt} \\
&= \nabla U \cdot M^{-1} p - M^{-1} p \cdot \nabla U \\
&= 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Second, it preserves volume in the $(x, p)$ space (Liouville's theorem): the velocity field $(M^{-1}p,\ -\nabla U(x))$ has zero divergence, since its $x$-part does not depend on $x$ and its $p$-part does not depend on $p$. Third, it is reversible: run it for a time $t$, negate the momentum, run it again for $t$, and you are back at the start with negated momentum.

If we could follow the exact flow, every proposal would keep its $H$ and be accepted however far it went.

## The leapfrog integrator

Euler's method updates $x$ and $p$ with the derivatives at the current point. On $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, where $H = x^2/2 + p^2/2$ and exact trajectories are circles in the $(x, p)$ plane, one Euler step maps $(x, p)$ to $(x + \varepsilon p,\ p - \varepsilon x)$, and

$$
(x + \varepsilon p)^2 + (p - \varepsilon x)^2 = (1 + \varepsilon^2)(x^2 + p^2) .
$$

Every step multiplies the energy by $1 + \varepsilon^2$, and the trajectory spirals outwards. The **leapfrog** (Störmer–Verlet) method splits the momentum update in two halves around a full position step:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_{1/2} &= p - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \nabla U(x) , \\
x' &= x + \varepsilon\, M^{-1} p_{1/2} , \\
p' &= p_{1/2} - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \nabla U(x') .
\end{aligned}
$$

Consecutive steps share a gradient, so $L$ steps cost $L$ gradients. Leapfrog does not conserve $H$ exactly, but it keeps the other two properties exactly ([Neal, 2011](#references), Section 2.3).

### Why leapfrog keeps volume

Each line is a shear: it changes one block of variables by an amount that depends only on the other block. The first line has the Jacobian

$$
\frac{\partial (x, p_{1/2})}{\partial (x, p)} = \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ -\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \nabla^2 U(x) & I \end{pmatrix} ,
$$

block-triangular with identity blocks on the diagonal, so its determinant is 1; the same holds for the other two lines and for their composition. This is the triangular-Jacobian argument of [coupling layers](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures#a-coupling-layers-nice-realnvp).

### Why leapfrog is reversible

Start from the end point with the momentum negated, $(x', -p')$, and apply the same three lines:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde p_{1/2} &= -p' - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \nabla U(x') = -p_{1/2} , \\
\tilde x &= x' + \varepsilon M^{-1} \tilde p_{1/2} = x , \\
\tilde p &= \tilde p_{1/2} - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2} \nabla U(x) = -p .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line uses the third leapfrog line, $p' = p_{1/2} - \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla U(x')$; the second uses the middle line, $x' - \varepsilon M^{-1} p_{1/2} = x$; the third uses the first line. The step returns to $(x, -p)$.

### The energy error stays bounded

On $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, where $\nabla U(x) = x$, the three leapfrog lines combine into a linear map. With $c = 1 - \varepsilon^2/2$ and $k = 1 - \varepsilon^2/4$ it reads $x' = c x + \varepsilon p$ and $p' = c p - \varepsilon k x$, and substituting it (steps below) shows that it conserves exactly the **shadow energy**

$$
\tilde H(x, p) = \frac{p^2}{2} + \Big(1 - \frac{\varepsilon^2}{4}\Big)\frac{x^2}{2} ,
$$

so along a leapfrog trajectory $H - \tilde H = \varepsilon^2 x^2/8$ oscillates and never accumulates. For $\varepsilon \ge 2$ the coefficient of $x^2$ is no longer positive and trajectories run off to infinity; for a Gaussian with standard deviation $\sigma$ the limit is $\varepsilon < 2\sigma$, set by the narrowest direction. In general the error in $H$ is of order $\varepsilon^3$ after one step and of order $\varepsilon^2$ over a trajectory of fixed length ([Neal, 2011](#references), Section 2.3).

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

Substituting the map into $p'^2 + k x'^2$, the cross terms $\pm 2 c k \varepsilon\, x p$ cancel:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p'^2 + k x'^2 &= (c^2 + k \varepsilon^2)\, p^2 + k\,(k \varepsilon^2 + c^2)\, x^2 \\
&= p^2 + k x^2 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

since $c^2 + k\varepsilon^2 = 1 - \varepsilon^2 + \tfrac{\varepsilon^4}{4} + \varepsilon^2 - \tfrac{\varepsilon^4}{4} = 1$. Halving both sides gives $\tilde H(x', p') = \tilde H(x, p)$.

</details>

A toy step on $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, from $x = 1$, $p = 0.5$ with $\varepsilon = 0.5$:

| Quantity | Leapfrog | Euler |
| --- | --- | --- |
| half-step momentum $p_{1/2}$ | $0.5 - 0.25 \cdot 1 = 0.25$ | — |
| new position $x'$ | $1 + 0.5 \cdot 0.25 = 1.125$ | $1.25$ |
| new momentum $p'$ | $0.25 - 0.25 \cdot 1.125 = -0.03125$ | $0$ |
| energy, from $H = 0.625$ | $0.63330$ | $0.78125$ |
| error $\Delta H$ | $0.0083$ | $0.156$ |

Leapfrog's move would be accepted with probability $e^{-0.0083} = 0.992$; Euler's error, $\varepsilon^2 H$, is nineteen times larger. The shadow energy is $0.59375$ before and after the leapfrog step.

## One HMC iteration

An iteration of HMC from the current state $x$ has three parts ([Neal, 2011](#references), Section 3.2):

1. Refresh the momentum: draw $p \sim \mathcal N(0, M)$, independently of everything else. In the $(x, p)$ plane this is a vertical move, and it is the only part that changes the energy level.
2. Simulate: apply $L$ leapfrog steps of size $\varepsilon$ to $(x, p)$ to get $(x', p')$. The trajectory follows a contour of $H$ up to the small leapfrog error.
3. Accept $x'$ with probability $\min(1, e^{-\Delta H})$, where $\Delta H = H(x', p') - H(x, p)$; otherwise keep $x$. Drop the momentum.

<HmcPhase />

MALA is HMC with a single leapfrog step. From a fresh momentum $p = z$, one leapfrog step of size $h$ moves $x$ to $x + h z - \frac{h^2}{2}\nabla U(x)$: the MALA proposal with $\varepsilon = h^2$. Substituting the leapfrog momenta into the two proposal densities turns them into $e^{-K(p)}$ and $e^{-K(p')}$, so the MALA ratio is exactly $e^{-\Delta H}$ (steps below; [Neal, 2011](#references), Section 5.2). Without the test the chain is ULA, and on $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ the shadow energy explains its bias. Leapfrog keeps volume and $\tilde H$, and the refresh draws $p$ from $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, its conditional under $e^{-\tilde H}$ since $\tilde H$ separates; both steps leave $e^{-\tilde H}$ invariant. Its $x$-marginal has variance $1/(1 - h^2/4) = 1/(1 - \varepsilon/4)$, the ULA variance derived above.

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

The momenta match the proposal densities. With $y = x'$ and $\varepsilon = h^2$, the leapfrog lines give $y - x + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla U(x) = h p$ for the forward move and $x - y + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla U(y) = -h p'$ for the reverse one, so with the same constant

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(y \mid x) &\propto e^{-\lVert h p\rVert^2/(2h^2)} = e^{-K(p)} , \\
q(x \mid y) &\propto e^{-K(p')} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and the MALA ratio $\pi(y)\, q(x \mid y)/\big(\pi(x)\, q(y \mid x)\big)$ equals $e^{-U(y) - K(p') + U(x) + K(p)} = e^{-\Delta H}$ ([Neal, 2011](#references), Section 5.2).

</details>

## Why the acceptance needs only the energy error

Write $z = (x, p)$, let $\Lambda$ be one leapfrog step and $F(x, p) = (x, -p)$ the momentum flip, and define the proposal map $\Psi = F \circ \Lambda^L$: run $L$ steps, then negate the momentum. Two facts about $\Psi$ make the acceptance simple.

- $\Psi$ is its own inverse. Reversibility says $F \circ \Lambda \circ F = \Lambda^{-1}$, so $F \Lambda^L F = \Lambda^{-L}$ and $\Psi \circ \Psi = F \Lambda^L F \Lambda^L = \Lambda^{-L} \Lambda^L$, the identity.
- $\Psi$ preserves volume, $\lvert \det \partial\Psi / \partial z \rvert = 1$, because each leapfrog step and the flip do.

Accept the deterministic proposal $\Psi(z)$ with probability $\alpha(z) = \min\big(1, \pi(\Psi(z))/\pi(z)\big)$. For two regions $A$ and $B$ of phase space, the probability that the chain is in $A$ and moves into $B$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\int_A \pi(z)\, \alpha(z)\, \mathbf 1[\Psi(z) \in B]\, dz \\
&\quad = \int_B \pi(\Psi(w))\, \alpha(\Psi(w))\, \mathbf 1[\Psi(w) \in A]\, dw \\
&\quad = \int_B \pi(w)\, \alpha(w)\, \mathbf 1[\Psi(w) \in A]\, dw ,
\end{aligned}
$$

the probability of the reverse move. The first equality substitutes $z = \Psi(w)$, allowed because $\Psi$ is its own inverse with unit Jacobian; the second uses $\pi(z)\, \alpha(z) = \min\big(\pi(z), \pi(\Psi(z))\big)$, which is unchanged when $z$ and $\Psi(z)$ swap. This is detailed balance, so the accept step leaves $\pi(x, p)$ invariant. Since $K(-p') = K(p')$, the ratio itself is

$$
\frac{\pi(\Psi(z))}{\pi(z)} = e^{-H(x', -p') + H(x, p)} = e^{-\Delta H} ,
$$

and for the same reason the flip is skipped in practice. The refresh draws $p$ exactly from its conditional given $x$, a [Gibbs step](/notes/bayesian-methods/gibbs-sampling#the-gibbs-update), so it also leaves $\pi(x, p)$ invariant, and so does the whole iteration.

A proposal map that stretched space would need the factor $\lvert \det \partial\Psi/\partial z \rvert$ in the ratio, the change-of-variables term that [normalizing flows](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows) pay for in their likelihood. Euler's step on $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, the linear map with rows $(1, \varepsilon)$ and $(-\varepsilon, 1)$, has determinant $1 + \varepsilon^2$, so the factor is $(1 + \varepsilon^2)^L$, and the plain ratio $e^{-\Delta H}$ would sample the wrong distribution.

## The three samplers on a narrow Gaussian

The figure gives a random walk, MALA and HMC 3000 evaluations each of the bivariate normal with correlation $0.95$, counting one gradient as one evaluation. The random walk ($\sigma = 1$) accepts 22%. MALA's drift multiplies the narrow coordinate by $1 - \varepsilon/(2\sigma_{\min}^2)$, which drops below $-1$, so that the drift alone would push the coordinate ever farther out, for $\varepsilon > 4\sigma_{\min}^2 = 0.2$. Its step, $\varepsilon = 0.17$ with factor $-0.7$, sits just below that limit; it accepts 57%. HMC ($\varepsilon = 0.3$, $L = 10$, so 300 iterations) accepts 86%.

## How the step must shrink with dimension

Make the target a product of $d$ independent copies of one coordinate. Write $\Delta = -\log(\text{acceptance ratio})$ for the energy error of a proposal, a sum of $d$ independent terms $\Delta_1$. Its mean must stay of order one, or the acceptance rate collapses, so $\mathbb E[\Delta_1]$ must shrink like $1/d$. How fast it shrinks with the step decides the rest ([Neal, 2011](#references), Sections 4.4 and 5.2):

| Sampler | $\mathbb E[\Delta_1]$ grows as | step that keeps $\mathbb E[\Delta]$ fixed | proposals per independent sample |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| random walk, step $\sigma$ | $\sigma^2$ | $\sigma \propto d^{-1/2}$ | $\sigma^{-2} \propto d$ |
| MALA, variance $\varepsilon$ | $\varepsilon^3$ | $\varepsilon \propto d^{-1/3}$ | $\varepsilon^{-1} \propto d^{1/3}$ |
| HMC, step $\varepsilon$, fixed trajectory length | $\varepsilon^4$ | $\varepsilon \propto d^{-1/4}$ | $\varepsilon^{-1} \propto d^{1/4}$ leapfrog steps |

For the random walk, the Taylor expansion $U(x + c) - U(x) = c\, U'(x) + \tfrac12 c^2 U''(x) + O(c^3)$, averaged over $c = \pm\sigma$, cancels the linear term and leaves $\tfrac12 \sigma^2 U''(x)$, of order $\sigma^2$. For MALA and HMC, $\Delta_1$ is the [leapfrog energy error](#the-energy-error-stays-bounded): order $h^3$ for one step of size $h = \sqrt\varepsilon$ (MALA is one such step), order $\varepsilon^2$ over a fixed-length trajectory. Squared errors give the mean, through an identity that holds for any Metropolis–Hastings proposal made from a state distributed as $\pi$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\big[e^{-\Delta}\big] &= \iint \pi(x)\, q(y \mid x)\, \frac{\pi(y)\, q(x \mid y)}{\pi(x)\, q(y \mid x)}\, dx\, dy \\
&= \iint \pi(y)\, q(x \mid y)\, dx\, dy = 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Expanding $e^{-\Delta} \approx 1 - \Delta + \Delta^2/2$ gives $\mathbb E[\Delta] \approx \mathbb E[\Delta^2]/2$. In the last column the random walk and MALA diffuse, so they need (step length)$^{-2}$ proposals, while one HMC trajectory crosses the target in $1/\varepsilon$ leapfrog steps.

The same identity gives the tuning targets. For large $d$ the central limit theorem makes $\Delta$, a sum of $d$ independent terms, approximately $\mathcal N(\mu, s^2)$. A Gaussian has $\mathbb E[e^{-\Delta}] = e^{-\mu + s^2/2}$, its moment generating function at $-1$, and the identity sets this to 1, so $s^2 = 2\mu$: the variance is twice the mean. The acceptance rate $\mathbb E \min(1, e^{-\Delta})$ splits at $\Delta = 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a(\mu) &= P(\Delta < 0) + \mathbb E\big[e^{-\Delta}\, \mathbf 1[\Delta > 0]\big] \\
&= 2\,\Phi\big(-\sqrt{\mu/2}\big) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function (steps below). By the table the cost per independent sample is $1/(a(\mu)\,\mu^k)$, with $k = 1$, $1/3$ and $1/4$. Minimizing it numerically gives the optimal acceptance rates $0.234$ for the random walk, $0.574$ for MALA and $0.651$ for HMC. Every gradient or density value costs order $d$, so the total work per independent sample grows as $d^2$, $d^{4/3}$ and $d^{5/4}$.

<summary>Derivation</summary>

The first term is $\Phi(-\mu/s) = \Phi(-\sqrt{\mu/2})$. For the second, completing the square shows that $e^{-\Delta}$ times the density of $\mathcal N(\mu, s^2)$ is $e^{-\mu + s^2/2} = 1$ times the density of $\mathcal N(\mu - s^2, s^2) = \mathcal N(-\mu, 2\mu)$, whose probability above zero is again $\Phi(-\sqrt{\mu/2})$.

## Tuning HMC: step size, trajectory length and mass matrix \{#tuning-hmc\}

The step size must stay below twice the standard deviation of the narrowest direction ($0.45$ for the target above), and in practice somewhat below, at an acceptance rate of about $0.65$.

The trajectory length $\varepsilon L$ decides how far a proposal goes. Too short a trajectory makes HMC a random walk of small steps (with $L = 1$ it is MALA); too long a one turns back and wastes gradients; a fixed length can resonate with a period of the target. On a Gaussian direction of variance $\sigma^2$ the leapfrog step is a linear map with determinant 1 and trace $2 - \varepsilon^2/\sigma^2$, a rotation (after rescaling) by $\theta$ with $\cos\theta = 1 - \varepsilon^2/(2\sigma^2)$. In the narrow direction above, $\varepsilon = 0.25$ gives $\theta = 1.19$, and $L = 10$ steps make $1.89$ turns, which bring that coordinate back near its start. Drawing $\varepsilon$ or $L$ at random in a small range at every iteration breaks such resonances ([Neal, 2011](#references), Section 3.2).

Setting the inverse mass matrix $M^{-1}$ to an estimate $\Sigma$ of the covariance of $\pi$ is equivalent to running HMC with identity mass on the whitened variable $\Sigma^{-1/2} x$, in which the target is roughly isotropic and the stability limit no longer depends on the correlations ([Neal, 2011](#references), Section 4.1). In high dimensions a diagonal $M$ estimated during warm-up is the usual compromise.

## NUTS: stop at the U-turn \{#nuts\}

The **No-U-Turn Sampler** ([Hoffman and Gelman, 2014](#references)) removes the choice of $L$: it extends the trajectory until it starts to come back. With identity mass, the squared distance from the start $x$ to the current point $\tilde x$ changes at the rate

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\, \frac{\|\tilde x - x\|^2}{2} = (\tilde x - x) \cdot \tilde p ,
$$

by the chain rule and $d\tilde x/dt = \tilde p$. While this is positive, more simulation moves the proposal farther away.

Stopping at the first negative value would break reversibility, since a trajectory run back from the stopping point would usually stop elsewhere. NUTS instead doubles the trajectory, $1, 2, 4, \dots$ leapfrog steps, each time forwards or backwards at random, until its two ends start to approach each other, and draws the next state from the whole trajectory with weights that keep $\pi(x, p)$ invariant ([Hoffman and Gelman, 2014](#references), Section 3.1). During warm-up, dual averaging tunes $\varepsilon$ towards a target acceptance $\delta$ (Section 3.2). Pyro's `NUTS(potential_fn=...)` runs it on any potential, with $\delta$ = `target_accept_prob`, 0.8 by default ([Pyro documentation](#references)).

## Where it breaks: separated modes

A particle that starts a trajectory with kinetic energy $K$ can climb at most $K$ in potential. Take the mixture $\pi = \tfrac12 \mathcal N(-m, 1) + \tfrac12 \mathcal N(m, 1)$. Between the modes, at $x = 0$, both components contribute $\tfrac12\varphi(m)$, where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density; at a mode the far component is negligible. The barrier is

$$
\begin{aligned}
U(0) - U(m) &\approx \log \frac{\tfrac12 \varphi(0)}{\varphi(m)} \\
&= \frac{m^2}{2} - \log 2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

A chain at a mode crosses only when the fresh momentum supplies that much energy, $p^2/2 > U(0) - U(m)$. A run of 100,000 HMC iterations ($\varepsilon = 0.2$, $L = 25$) started at $x = m$:

| $m$ | barrier | $P(p^2/2 > \text{barrier})$ | crossings of 0 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 1.31 | 0.11 | 21,027 |
| 3 | 3.81 | 0.0058 | 1,903 |
| 4 | 7.31 | $1.3 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | 67 |
| 5 | 11.81 | $1.2 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | 0 |

The barrier grows with $m^2$, and the crossing probability falls roughly like $e^{-\text{barrier}}$. At $m = 5$ the chain never leaves its starting mode while accepting 99.7% of its proposals, and every diagnostic computed from it looks healthy. Only chains started in different places, compared as in the [probability course](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#mixing-and-diagnostics), reveal the second mode; tempering methods, which run chains on flattened versions of the target, cross it at extra cost.

## What you should remember

- MALA: Euler step of $dX = \frac12\nabla\log\pi\,dt + dW$ as the proposal, with $q$ both ways in the test; exact for every $\varepsilon$.
- HMC: momentum $p \sim \mathcal N(0, M)$, $L$ leapfrog steps, accept with $\min(1, e^{-\Delta H})$.
- Leapfrog is a composition of shears (volume 1) and is reversible, so no Jacobian enters the test; Euler is neither.
- Steps scale as $d^{-1/2}$, $d^{-1/3}$, $d^{-1/4}$; target acceptance $0.234$, $0.574$, $0.65$; leapfrog needs $\varepsilon < 2\sigma_{\min}$.
- No sampler here crosses a high energy barrier between modes.
