You have two photographs and want the sequence of images halfway between them. A GAN draws sharp images, but it has no way back from a photograph to its code; even [StyleGAN](/notes/generative-models/stylegan) must solve an optimization problem for each one. A [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) has an encoder, so every image has a code, but its samples are blurry. And nothing ties a GAN to every training image, so it can drop whole modes of the data.

Can one model keep the encoder and the coverage of a VAE and the sharpness of a GAN? The models below keep the VAE's encoder and reconstruction term and hand the troublesome parts of its objective to discriminators.

## What each family is missing

The VAE maximizes the ELBO, a reconstruction term minus a KL term ([VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from)):

$$
\mathcal L(x) = \mathbb E_{q(z \mid x)}\big[\log p(x \mid z)\big] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big) .
$$

With a Gaussian decoder $p(x \mid z) = \mathcal N(G(z), s^2 I)$ the first term is $-\lVert x - G(z)\rVert^2 / (2s^2)$ plus a constant, a squared error. Where several images are plausible for one code, its optimum is their average, since the derivative $2(a - \mathbb E x)$ of $\mathbb E\lVert x - a\rVert^2$ vanishes at $a = \mathbb E x$; and the average is blurry ([VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#what-the-organized-latent-space-buys)). Its benefit is coverage: every training image must be rebuilt from its code, so the model cannot quietly ignore part of the data.

The KL term is worth splitting. Write $P$ for the data distribution and $q(z) = \mathbb E_{x \sim P}\, q(z \mid x)$ for the **aggregate posterior**, the distribution of codes over the whole dataset. Averaged over the data, the KL term splits into two parts, the "ELBO surgery" of [Isachenko (2025)](#references):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb E_{x}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)\big) \\
&\quad = I_q(x; z) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\|\, p(z)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first part, the mutual information between images and codes, charges every image for an informative code, although good reconstruction needs that information. Only the second part, the mismatch between the cloud of codes and the prior, matters for sampling: prior samples decode well only if they land where the codes of real images lie. The [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#what-is-fixed-the-prior-not-the-posterior) derives the split by writing $q(z \mid x)/p(z) = \big(q(z)/p(z)\big) \cdot \big(q(z \mid x)/q(z)\big)$ inside the logarithm and averaging each factor's log over images and codes: the first gives the marginal KL, the second the mutual information.

A GAN avoids both terms. Its discriminator judges whole images, so blur is easy to detect and the samples are sharp. But there is no encoder, and there is no per-image constraint: the generator learns only through the discriminator's verdicts on its own samples, so it can [collapse onto a few modes](/notes/generative-models/gans#mode-collapse-and-how-to-fight-it). The Wasserstein loss of the [previous note](/notes/generative-models/wgan) changes the gradient but adds neither an encoder nor a tie to each training image.

## Adversarial autoencoders: a discriminator on codes

The **adversarial autoencoder** (AAE) of [Makhzani et al. (2015)](#references) is an ordinary autoencoder with a third network attached to its code. The **encoder** $E$ maps an image to a code $\hat z = E(x)$, the **generator** (decoder) $G$ maps it back to the reconstruction $\hat x = G(E(x))$, and the **code discriminator** $C$ tells codes from samples of the prior $p(z) = \mathcal N(0, I)$. For a deterministic encoder, $q(z)$ is the distribution of $E(x)$.

Each minibatch has two phases. In the reconstruction phase, $E$ and $G$ minimize $\mathbb E_{x}\lVert x - G(E(x))\rVert_2^2$. In the regularization phase, $C$ learns to label prior samples real and codes fake,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_C ={}& -\mathbb E_{z \sim p}\log \sigma\big(C(z)\big)\\
&- \mathbb E_{x \sim P}\log\Big(1 - \sigma\big(C(E(x))\big)\Big),
\end{aligned}
$$

and then the encoder, the generator of this small GAN, is updated to fool it, for example with the non-saturating loss $-\mathbb E_{x}\log\sigma\big(C(E(x))\big)$. Here $C$ outputs a real number, its **score**, and $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid, so $\sigma(C(z))$ is the probability that $z$ came from the prior.

### What the code discriminator matches

$C$ sees one code at a time, mixed with the codes of other images, and never learns which image a code came from. It therefore compares the whole cloud of codes with the prior: $q(z)$ against $p(z)$. The [optimal-discriminator argument](/notes/generative-models/gans#step-1-the-optimal-discriminator-for-a-fixed-generator) applied to codes gives $\sigma(C^*(z)) = p(z) / \big(p(z) + q(z)\big)$. Inverting the sigmoid, $C = \log\big(\sigma(C)/(1 - \sigma(C))\big)$, gives the score

$$
C^*(z) = \log \frac{p(z)}{q(z)} .
$$

A classifier's logit estimates a log density ratio; this **density-ratio trick** is the tool behind every model below. Substituting $C^*$ into the minimax loss, as in [step 2 of the GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#step-2-substitute-the-optimal-discriminator-and-recognize-js), leaves $2\,D_{\mathrm{JS}}(p \,\|\, q) - 2\ln 2$, so the encoder minimizes the Jensen–Shannon divergence between $q(z)$ and $p(z)$. Compared with the ELBO, the AAE keeps the marginal mismatch, measured by JS instead of KL, and drops the mutual-information penalty on informative codes. In the InfoVAE family of objectives it is the member that keeps only the marginal term and measures it by JS ([Zhao, Song and Ermon, 2019](#references), Section 4; [Murphy, 2023](#references), Section 21.3.2).

The discriminator needs only samples from the prior, never its density, so any prior that can be sampled will do, such as a mixture of ten 2-D Gaussians. With a 2-D Gaussian prior, the codes of held-out MNIST digits fill the prior without gaps, while a VAE with the same architecture leaves regions where no image is encoded ([Makhzani et al., 2015](#references), Figure 2). Those holes are where prior samples decode poorly.

A cheaper alternative penalizes the batch mean and variance of each code coordinate. [Rosca et al. (2017)](#references) tried it: the means moved toward zero, but the coordinates stayed strongly correlated, which a per-coordinate penalty cannot see. The discriminator, which sees whole code vectors, gave decorrelated codes.

The AAE leaves the decoder's training signal alone: $G$ still learns only from a pixel error, so its outputs carry the same averaging as a VAE decoder.

There is a different way to give a GAN an encoder, with no reconstruction at all. BiGAN and ALI train one discriminator on pairs, telling $(x, E(x))$ with real $x$ from $(G(z), z)$ with prior $z$ ([Murphy, 2023](#references), Section 26.5). For deterministic $E$ and $G$ and an optimal discriminator, the encoder–generator objective is $2\,D_{\mathrm{JS}} - 2\ln 2$ between the two joint distributions, by the same substitution as for the AAE. It is minimal only when they coincide, and then $G(E(x)) = x$ for almost every data point and $E(G(z)) = z$ for almost every code ([Donahue, Krähenbühl and Darrell, 2017](#references), Proposition 2 and Theorems 1–2, proofs in their Section 3 and Appendix A). The intuition: a pair $(x, E(x))$ and a pair $(G(z), z)$ can be indistinguishable only if each map undoes the other. The models below keep the reconstruction term instead, because it is what ties the generator to every training image.

## α-GAN: a discriminator on images as well \{#alpha-gan-a-discriminator-on-images-as-well\}

[Rosca et al. (2017)](#references) start again from the ELBO and replace each term with something a discriminator can estimate. The KL term becomes a code discriminator, as in the AAE: by the density-ratio trick, $-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p) = \mathbb E_q \log\big(p(z)/q(z)\big) \approx \mathbb E_q\, C(z)$. Since $C$ compares the aggregate $q(z)$ with $p(z)$, this is the marginal KL, the part sampling needs.

The likelihood term gets two candidates that fail in opposite ways. One is a Laplace likelihood, $p(x \mid z) = (\lambda/2)^D \exp\big(-\lambda\lVert x - G(z)\rVert_1\big)$ for $D$ pixels, whose negative logarithm is the $L_1$ reconstruction loss $\lambda\lVert x - G(z)\rVert_1$ plus a constant. It covers every training image but blurs: per pixel, the derivative of $\mathbb E\lvert x - a\rvert$ in $a$ is $P(x < a) - P(x > a)$, so its optimum is the median of the plausible values, where the squared error's is their mean. The other is a *synthetic likelihood*: the score $D(\hat x)$ of an **image discriminator** $D$ trained to tell data from decoded images. It gives sharp images but, as in any GAN, allows mode collapse. α-GAN uses both. With a deterministic encoder, $E$ and $G$ maximize

$$
\mathbb E_{x \sim P}\Big[-\lambda\big\lVert x - \hat x\big\rVert_1 + D(\hat x) + C(\hat z)\Big] .
$$

The adversarial terms are plain scores because of the loss Rosca et al. use on the generator side, $-\log\sigma(s) + \log\big(1 - \sigma(s)\big)$, the sum of the non-saturating and the minimax losses. With $\sigma(s) = 1/(1 + e^{-s})$ we have $1 - \sigma(s) = e^{-s}/(1 + e^{-s})$, so $\sigma(s)/(1 - \sigma(s)) = e^s$, and taking logarithms,

$$
-\log\sigma(s) + \log\big(1 - \sigma(s)\big) = -s .
$$

The loss is minus the logit. Its gradient with respect to the logit is $-1$ for every $s$, so it never saturates. Its two parts do: from $\sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$, the derivative of $\log(1 - \sigma(s))$ is $-\sigma(s)$ and that of $-\log\sigma(s)$ is $-(1 - \sigma(s))$, so at $\sigma(s) = 0.05$ they give $-0.05$ and $-0.95$, the comparison of the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#which-generator-loss-is-used-in-practice).

The image discriminator also receives samples $G(z)$, $z \sim p(z)$, as fakes next to the reconstructions, so that samples are pushed toward the data as well as reconstructions. With $\hat z = E(x)$ and $\hat x = G(\hat z)$, each network minimizes its own loss with the others fixed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_E &= \mathbb E_{x}\big[\lambda\lVert x - \hat x\rVert_1 - C(\hat z)\big],\\
\mathcal L_G &= \mathbb E_{x}\big[\lambda\lVert x - \hat x\rVert_1 - D(\hat x)\big] - \mathbb E_{z}\, D(G(z)),\\
\mathcal L_D &= -\mathbb E_{x}\Big[\log\sigma\big(D(x)\big) + \log\big(1 - \sigma(D(\hat x))\big)\Big]\\
&\quad - \mathbb E_{z}\log\big(1 - \sigma(D(G(z)))\big),\\
\mathcal L_C &= -\mathbb E_{x}\log\big(1 - \sigma(C(\hat z))\big) - \mathbb E_{z}\log\sigma\big(C(z)\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

The two discriminators train with the binary cross-entropy of the GAN note.

### Why all three parts are needed

Suppose both matching conditions hold without error: the codes are distributed as the prior, $q = p$, and every image is reconstructed, $G(E(x)) = x$. Then for $z \sim p(z)$, $G(z)$ has the distribution of $G(E(x))$ with $x \sim P$, because $z$ and $E(x)$ have the same distribution and the same $G$ is applied to both: $P(G(z) \in A) = P(z \in G^{-1}(A))$ depends only on the distribution of $z$. And $G(E(x)) = x$ has distribution $P$ itself, so the samples are distributed as the data.

Each term guards one condition. The code discriminator enforces the first; without it the codes could sit anywhere in $\mathbb R^d$, and prior samples would land where $G$ never learned anything, the failure of a plain autoencoder. The reconstruction term enforces the second, and it is what discourages mode collapse: a generator that dropped a mode could not rebuild the images of that mode, and its $L_1$ loss would grow. Both hold only approximately, so the image discriminator checks the samples directly, and by judging the reconstructions it pushes $G$ toward a sharp image near $x$ instead of the blurry median.

On ColorMNIST, CelebA and CIFAR-10, α-GAN was competitive with DCGAN ([Radford, Metz and Chintala, 2016](#references)) and WGAN-GP, but not uniformly better, and its reconstructions beat those of the autoencoder-based AGE ([Ulyanov, Vedaldi and Lempitsky, 2017](#references)).

## α-WGAN: two Wasserstein critics \{#alpha-wgan-two-wasserstein-critics\}

The generator-side losses of α-GAN are already minus a score, the form of the [WGAN generator loss](/notes/generative-models/wgan#wgan-losses). Only the training of the discriminators separates α-GAN from a Wasserstein version. [Kwon, Han and Kim (2019)](#references) keep the encoder and generator losses and train both $D$ and $C$ as critics with the [gradient penalty](/notes/generative-models/wgan#wgan-gp-gradient-penalty). With $\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}$ the penalty weight,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_D ={}& \mathbb E_{x} D(\hat x) + \mathbb E_{z} D(G(z)) - 2\,\mathbb E_{x} D(x)\\
&+ \lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}\,\mathrm{GP}_D,\\
\mathcal L_C ={}& \mathbb E_{x} C(\hat z) - \mathbb E_{z} C(z) + \lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}\,\mathrm{GP}_C,\\
\mathcal L_{EG} ={}& \lambda\,\mathbb E_{x}\lVert x - \hat x\rVert_1 - \mathbb E_{x} D(\hat x)\\
&- \mathbb E_{z} D(G(z)) - \mathbb E_{x} C(\hat z).
\end{aligned}
$$

The penalties are those of WGAN-GP, on random points between real and fake inputs:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{GP}_D &= \mathbb E_{\tilde x}\big(\lVert\nabla_{\tilde x} D(\tilde x)\rVert_2 - 1\big)^2,\\
\mathrm{GP}_C &= \mathbb E_{\tilde z}\big(\lVert\nabla_{\tilde z} C(\tilde z)\rVert_2 - 1\big)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\tilde x = \alpha x + (1 - \alpha)\,x_{\mathrm f}$ lies between a real image $x$ and a fake $x_{\mathrm f}$, and $\tilde z = \alpha z + (1 - \alpha)\,\hat z$ between a prior sample and a code, with $\alpha \sim U(0, 1)$ per example. Kwon et al. use $\lambda = \lambda_{\mathrm{GP}} = 10$.

<AlphaFlow />

The image critic's loss is two WGAN comparisons sharing one critic, reconstructions against data and samples against data, hence the factor 2 on the real term. $E$ and $G$ are optimized as one network on the sum $\mathcal L_{EG}$, so each term reaches the networks it depends on:

| Term of $\mathcal L_{EG}$ | Trains | Asks for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\lambda\lVert x - \hat x\rVert_1$ | $E$ and $G$ | every image can be rebuilt from its code |
| $-D(\hat x)$ | $G$, and $E$ through $\hat x$ | reconstructions that look real |
| $-D(G(z))$ | $G$ | samples that look real |
| $-C(\hat z)$ | $E$ | codes that look like prior samples |

Why a Wasserstein critic for the codes too? Early in training the codes of a deterministic encoder can sit far from the prior. A classifier then separates them easily, and an optimal one is constant on the codes, the case of non-overlapping supports in the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#where-it-breaks-supports-that-do-not-overlap), where the generator's gradient vanishes. The gradient penalty instead holds the critic's slope near 1 between codes and prior samples, so it keeps pointing the codes toward the prior. Kwon et al. made the change because their model, trained on 3D brain MRI volumes, was unstable with the classifier losses.

## Worked example: a code critic in one dimension

Take $d = 1$, prior $p = \mathcal N(0, 1)$, and suppose every code is shifted by $\mu$, so $q = \mathcal N(\mu, 1)$. What does each critic tell the encoder?

The classifier's optimal score is the log ratio:

$$
\begin{aligned}
C^*(z) &= \log\frac{p(z)}{q(z)} \\
&= -\frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{(z - \mu)^2}{2} \\
&= -\mu z + \frac{\mu^2}{2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Its mean over the codes, using $\mathbb E_q z = \mu$, is $\mathbb E_q C^* = -\mu^2 + \mu^2/2 = -\mu^2/2$. By the definition of KL, $\mathbb E_q \log(p/q) = -D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\|\,p)$, so this is also a computation of the KL between unit-variance Gaussians whose means differ by $\mu$: $\mu^2/2$. With the critic held fixed, the encoder's loss $-\mathbb E_q C^*$ has derivative $\mu$ with respect to the shift.

For the Wasserstein critic, take $\mu > 0$ and $f(z) = -z$, which is 1-Lipschitz and gives $\mathbb E_p f - \mathbb E_q f = 0 + \mu = \mu$. By the [Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality](/notes/generative-models/wgan#the-dual-form), $W_1(p, q)$ is the supremum of this difference over 1-Lipschitz functions, so $W_1 \ge \mu$; moving every point by $\mu$ is a transport plan of cost $\mu$, so $W_1 \le \mu$. Hence $W_1 = \mu$ and $f$ is optimal. Its gradient has norm 1 everywhere, so $\mathrm{GP}_C = 0$. The encoder's loss $-\mathbb E_q f = \mu$ has derivative 1.

| Shift $\mu$ | Classifier: gradient on the shift | Critic: gradient on the shift |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.5 | 0.5 | 1 |
| 0.05 | 0.05 | 1 |

The classifier's push fades as the codes approach the prior; the critic keeps pushing at the same rate until the codes are centred. Toy numbers, with each critic at its optimum for the current codes.

## Training: four networks, three optimizers

One training step has two parts. First, one update of $E$ and $G$ on $\mathcal L_{EG}$ with the critics fixed. Then updates of $D$ on $\mathcal L_D$ and of $C$ on $\mathcal L_C$, in which reconstructions, samples and codes enter as constants. The ratio between the parts is a choice: WGAN-GP takes 5 critic steps per generator step, each on a fresh real minibatch, while α-GAN updated the encoder and generator twice per discriminator step.

Why not one optimizer for everything? Suppose $\mathcal L_D$ and $\mathcal L_{EG}$ were summed and all parameters optimized together. The gradient with respect to $D$'s parameters, ignoring the penalty, would be

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\nabla\big(\mathbb E D(\hat x) + \mathbb E D(G(z)) - 2\,\mathbb E D(x)\big)\\
&\quad + \nabla\big(-\mathbb E D(\hat x) - \mathbb E D(G(z))\big)\\
&= -2\,\nabla\,\mathbb E D(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The terms on fakes cancel, and the critic would only learn to raise its score on real data. Each player therefore gets its own optimizer over its own parameters: one for $E$ and $G$ together, one for $D$, one for $C$.

Four networks can fail separately, so log every loss term on its own. The reconstruction error on held-out images is a stable number for choosing checkpoints, but it measures the autoencoder, and a run can keep improving it while its samples collapse. Raw critic scores cannot be compared between epochs. Replace $D$ by $D + c$ for a constant $c$: in $\mathcal L_D$ the two fake terms gain $2c$ and the real term loses $2c$, and the penalty sees only gradients, so no loss changes. The same holds for $C$. Nothing fixes the level of the scores, and it drifts with the optimizer's steps; only differences such as $\mathbb E\, D(x) - \mathbb E\, D(\hat x)$ carry meaning. Judge the model from three sides: reconstructions of held-out images, samples from the prior with the metrics of the [evaluation notes](/notes/generative-models/fid), and interpolations between real images.

## Latent interpolation: lerp or slerp?

With an encoder we can walk between two real images: encode them, $z_0 = E(x_0)$ and $z_1 = E(x_1)$, move along a path $z_t$, and decode $G(z_t)$. The simplest path is **linear interpolation** (lerp), $z_t = (1 - t)\,z_0 + t\,z_1$ for $t \in [0, 1]$.

**Spherical linear interpolation** (slerp) follows a great circle instead ([Shoemake, 1985](#references); [White, 2016](#references)). With $\Omega$ the angle between $z_0$ and $z_1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos\Omega &= \frac{\langle z_0, z_1\rangle}{\lVert z_0\rVert\,\lVert z_1\rVert}, \\
z_t &= \frac{\sin\big((1 - t)\,\Omega\big)}{\sin\Omega}\, z_0 + \frac{\sin(t\,\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\, z_1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Why is this a great circle? Let $z_0$ and $z_1$ have the same length $r$, and write $z_1 = r(\cos\Omega\, u + \sin\Omega\, v)$ with $u = z_0/r$ and $v$ a unit vector orthogonal to $u$ in their plane. Call the two coefficients $a$ and $b$. The sine difference formula gives $\sin\big((1 - t)\Omega\big) = \sin\Omega\cos t\Omega - \cos\Omega \sin t\Omega$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_t &= r\big[(a + b\cos\Omega)\, u + b\sin\Omega\, v\big] \\
&= r\big[\cos(t\Omega)\, u + \sin(t\Omega)\, v\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

The point turns from $z_0$ toward $z_1$ at the angle $t\Omega$ and keeps the length $r$, since $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$. As $\Omega \to 0$ the coefficients $\sin(k\Omega)/\sin\Omega$ tend to $k$, because $\sin x / x \to 1$: to $1 - t$ and $t$, so implementations fall back to lerp for nearly parallel vectors. For nearly antipodal ones the great circle is not unique, and a path has to be chosen explicitly.

Why does the difference matter? In high dimension, samples of $\mathcal N(0, I_d)$ lie in a thin shell: the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#high-dimensions-the-shell) shows that $\lVert z\rVert^2$, a sum of $d$ independent squared standard normals, has mean $d$ and variance $2d$. A first-order Taylor expansion of the square root turns this into a length near $\sqrt d$ with standard deviation about $\sqrt{2d}/(2\sqrt d) = 1/\sqrt 2 = 0.71$. Two independent samples are nearly orthogonal: $\langle z_0, z_1\rangle = \sum_i z_{0i} z_{1i}$ is a sum of $d$ independent terms of mean 0 and variance 1, so it has standard deviation $\sqrt d$, and dividing by $\lVert z_0\rVert\,\lVert z_1\rVert \approx d$ gives a cosine with standard deviation $1/\sqrt d$. Their lerp midpoint $(z_0 + z_1)/2$ is an [affine map](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps) of the pair, with covariance $\tfrac14(I + I) = \tfrac12 I$, so its length is close to $\sqrt{d/2}$:

| For $d = 128$ | Value |
| --- | --- |
| mean length of a prior sample (chi distribution) | 11.29, spread 0.71 |
| angle between two samples, $1/\sqrt{128} = 0.088$ rad | $90° \pm 5°$ |
| mean length of the lerp midpoint | 7.98, spread 0.50 |
| prior probability of a squared length $\le d/2$ ($\chi^2_{128}$ distribution function) | $4 \times 10^{-7}$ |

The midpoint lies $(11.29 - 7.98)/0.71 = 4.7$ standard deviations inside the shell, in a region that training on $z \sim p(z)$ almost never visits. Slerp keeps the length typical even when the two lengths differ: at $\Omega = 90°$ its two terms are orthogonal, so by Pythagoras $\lVert z_t\rVert^2 = \cos^2(\pi t/2)\lVert z_0\rVert^2 + \sin^2(\pi t/2)\lVert z_1\rVert^2$, a weighted average of the two squared lengths.

<aside class="callout"><strong>High density is not the same as typical.</strong> The lerp midpoint has a higher prior density than any typical sample: the log-density of $\mathcal N(0, I)$ is $-\lVert z\rVert^2/2$ plus a constant, so at squared length $d/2$ it exceeds its value at squared length $d$ by $d/4 = 32$ nats. Yet points of that length almost never occur, because the volume near the centre is tiny. A generator learns what the prior produces.</aside>

In two dimensions take $z_0 = (3, 0)$ and $z_1 = (0, 3)$. The lerp midpoint is $(1.5, 1.5)$, of length $3/\sqrt 2 = 2.12$. For slerp, $\Omega = 90°$ and both coefficients at $t = \tfrac12$ are $\sin 45° / \sin 90° = 0.707$, so the midpoint is $(2.12, 2.12)$, of length 3. In two dimensions this is only an illustration; in 128, almost every pair is nearly orthogonal, and the same factor $1/\sqrt 2$ moves almost every lerp midpoint out of the shell.

For an autoencoding GAN the endpoints are codes of real images. If the code critic has matched $q(z)$ to the prior, codes have prior-like lengths and codes of unrelated images are nearly orthogonal, so the argument carries over; codes of similar images are close in angle, and the two paths nearly coincide. Slerp only keeps the length typical. It does not keep the path away from holes in $q(z)$, which White calls dead zones. Linear interpolation is still common, for example in the VAE examples of [Murphy (2023](#references), Section 20.3.5), and with a non-Gaussian latent, such as StyleGAN's $\mathcal W$, the shell argument does not apply.

## Where it breaks

Rosca et al. found every model they tested, α-GAN and the baselines alike, sensitive to the architectures: minor changes led to catastrophic mode collapse, whatever the other hyperparameters. An autoencoding GAN has four networks, three optimizers, the weights $\lambda$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{GP}}$ and the update ratios, and the reconstruction term does not remove that sensitivity. A large $\lambda$ brings back the blur of the pixel loss; a small one weakens the guard against mode collapse.

The code size shows the tension concretely. The AAE paper ties a deterministic encoder's code dimension to the intrinsic dimension of the data, 5 to 8 for MNIST ([Makhzani et al., 2015](#references), Appendix A.1). A count of volumes shows why the code should not be larger. Suppose the images lie on a surface described by $m$ coordinates $u \in [0, 1]^m$, and the encoder is $L$-Lipschitz in them. Its codes then form a set of zero volume in $\mathbb R^d$ whenever $d > m$, so the prior gives that set probability zero.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: the codes have zero volume</summary>

Cut the cube of coordinates into $n^m$ small cubes of side $1/n$. Each has diameter $\sqrt m / n$, so by the Lipschitz bound its codes lie in a ball of radius $L\sqrt m / n$. With $V_d$ the volume of the unit ball in $\mathbb R^d$, adding up the balls gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{vol}(\text{codes}) &\le n^m\, V_d \big(L\sqrt m / n\big)^d \\
&= V_d\, (L\sqrt m)^d\, n^{m - d} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The bound holds for every $n$, and for $d > m$ it tends to 0 as $n \to \infty$.

</details>

For $d > m$ the cloud of codes and the prior therefore have [supports that do not overlap](/notes/generative-models/gans#where-it-breaks-supports-that-do-not-overlap): prior samples land off the surface of codes, where $G$ never learned anything. A stochastic encoder adds noise that fills the $d - m$ missing directions, which is why the AAE paper recommends one for codes of size 1000. The code cannot be too small either: if two images share a code, $G$ can return only one of them, and the reconstruction term grows. Kwon et al., with brain volumes, saw their model collapse with 100-dimensional codes and needed 1000. Neither number transfers: the code must be large enough for the decoder to rebuild every image and small enough for a critic to match its distribution to the prior, and only the held-out reconstructions and samples tell where that range lies.

## What changed

| | WGAN-GP | AAE | α-GAN | α-WGAN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Networks | $G$, critic | $E$, $G$, $C$ | $E$, $G$, $D$, $C$ | $E$, $G$, $D$, $C$ |
| Codes matched to the prior by | — | a classifier on codes | a classifier on codes | a WGAN-GP critic on codes |
| Pixel term | none | squared error | $\lambda\lVert x - \hat x\rVert_1$ | $\lambda\lVert x - \hat x\rVert_1$ |
| Image adversary sees | samples | — | reconstructions and samples | reconstructions and samples |
| Guard against mode collapse | none | reconstruction | reconstruction | reconstruction |
| Can encode real images | no | yes | yes | yes |

## What you should remember

- Averaged over data, the VAE's KL is $I_q(x; z) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z)\,\|\,p(z))$; sampling needs only the second part.
- A classifier's optimal logit is $\log(p/q)$: a code discriminator estimates the marginal mismatch from samples.
- α-GAN: maximize $-\lambda\lVert x - \hat x\rVert_1 + D(\hat x) + C(\hat z)$; matched codes plus exact reconstruction give samples distributed as the data.
- The loss $-\log\sigma(s) + \log(1 - \sigma(s)) = -s$ never saturates.
- α-WGAN: both discriminators are WGAN-GP critics; one optimizer per player.
- Interpolate Gaussian codes with slerp: a lerp midpoint has length near $\sqrt{d/2}$, outside the shell of radius $\sqrt d$.

## Notebook

The [α-WGAN notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/spring-2025-long/seminars/07_alpha-WGAN/alpha-WGAN.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* course trains an α-WGAN on CIFAR-10. Cell numbers count every cell, markdown included, from 1. The notebook has no saved outputs, so it documents the procedure rather than results.

- Setup and data (cells 1–6): logging goes to Comet.ml, which the training code calls, so the notebook needs an account or those calls removed. CIFAR-10 is resized to $64 \times 64$ and scaled to $[-1, 1]$.
- Networks (cells 7–14): a residual encoder and a mirrored generator; an image critic with batch normalization, which WGAN-GP advises against because its penalty is per example ([Gulrajani et al., 2017](#references)); and an MLP code critic.
- Losses (cells 15–17): one gradient-penalty function serves both critics. Reconstructions never reach the image critic, and the encoder–generator loss has no $-D(\hat x)$ term, so the model is an adversarial autoencoder with an extra critic on samples; the full α-WGAN adds the reconstructions to the critic loss with the real term doubled.
- Training loop (cells 18–22): three AdamW optimizers with cosine warm restarts ($T_0 = 10$ epochs, doubling), each scheduler stepped once at the end of an epoch; one encoder–generator step, then `n_critic` rounds of a $D$ and a $C$ step on the same real batch. Early stopping watches the reconstruction loss on the test split with a patience of 10 epochs: a best value at epoch 10, just before the first restart, stops the run at epoch 20. PyTorch's schedule sets the rate to $\eta_{\max} \cdot \tfrac12\big(1 + \cos(\pi T_{\text{cur}} / T_i)\big)$, with $T_i$ the length of the current cycle and $T_{\text{cur}}$ the epochs since its start. Epoch 20 runs after 19 steps, 10 of which closed the first cycle, so $T_{\text{cur}} = 9$, $T_i = 20$, and the rate is $2 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot \tfrac12\big(1 + \cos(9\pi/20)\big) = 10^{-4} \cdot 1.156 \approx 1.2 \cdot 10^{-4}$. On a fresh run it also crashes twice: `np.Inf` no longer exists in NumPy 2, and the first checkpoint is saved before its directory is created.
- Configuration and run (cells 23–29): $d = 128$, `base_channels = 256`, $\lambda = \lambda_{\mathrm{GP}} = 10$, learning rate $2 \cdot 10^{-4}$, betas $(0, 0.9)$, $n_{\text{critic}} = 5$, up to 100 epochs.
- Evaluation and interpolation (cells 30–39): samples, test reconstructions, loss curves, and slerp in seven steps between the codes of consecutive test images.

## Next

So far every GAN in the course has generated images from noise, and the encoder of this note only gave that noise a meaning. Rosca et al. point out that α-GAN matches images to their reconstructions point by point, but codes only in distribution; adding a pointwise code reconstruction $\lVert z - E(G(z))\rVert$ would make it resemble CycleGAN. The [next note](/notes/generative-models/cyclegan) replaces the code space by a second image domain: two generators translate images between domains without paired examples, and a cycle of reconstructions ties every output to its input.
