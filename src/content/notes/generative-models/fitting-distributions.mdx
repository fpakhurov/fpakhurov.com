The practical discussion returns to a small problem: move and reshape a Gaussian model so that it describes a target distribution. The ingredients are samples, a density evaluated at those samples, trainable parameters and an optimizer. No neural network is required to see how they fit together.

## What the optimizer changes

Let $P$ be the fixed target and let

$$
Q_\theta=\mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta,\Sigma_\theta)
$$

be the model. The mean determines its location; the covariance determines its scale and orientation. The notes refer to `train_mu` and `train_sigma`: these are quantities to optimize, rather than samples or already computed densities.

In one dimension, a positive standard deviation can be represented as $\sigma=\exp(a)$, with unconstrained trainable $a$. For a two-dimensional covariance, the associated notebook uses

$$
L=\begin{pmatrix}e^a&0\\b&e^c\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Sigma=LL^\top.
$$

The diagonal of $L$ is positive, so it is invertible and $\Sigma$ is positive definite for finite parameters in exact arithmetic. The mean and the three entries $a,b,c$ are the parameters supplied to the optimizer. Optimizing arbitrary covariance entries would not preserve this constraint.

## Evaluate both densities at the same target samples

For forward KL, the expectation is taken under $P$:

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q_\theta)
=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}\left[\log p(X)-\log q_\theta(X)\right].
$$

With samples $x_1,\ldots,x_N\sim P$, a Monte Carlo estimate is

$$
\widehat{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}
=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\log p(x_i)-\log q_\theta(x_i)\right].
$$

Here `Q` is a distribution object; `Q.log_prob(x)` evaluates its log-density at the target samples. Drawing unrelated samples from $Q$ and subtracting the two sets of density values would not estimate this forward-KL expectation. Model samples are useful for plotting the current fit.

In the toy experiment, $P$ is a known Gaussian, so its density is available. With real data we usually have observations without a known $p(x)$. The target term does not depend on $\theta$, and can be omitted during optimization:

$$
\nabla_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q_\theta)
=-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}\left[\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(X)\right],
$$

under conditions allowing differentiation through the expectation. This gives the average negative log-likelihood (NLL):

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac1N\sum_i\log q_\theta(x_i).
$$

NLL has the same parameter gradients as the empirical forward-KL estimate. Its numerical value is different: without $p(x)$, we cannot report the KL value itself. When the entropy decomposition is finite, this is the same constant-entropy argument used in [Entropy and divergences](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#why-cross-entropy-is-a-training-objective).

The margin also recalls probabilities $1/2$ and $2/10$. These belong to the [earlier surprise discussion](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#surprise-of-an-outcome): they give $1$ bit and $\log_2 5\approx2.322$ bits of surprise, respectively. Their role is to explain the logarithmic penalty, rather than to specify Gaussian parameters.

## From a scalar loss to an optimizer step

The handwritten sequence is `loss` → `backward` → `optim.step`. First clear old gradients. Then construct the current distribution, evaluate the scalar loss and call `loss.backward()` to accumulate derivatives with respect to the trainable leaf parameters. `optimizer.step()` uses those derivatives to update their values. A plain gradient-descent step is

$$
\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}.
$$

The margin mentions `torch.optim.SGD`; the linked notebook uses Adam, which adjusts the update using running statistics of gradients. Both receive the tensors to optimize. The loss is recomputed with the updated parameters on the next iteration. PyTorch's [autograd tutorial](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/beginner/basics/autogradqs_tutorial.html) explains gradient accumulation and the graph rebuilt during each forward pass.

The following compact version follows notebook cells 5, 8 and 15. It keeps calculations in log space and uses the Cholesky factor directly through [`MultivariateNormal(scale_tril=...)`](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/distributions.html#torch.distributions.multivariate_normal.MultivariateNormal):

```python
import torch
from torch.distributions import MultivariateNormal

target = MultivariateNormal(torch.tensor([-5.0, -5.0]), torch.eye(2))
x = target.sample((1024,))  # Fixed observations; shape (1024, 2).

mu = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(2))
raw = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(3))  # a, b, c
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam([mu, raw], lr=0.05)

for _ in range(1000):
    optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    a, b, c = raw.unbind()
    zero = raw.new_zeros(())
    L = torch.stack([
        torch.stack([a.exp(), zero]),
        torch.stack([b, c.exp()]),
    ])
    Q = MultivariateNormal(mu, scale_tril=L)
    loss = -Q.log_prob(x).mean()  # NLL in nats, not a KL value.
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
```

This is an editorial example, not a new execution result. A fixed finite sample is fitted here, so its estimated mean and covariance need not exactly equal those of the target. Rebuilding $Q$ inside the loop preserves the connection between its current density and the trainable tensors. Direct `log_prob` also avoids exponentiating a small density only to take its logarithm again.

The exponential parameterization enforces positive diagonal entries mathematically. Extreme values can still underflow or overflow in floating-point arithmetic, so it does not guarantee numerical stability for every training run.

The notebook's `kl_loss` uses base-2 logarithms; `log_prob` returns natural logarithms. The units are related by

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\text{bits}}=\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\text{nats}}}{\ln 2}.
$$

The same conversion applies to NLL. Changing the base scales the objective and its gradients by a positive constant, preserving the minimizer; numerical optimizer steps can still depend on that scaling.

## Support and model restrictions

The notes emphasize that $q(x)$ must not vanish where the target has probability mass. More precisely, forward KL is infinite if a set has positive probability under $P$ and zero probability under $Q$. Mere overlap of supports is insufficient. Two nonsingular Gaussians have positive densities throughout $\mathbb{R}^d$, although floating-point calculations can still underflow and support coverage alone does not guarantee finite KL for arbitrary distributions.

A single Gaussian also cannot reproduce every target shape. Notebook cells 17-19 fit one Gaussian to a two-Gaussian mixture. Forward KL can encourage the model to spread across both modes, but no optimizer can turn this restricted family into a mixture. The objective and the representational capacity of $Q$ are separate choices.

## Seminar notebook and review

The [Distances between Distributions notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/42dd64b535f5bedf1d2b3db28f2f21ce96c9f09f/seminars/01_metrics-and-distances/distances.ipynb) supplies the practical context. Cell numbers count all cells from 1 in this pinned snapshot; the exact historical version shown during the seminar is unconfirmed. Cells 4-5 define plotting and distribution helpers, cells 8-11 compare the target and initial model, and cells 12-15 construct and optimize the forward-KL estimate.

The later experiments need separate qualifications. Cell 21 combines KL terms with weights $0.7$ and $0.3$, unlike symmetric JS. It also draws model samples with `sample()`, so ordinary backpropagation through the evaluated densities misses the derivative arising from the parameter-dependent sampling distribution. A differentiable estimator needs a suitable score-function or reparameterization treatment, as described in PyTorch's [distribution gradient documentation](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/distributions.html#score-function). This issue does not affect the forward-KL loop above: its observations come from the fixed target.

Cell 26 draws a new set of target samples but reuses previously calculated `px`. A density-MSE comparison must evaluate both $p(x)$ and $q(x)$ at those same new points. Its displayed results should not be interpreted as a verified density-fitting comparison without that correction.
