The [previous note](/notes/generative-models/mcmc-samplers) drew samples from a density known only up to a constant, with MALA, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and NUTS. There the target was fixed. An **energy-based model** (EBM) makes it learnable: a neural network $E_\theta(x)$ with one scalar output defines the density $p_\theta(x) = e^{-E_\theta(x)}/Z_\theta$, and training moves $\theta$ until that density fits the data ([LeCun et al., 2006](#references)). Nothing restricts the architecture: no invertibility as in flows, no ordering of the pixels as in autoregressive models, no encoder as in a VAE. The price is the normalizer $Z_\theta$. The likelihood cannot be evaluated, its gradient needs samples from the model, and so every training step runs a Markov chain on the model being trained. This note derives that gradient, builds the standard recipe around it (short Langevin chains, a replay buffer, a penalty on the energies), explains why training is unstable, gives two estimators that need no samples during training, and ends with what energies are used for besides generation.

**Notation.** $x \in \mathbb R^d$ is a data point, for example an MNIST image with $d = 784$ pixel values in $[-1, 1]$. $P$ is the data distribution with density $p$. The **energy** $E_\theta: \mathbb R^d \to \mathbb R$ is a network with parameters $\theta$, and the model density is $p_\theta(x) = e^{-E_\theta(x)}/Z_\theta$ with the **partition function** $Z_\theta = \int e^{-E_\theta(x)}\,dx$. Data samples $x^+ \sim P$ are the positive examples; samples $x^-$ meant to come from $p_\theta$ are the negative examples. $\nabla_\theta$ is the gradient with respect to the parameters and $\nabla_x$ with respect to the input. One Langevin step of size $\varepsilon$ is $x \leftarrow x - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla_x E_\theta(x) + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,z$ with $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, as in the [probability course](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#langevin-dynamics), and $K$ is the number of steps of a chain. The second argument of $\mathcal N$ is the variance.

## An energy defines a density

Low energy means high probability. Exponentiation turns any real-valued $E_\theta$ into a positive function, and dividing by $Z_\theta$ makes it integrate to one. This is the Boltzmann, or Gibbs, distribution of statistical physics at temperature 1. The only condition is that $Z_\theta$ be finite: the energy must grow fast enough at infinity, or the domain must be bounded, as it is for images with pixels in $[-1, 1]$. The quadratic energy $E(x) = x^2/2$ gives $Z = \sqrt{2\pi}$ and $p = \mathcal N(0, 1)$; a network can draw any landscape, with as many valleys as the data have modes.

Only differences of energy matter:

$$
\frac{p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(y)} = e^{E_\theta(y) - E_\theta(x)} .
$$

Adding a constant $c$ to the energy multiplies $Z_\theta$ by $e^{-c}$ and leaves $p_\theta$ unchanged. This freedom returns below as the offset problem.

Why not compute $Z_\theta$? It is an integral over $\mathbb R^d$. A grid with just 10 points per pixel of an MNIST image has $10^{784}$ nodes. Everything below is therefore arranged so that $Z_\theta$ cancels or is never needed.

### Decisions need no normalizer

Many uses of an energy compare values and never normalize them. With an energy $E_\theta(x, y)$ of an input $x$ and an answer $y$, inference is $\hat y = \arg\min_y E_\theta(x, y)$: classification when $y$ is a label, restoration when $x$ is a noisy image and $y$ a clean one ([LeCun et al., 2006](#references), Section 1.1 and Figure 2). Only the order of the energies matters, so $Z$ never appears. Image EBMs do the same with Langevin dynamics: started at a corrupted test image, the chain removes salt-and-pepper noise or fills a masked region ([Du and Mordatch, 2019](#references), Section 4.2). A density, and with it $Z$, is needed when the model must generate samples, report a likelihood, or be combined with other models on a common scale.

## The likelihood gradient: positive and negative phases

Maximum likelihood minimizes the negative log-likelihood, which equals the KL divergence from the data to the model up to a constant ([entropy note](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#why-cross-entropy-is-the-loss-we-minimize)). For an EBM, $\log p_\theta(x) = -E_\theta(x) - \log Z_\theta$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\theta) &= -\mathbb E_{x\sim P}\log p_\theta(x)\\
&= \mathbb E_{x\sim P}\,E_\theta(x) + \log Z_\theta .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term is an average over the data; its gradient is $\mathbb E_{x\sim P}\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)$. The second term is where the difficulty lives. Three steps:

1. The chain rule for the logarithm: $\nabla_\theta \log Z_\theta = \nabla_\theta Z_\theta / Z_\theta$.
2. Differentiate under the integral sign, which is allowed when $E_\theta$ is differentiable in $\theta$ and the gradient of the integrand is bounded by an integrable function: $\nabla_\theta Z_\theta = \int \nabla_\theta e^{-E_\theta(x)}\,dx = \int e^{-E_\theta(x)}\big(-\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)\big)\,dx$.
3. Divide by $Z_\theta$ and recognize the model density $e^{-E_\theta(x)}/Z_\theta = p_\theta(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta \log Z_\theta &= \int p_\theta(x)\big(-\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)\big)\,dx\\
&= -\mathbb E_{x\sim p_\theta}\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Together,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta \mathcal L(\theta) = {}& \underbrace{\mathbb E_{x^+\sim P}\,\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x^+)}_{\text{positive phase}}\\
&- \underbrace{\mathbb E_{x^-\sim p_\theta}\,\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x^-)}_{\text{negative phase}} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and the gradient of the log-likelihood is the same with the sign flipped, $-\mathbb E_P \nabla_\theta E_\theta(x^+) + \mathbb E_{p_\theta}\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x^-)$. A gradient step $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta\mathcal L$ lowers the energy at the data and raises it at the model's own samples: the negative phase pulls up the energy of every point in proportion to its probability under the model ([LeCun et al., 2006](#references), Section 2.2.4). The update stops when the two expectations agree, $\mathbb E_P\nabla_\theta E_\theta = \mathbb E_{p_\theta}\nabla_\theta E_\theta$: maximum likelihood matches the averages of the statistics $\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)$ under the data and under the model. The derivation holds for any architecture ([Song and Kingma, 2021](#references), Section 3).

**Worked example: three states.** Let $x \in \{a, b, c\}$ and let the energy be a table, $\theta = (E_a, E_b, E_c) = (0, 1, 2)$. Then $Z = 1 + e^{-1} + e^{-2} = 1.503$ and $p_\theta = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$. Let the data be $P = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$. For a table, $\partial E_\theta(x)/\partial E_j$ is 1 if $x = j$ and 0 otherwise, so the two phases are $P(j)$ and $p_\theta(j)$:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial E_j} = P(j) - p_\theta(j) = (-0.165,\ 0.055,\ 0.110) .
$$

A step against this gradient raises $E_a$, where the model puts too much mass, and lowers $E_b$ and $E_c$. With step size 1 the energies become $(0.165, 0.945, 1.890)$ and $p_\theta = (0.611, 0.280, 0.109)$, closer to $P$. The fixed point is $p_\theta = P$, that is $E_j = -\log P(j) + \text{const}$. The three components of the gradient sum to zero, so the mean energy stays at 1: the gradient never moves the overall level of the energies.

<aside class="callout"><strong>The loss behind the gradient.</strong> In code the gradient comes from the surrogate loss $\mathcal L_{\text{CD}}(\theta) = \frac1N\sum_i E_\theta(x^+_i) - \frac1N\sum_i E_\theta(x^-_i)$, with the negative samples $x^-_i$ treated as constants: no gradient flows through the sampler. Its gradient is a Monte Carlo estimate of the expression above. Its value is not the negative log-likelihood. It is close to zero whenever samples and data receive similar energies, so it does not show whether training is making progress. With $f = -E_\theta$ it has the form of the <a href="/notes/generative-models/wgan#wgan-losses">WGAN critic loss</a>; the difference is that the negative examples come from MCMC on the critic itself rather than from a generator network (<a href="#references">Du and Mordatch, 2019</a>, Section 3.1).</aside>

## Why the negative phase needs samples from the model

The positive phase is an average over the training set. The negative phase is an average under $p_\theta$: the distribution being trained, which changes after every update and whose normalizer is unknown. [Importance sampling](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#importance-sampling) would need a proposal close to $p_\theta$ in $d$ dimensions, which is as hard as the original problem. What remains is Markov chain Monte Carlo, which needs only

$$
\nabla_x \log p_\theta(x) = -\nabla_x E_\theta(x) - \underbrace{\nabla_x \log Z_\theta}_{=\,0} ,
$$

since $Z_\theta$ depends on $\theta$ but not on $x$. **Langevin dynamics**,

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\nabla_x E_\theta(x_k) + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,z_k,\\
z_k \sim \mathcal N(0, I),
\end{gathered}
$$

is the usual choice for images: each step costs one gradient of the energy network with respect to its input, a forward and a backward pass ([Du and Mordatch, 2019](#references), Section 3). The distribution of $x_K$ approaches $p_\theta$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$ and $K \to \infty$; MALA and HMC from the [previous note](/notes/generative-models/mcmc-samplers) remove the step-size bias at the cost of a density ratio, which for an EBM is $e^{E_\theta(x) - E_\theta(x')}$ and also free of $Z_\theta$. The EBM literature often calls the sampler **SGLD**, after the stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics of [Welling and Teh (2011)](#references), who used Langevin steps with minibatch gradients to sample model parameters from a posterior. Here the gradient is exact and the chain runs in data space.

The obstacle is cost. On a multimodal energy, such as one that fits images, Langevin chains mix slowly: chains from different starts get trapped in different modes ([Nijkamp et al., 2019](#references), Section 1.1). A long chain would be needed after every parameter update. The methods below shorten the chains and pay for it with bias.

## Contrastive divergence: stopping the chains early

[Hinton (2002)](#references) proposed to stop the chains early. **Contrastive divergence** (CD-$k$) starts each chain at a data point, runs only $k$ steps (one full Gibbs step in the original work on products of experts) and uses the endpoints as negative examples. It does not follow the likelihood gradient. Write $P_0 = P$ for the data, $P_1$ for the distribution after one step of the chain and $P_\infty = p_\theta$ for its equilibrium. CD-1 follows, after dropping a term that is usually small, the gradient of

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_0\,\|\,P_\infty) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_1\,\|\,P_\infty) .
$$

The difference is never negative, because a step of a Markov chain cannot increase the KL divergence to its stationary distribution. For a chain whose transitions all have positive probability, it is zero only when $P_1 = P_0$, which means the data are already the equilibrium: the model is perfect ([Hinton, 2002](#references), Section 3). In words, CD asks that the chain not walk away from the data.

Modern image EBMs keep the short chains but start them from noise: uniform noise in the pixel range, followed by $K$ Langevin steps on the current energy, with $K$ between about 10 and 100 ([Du and Mordatch, 2019](#references); [Nijkamp et al., 2019](#references)). The gradient estimate is the same, with $x^-$ the chain endpoints. Because the endpoints are not samples from $p_\theta$, the estimate is biased ([Song and Kingma, 2021](#references), Section 3).

### What short chains learn

The bias has a definite direction, and a one-parameter example shows it exactly. Let the data be $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ and the energy $E_v(x) = x^2/(2v)$, so that $p_v = \mathcal N(0, v)$ and the right answer is $v = 1$. Since $\partial E_v/\partial v = -x^2/(2v^2)$, the CD gradient is

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\text{CD}}}{\partial v} = -\frac{1}{2v^2}\Big(\mathbb E\,(x^+)^2 - \mathbb E\,(x^-)^2\Big) .
$$

Training stops where the negative samples have the second moment of the data, $\mathbb E(x^-)^2 = 1$, whatever $v$ that takes. Let the negative samples come from $K$ Langevin steps started at $U(-3, 3)$, whose variance is 3. Each step multiplies $x$ by $a = 1 - \varepsilon/(2v)$, with $0 < a < 1$ for $v > \varepsilon/2$, and adds independent noise of variance $\varepsilon$, so after $K$ steps

$$
\operatorname{Var} x^- = 3\,a^{2K} + \varepsilon\,\frac{1 - a^{2K}}{1 - a^2} .
$$

With $\varepsilon = 0.01$ and $K = 10$ the chain has barely contracted the noise, and the only way to bring its output variance down to 1 is a steep energy: CD stops at $v = 0.088$, a model with standard deviation 0.30 instead of 1. With $K = 100$ it stops at $v = 0.58$. With $K = 1000$ it stops at $0.997$, essentially the long-run value $0.9975$ of unadjusted Langevin with this step size, slightly below 1 because of its discretization bias. The short chain's samples match the data in variance, the only statistic this energy can see; the model's density does not. [Nijkamp et al. (2019)](#references) describe the same effect for image EBMs trained with short chains from noise: the result is a poor density but a good sampler, a fixed number of Langevin steps that turns noise into realistic images.

## Persistent chains and the replay buffer

Chains restarted at every step throw away the work of the previous step. **Persistent contrastive divergence** ([Tieleman, 2008](#references)) keeps them: each training step continues the chains from where the last one stopped. The parameters change little between steps, so a chain close to equilibrium for the old model stays close for the new one, and the effective length of the chains grows over training. A **replay buffer** generalizes this to a pool of past samples ([Du and Mordatch, 2019](#references), Section 3.2). A chain starts from a stored sample with probability 0.95 and from uniform noise otherwise, runs $K$ Langevin steps, and its endpoint goes back into the buffer. The stored samples let refinement continue across training steps, so short chains suffice. The fresh noise keeps exploring and stops the buffer from collapsing onto a few images. Du and Mordatch found the buffer essential: on continuous MNIST, training with plain persistent chains instead gave a markedly worse likelihood, and training was insensitive to the buffer size above about 10,000 samples (Appendices A.3 and A.4).

In the Gaussian example the buffer makes the age of a sample geometric: it has run $n$ rounds of $K$ steps with probability $0.05 \cdot 0.95^{\,n-1}$, 20 rounds on average. CD then stops at $v = 0.52$ for $K = 10$ instead of $0.088$, and at $0.94$ for $K = 100$ instead of $0.58$, the blue curve in the figure above. The fresh 5% keep a bias: they enter the negative phase after only $K$ steps.

### One training step

A configuration that trains a small convolutional energy on MNIST (four stride-2 convolutions with Swish activations and two linear layers, 76,993 parameters; pixels in $[-1, 1]$) puts these pieces together. The figure shows one step; the numbers match its badges.

1. **Starts.** 128 chains. Each starts from a stored sample with probability 0.95 or from uniform noise on $[-1, 1]^{784}$ with probability 0.05, about 6.4 fresh starts per batch. The buffer holds up to 8192 images.
2. **Langevin steps.** 60 steps with the network's parameters held fixed. Each step adds Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.005, clamps the pixels to $[-1, 1]$, moves against the energy gradient with step size 10 after clipping every component of the gradient to $\pm 0.03$, and clamps again. The gradient step moves a pixel by at most 0.3.
3. **Two phases.** The network scores a batch of 128 data images $x^+$ and the 128 samples $x^-$.
4. **Loss and update.** $\mathcal L = \text{mean}\,E_\theta(x^+) - \text{mean}\,E_\theta(x^-) + \alpha\,\text{mean}\big(E_\theta(x^+)^2 + E_\theta(x^-)^2\big)$ with $\alpha = 0.1$, the penalty explained in the next section, minimized by Adam with $\beta_1 = 0$ and learning rate $10^{-4}$.
5. **Write back.** The samples go to the front of the buffer; beyond 8192 images the oldest drop out.

Du and Mordatch's CIFAR-10 setting is close: 60 steps of size 10, gradient components clipped to 0.01, noise of standard deviation 0.005, a buffer of 10,000 images, penalty weight 1, Adam with $\beta_1 = 0$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$ and learning rate $10^{-4}$, and spectral normalization in all layers, with images scaled to $[0, 1]$ ([Du and Mordatch, 2019](#references), Appendix A.11).

<aside class="callout"><strong>Not quite Langevin.</strong> The sampler in step 2 is not a Langevin sampler of $p_\theta$. A drift step $\eta = 10$ on $\nabla_x E_\theta$ would need noise of standard deviation $\sqrt{2\eta} = 4.47$; the noise used is 0.005, 894 times smaller. A drift step $\eta$ and a noise level $s$ chosen separately make exact Langevin dynamics for the sharpened density $\propto p_\theta(x)^{1/T}$: matching $x - \eta\nabla_x E_\theta + s\,z$ with $x - \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla_x (E_\theta/T) + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,z$ gives $\varepsilon = s^2$ and temperature $T = s^2/(2\eta)$, here $1.25 \cdot 10^{-6}$. Ignoring the clipping, the chain is gradient descent on the energy with a little noise, and its endpoints sit near local minima. The relaxation is standard: with the proper ratio, the noise drowns the gradient at any step size large enough to move the chain (<a href="#references">Grathwohl et al., 2019</a>, Appendix H.1). Du and Mordatch found that very low noise gave poor results and high noise made the sampler fail early in training, and recommend fixing the noise at 0.005 and tuning the step size, typically 10 to 100 (Appendix A.12). The lesson of the Gaussian example applies with more force: the trained energy is best read as a function whose short descent from noise produces data, not as a calibrated density.</aside>

**Generating after training.** New images come from chains started at uniform noise and run longer than during training, for example 256 steps instead of 60: generation happens once rather than at every update, and it has no buffer to start from. Du and Mordatch instead sample at test time from a replay buffer, with somewhat more noise than in training, because low noise makes the chains settle into a few high-probability images (Appendix A.2).

## The offset problem: why energies drift

The CD loss depends only on differences between energies. Shift every energy by the same constant and $\mathcal L_{\text{CD}}$ is unchanged, and so is $p_\theta$. In the three-state example the gradient components sum to zero; for a network whose last layer has a bias $b$, every energy depends on $b$ with derivative 1, so $\partial\mathcal L_{\text{CD}}/\partial b = 1 - 1 = 0$ exactly. Nothing in the loss holds the overall level of the energies, and the other parameters move it freely as a side effect of their updates. The scale is not held either: whenever the sampler lags behind the model, the network can widen the gap $E_\theta(x^-) - E_\theta(x^+)$ by making all energies larger in magnitude. In practice the difference between positive and negative energies stays meaningful while the values themselves drift to numerically unstable sizes ([Du and Mordatch, 2019](#references), Section 3.3).

**The L2 term.** Add a small multiple $\alpha \ll 1$ of the mean squared energy of both batches:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\theta) = {}& \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N \Big[E_\theta(x_i^+) - E_\theta(x_i^-)\Big]\\
&+ \frac{\alpha}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\Big[E_\theta(x_i^+)^2 + E_\theta(x_i^-)^2\Big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

It does two things. When the samples look like the data, both batches have the same distribution of energies, the CD term vanishes in expectation, and the penalty pulls the common level to zero, so data end up with energies near 0. When the samples do not look like the data, the penalty bounds the gap.

**Worked example.** Suppose the network could set one energy $E^+$ for all data and one energy $E^-$ for all samples. It would minimize $E^+ - E^- + \alpha\big((E^+)^2 + (E^-)^2\big)$. Setting both derivatives to zero gives $1 + 2\alpha E^+ = 0$ and $-1 + 2\alpha E^- = 0$, so $E^\pm = \mp 1/(2\alpha)$. With $\alpha = 0.1$ the energies stop at $\mp 5$, a gap of 10; with $\alpha = 1$ at $\mp 0.5$. Without the penalty there is no minimum: the loss decreases without bound as $E^+ \to -\infty$ and $E^- \to +\infty$.

The penalty is not part of the likelihood and shifts the optimum slightly, which is why $\alpha$ is kept small: 0.1 works for the MNIST configuration above, and Du and Mordatch use 1 on CIFAR-10. They combine it with spectral normalization of all layers, which bounds the Lipschitz constant of the energy and keeps its gradients from changing sharply ([WGAN note](/notes/generative-models/wgan#sn-gan-spectral-normalization)). Du and Mordatch also credit both with keeping $e^{-E_\theta}$ integrable (Section 3.3).

## Practical choices

- **Smooth activations.** The sampler follows $\nabla_x E_\theta$. For a ReLU network the energy is piecewise linear in $x$, and its input gradient jumps between linear regions. Smooth activations such as Swish, $x\,\sigma(x)$, keep the gradient field smooth; Du and Mordatch report that Swish noticeably improves stability (Appendix A.12).
- **No normalization layers.** Du and Mordatch found that batch, group, layer and other normalization layers hurt sampling, likely because they make a Langevin step depend on other data points (Appendix A.12).
- **No momentum.** The negative samples are regenerated from the current model at every step, so the loss surface moves with the parameters, and momentum keeps pushing in directions computed for an earlier surface. Adam with $\beta_1 = 0$ is the usual setting (Appendix A.11).
- **A little noise on the data.** The samples always carry sampler noise, while many data pixels are exactly at the edge of the range (the black background of MNIST). Adding comparable noise to the data, standard deviation 0.005 in the MNIST configuration and 0.03 in JEM ([Grathwohl et al., 2019](#references), Appendix A), keeps the energy from separating the two by such a trivial cue.
- **Watch samples, not the loss.** The CD term and the penalty settle near zero early and stay there, because the negative samples follow the model. Progress shows elsewhere: in images generated from noise every few epochs, in the contents of the buffer, which are what the model currently treats as negatives, and in the mean energy of uniform noise, which should keep rising.

## Instability

EBM training diverges easily ([Grathwohl et al., 2019](#references), Section 6 and Appendix H.3). The typical failure starts when the energy surface develops many sharp local minima. Chains from noise fall into the nearest one and stay noise-like. The negative phase keeps raising the energy where they sit and the positive phase keeps lowering it at the data, so the gap grows while the samples stop improving. The model then gives high probability to the training data, the sampler produces only noise, and the gradient field has become useless for sampling. A warning sign is negative samples whose energies are much larger than those of training data ([Du and Mordatch, 2019](#references), Appendix A.12).

Remedies, roughly from the cheapest:

- the L2 term and spectral normalization, which bound the energies and their gradients;
- a smaller learning rate, or more Langevin steps per update. Grathwohl et al. found these the only two changes that consistently stabilized a diverging model. At their scale, chains restarted from noise needed 80 steps per update for stable training, and even those models diverged late in training; persistent chains with 20 steps were four times faster but less stable (Appendices H.2 and H.3);
- restarting from an earlier checkpoint that had not yet diverged, possibly with a different random seed (Appendix H.3).

The most sensitive hyperparameters are those of the sampler: step size, number of steps and noise level, followed by the learning rate and the width of the network. Du and Mordatch found the step size the most sensitive, with a usable range of two to three orders of magnitude (Appendix A.12).

## Evaluating a model without its normalizer

Without $Z_\theta$ there is no likelihood to report. **Annealed importance sampling** ([Neal, 2001](#references)) estimates it by moving from a tractable distribution $p_0$ to $p_\theta$ through intermediate densities proportional to $p_0(x)^{1-\beta}\,e^{-\beta E_\theta(x)}$ for $\beta$ from 0 to 1, with MCMC steps at each stage and importance weights that multiply along the path; the average weight is an unbiased estimate of $Z_\theta/Z_0$. It works on small problems. [Du and Mordatch (2019)](#references) obtained lower and upper bounds on the likelihood of continuous MNIST this way, but on CIFAR-10 more than 300,000 chains run for two days still left a large gap between the bounds (Appendix A.3). Image EBMs are therefore judged by their samples, with [FID](/notes/generative-models/fid) for example, and by the tasks in the last sections.

## Noise-contrastive estimation: learning Z by classification

The two estimators in this and the next section need no samples from the model during training. **Noise-contrastive estimation** (NCE, [Gutmann and Hyvärinen, 2010](#references)) treats the log-normalizer as one more parameter. The model is

$$
\log p_m(x) = -E_\theta(x) + c ,
$$

where $c$ estimates $-\log Z_\theta$. Choose a **noise distribution** $p_n$ that is easy to sample and whose density can be evaluated, for example a Gaussian. Draw as many noise samples as data samples and train a logistic regression that tells them apart, with the logit fixed to the log-ratio of model and noise:

$$
\begin{gathered}
h(x) = \sigma\big(\log p_m(x) - \log p_n(x)\big) = \frac{p_m(x)}{p_m(x) + p_n(x)},\\
J(\theta, c) = \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{x\sim P}\log h(x)\\
+ \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{y\sim p_n}\log\big(1 - h(y)\big) .
\end{gathered}
$$

$J$ is the log-likelihood of the labels "data" and "noise" ([Gutmann and Hyvärinen, 2010](#references), Section 2.2). Why does a classifier learn a density? With equal numbers of data and noise samples, the true probability that a point $x$ is data is $p(x)/(p(x) + p_n(x)) = \sigma\big(\log p(x) - \log p_n(x)\big)$. The best classifier has $\log p_m = \log p$ exactly, normalization included. Gutmann and Hyvärinen show that $J$, as a function of $\log p_m$, has its maximum at $\log p$ and no other extremum, provided $p_n > 0$ wherever $p > 0$ (Theorem 1), and that the estimate of $(\theta, c)$ is consistent under conditions analogous to those of maximum likelihood (Theorem 2). The fitted $e^{-E_\theta + c}$ integrates to one without any constraint, and $c$ ends at $-\log Z_\theta$.

Maximum likelihood cannot do this: with $c$ free, the likelihood grows without bound as $c \to \infty$, since nothing forces $e^{-E_\theta + c}$ to integrate to one (Section 1). The classification objective does. A $c$ that is too large makes the classifier call noise "data" as well, and it pays for that on the noise samples.

**Worked example.** Data $\mathcal N(0, 1)$, noise $p_n = \mathcal N(0, 4)$, and an energy with the right shape, $E(x) = x^2/2$, so the only unknown is $c$. The correct value is $c^* = -\log\sqrt{2\pi} = -0.919$. At $x = 0$ the true probability of "data" is $0.399/(0.399 + 0.199) = 0.667$, and with $c^*$ the classifier gives $h(0) = \sigma(-0.919 + 1.612) = 0.667$. The unnormalized model, $c = 0$, gives $h(0) = \sigma(1.612) = 0.834$: too confident, so the logistic loss lowers $c$. Maximizing the population objective $J$ over $c$ numerically returns $-0.919$.

The noise distribution decides how much the model learns. It should be easy to sample, have a density that can be evaluated, and be close to the data. If the noise equals the data distribution, the asymptotic covariance of the estimate is twice the Cramér–Rao bound (Section 2.4). Far noise makes the task trivial: uniform noise is told apart from digits by a few crude features, such as smoothness or saturated background pixels, and the energy learns nothing finer. Better noise comes from a preliminary model of the data, for example a flow ([Song and Kingma, 2021](#references), Section 5). Seen from the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans), NCE is a discriminator trained against a fixed generator $p_n$, with its logit constrained to $\log p_m - \log p_n$; that constraint turns the discriminator into a density.

## Score matching: no samples and no Z

The score of an EBM does not involve $Z_\theta$: $\nabla_x \log p_\theta(x) = -\nabla_x E_\theta(x)$, as shown above. Fitting the model's score to the data's score, by minimizing the Fisher divergence $\tfrac12\,\mathbb E_P\big\|\nabla_x \log p(x) + \nabla_x E_\theta(x)\big\|^2$, therefore trains the energy with neither the normalizer nor samples ([Hyvärinen, 2005](#references)). The data score is unknown. The [score matching note](/notes/generative-models/score-matching) removes it by integration by parts and derives the cheaper [sliced and denoising versions](/notes/generative-models/score-matching#two-cheaper-objectives). For an energy, Hyvärinen's objective is $\mathbb E_P\big[\tfrac12\|\nabla_x E_\theta(x)\|^2 - \Delta_x E_\theta(x)\big]$ up to a constant, with $\Delta_x$ the Laplacian, the trace of the Hessian; denoising score matching regresses $-\nabla_x E_\theta(x + \sigma\varepsilon)$ onto $-\varepsilon/\sigma$. Either way the loss contains the gradient of the energy with respect to its input, so each training step differentiates twice, and generation still needs Langevin dynamics. The two sampling-free estimators are related: NCE whose noise is the data shifted by a small vector, with a slightly modified classifier, reduces to sliced score matching as the shift goes to zero ([Song and Kingma, 2021](#references), Section 5.1).

## Out-of-distribution detection

An energy trained with a negative phase is low on the data and has been pushed up wherever its own samples went. That makes $E_\theta(x)$ a natural detector of unusual inputs: flag $x$ as out of distribution when its energy exceeds a threshold chosen on held-out data. A classifier's confidence is a weak substitute, since modern classifiers tend to be overconfident ([Grathwohl et al., 2019](#references), Section 5.2). Four caveats apply.

- **High density is not typicality.** Flows, VAEs and PixelCNNs trained on CIFAR-10 assign higher likelihood to SVHN house numbers than to CIFAR-10 test images ([Nalisnick et al., 2019](#references)). A likelihood model is not automatically an outlier detector.
- **EBMs do better, not well.** In the AUROC of separating CIFAR-10 test images from other data (0.5 is chance), an EBM scores 0.63 against SVHN, where PixelCNN++ scores 0.32 and Glow 0.24; averaged over five kinds of outliers, 0.62 against 0.47 and 0.42. The same EBM scores 0.30 on constant images and 0.48 on textures, below chance ([Du and Mordatch, 2019](#references), Section 4.4 and Figure 10).
- **Only what the sampler visited was pushed up.** Uniform noise, where the chains start, gets a high energy. Changes the sampler never produced, such as a rotated or shrunken digit, may raise the energy only slightly.
- **Energies have no common scale.** The threshold must be calibrated for each model; the L2 term keeps data energies near zero, which helps choose it.

[Grathwohl et al. (2019)](#references) propose a different score for EBMs, the norm of the input gradient $\|\nabla_x \log p_\theta(x)\|$: a typical input lies where the density changes slowly, so a large gradient flags a point of high density but low mass (Section 5.3.3).

## JEM: your classifier is secretly an EBM

A classifier with $C$ classes outputs logits $f_\theta(x) \in \mathbb R^C$ and class probabilities $p_\theta(y \mid x) = \operatorname{softmax}(f_\theta(x))_y$. [Grathwohl et al. (2019)](#references) read the same logits as the negative energy of the pair $(x, y)$:

$$
p_\theta(x, y) = \frac{e^{f_\theta(x)[y]}}{Z_\theta}, \qquad E_\theta(x, y) = -f_\theta(x)[y] .
$$

Summing over $y$ gives a density of the input alone,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_\theta(x) &= \frac{1}{Z_\theta}\sum_y e^{f_\theta(x)[y]},\\
E_\theta(x) &= -\log\sum_y e^{f_\theta(x)[y]}\\
&= -\operatorname{logsumexp}_y f_\theta(x)[y] ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and dividing the joint by the marginal cancels $Z_\theta$ and returns the softmax: $p_\theta(y \mid x)$ is the classifier as before. Adding the same constant to all logits of an input leaves the classifier unchanged but changes $p_\theta(x)$. This degree of freedom, unused by ordinary training, is where the density lives; the model is called a **joint energy-based model**, JEM.

**Worked example.** Logits $(2, 0, -1)$ give class probabilities $(0.844, 0.114, 0.042)$, $\operatorname{logsumexp} = 2.170$ and $E(x) = -2.170$. Logits $(3, 1, 0)$ give the same probabilities and $E(x) = -3.170$. The classifier cannot tell the two inputs apart; under $p_\theta(x)$ the second is $e \approx 2.72$ times more probable.

JEM trains with $\log p_\theta(x, y) = \log p_\theta(x) + \log p_\theta(y \mid x)$: cross-entropy for the classifier term, which is exact, plus the EBM gradient for $\log p_\theta(x)$ with energy $-\operatorname{logsumexp}$, its negative samples drawn by Langevin steps from a replay buffer of 10,000 images, 20 steps per update, 5% of the chains restarted from uniform noise, noise of standard deviation 0.01 (Section 4 and Appendix A). A WideResNet-28-10 without batch normalization trained this way on CIFAR-10 reaches 92.9% accuracy together with an Inception Score of 8.76 and an FID of 38.4 (Table 1). The joint training also improves calibration, out-of-distribution detection and adversarial robustness over the same classifier trained alone (Section 5). The cost is the EBM's: each Langevin step costs about as much as a classifier training step, and the models regularly diverged and had to be restarted with lower learning rates or stronger regularization (Sections 6 and 7).

## Composing energies

Adding energies multiplies densities:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{A+B}(x) &= E_A(x) + E_B(x)\\
\Longrightarrow\quad p_{A+B}(x) &\propto e^{-E_A(x)}\,e^{-E_B(x)} \propto p_A(x)\,p_B(x) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

a **product of experts** ([Hinton, 2002](#references)). The product is large only where both factors are, so a sum of energies acts as a logical AND of the constraints the energies encode. The score of the sum is the sum of the scores, so Langevin dynamics on the composed model needs only the gradients of the parts and no new training.

**Worked example.** $E_A(x) = x^2/2$ is $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ and $E_B(x) = (x - 2)^2/2$ is $\mathcal N(2, 1)$. Their sum is $x^2 - 2x + 2 = (x - 1)^2 + 1$, the energy of $\mathcal N(1, \tfrac12)$: the product sits between the two and is narrower than either.

[Du and Mordatch (2019)](#references) train separate conditional energies for the scale, position and rotation of a shape and generate images with all three attributes by Langevin dynamics on the sum; summed energies, fine-tuned on the training sets, also produce combinations of size and position never seen together in training (Section 7). The parts must be on comparable scales. Separately trained energies are measured in arbitrary units ([LeCun et al., 2006](#references), Section 1.3), and a weight on one term, equivalently a temperature, changes the result.

## What changed

| | WGAN critic | EBM, MCMC training | NCE | Score matching |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Network output | critic score $f(x)$ | energy $E_\theta(x)$ | energy and a constant $c$ | energy; its input gradient is the score |
| Negative examples | from a generator network | Langevin chains on $E_\theta$, often from a buffer | samples of a fixed noise density | none |
| Gradient through the sampler | yes, the generator is trained | none | none | no sampler |
| Normalizer $Z_\theta$ | not defined | never computed | learned as $-c$ | cancels |
| What keeps it in check | Lipschitz constraint | L2 on energies, spectral normalization | noise close to the data | second derivatives or a noise level |
| Sampling after training | one generator pass | Langevin dynamics | Langevin dynamics | Langevin dynamics |
| Typical failure | critic not Lipschitz | divergence; density shaped by the sampler | trivial classification against far noise | cost of the Hessian trace; score of noised data |

## Limitations

- **Sampling inside training.** Every update runs $K$ forward and backward passes of the network for the sampler, 60 in the MNIST configuration, so training is tens of times slower than training a classifier of the same size ([Grathwohl et al., 2019](#references), Section 7).
- **Biased gradients.** Short chains, and the relaxed sampler at a temperature near $10^{-6}$, do not sample $p_\theta$. The learned density is shaped by the sampler, as the Gaussian example shows, and is not a calibrated likelihood.
- **Instability.** Training diverges without the penalty, spectral normalization and careful tuning of the sampler, and sometimes despite them.
- **No likelihood for free.** Reporting $\log p_\theta(x)$ needs $Z_\theta$, and annealed importance sampling is too slow for images of CIFAR-10 size.
- **Slow generation.** Hundreds of Langevin steps per sample, and long chains explore modes slowly; Du and Mordatch found mode exploration with Langevin dynamics "took a very long time" for unconditional CIFAR-10 (Section 4.1).
- **The sampling-free estimators have their own costs.** NCE depends on a noise distribution close to the data, which is hard to find for images; score matching needs second derivatives or a noise level, and sampling still needs Langevin dynamics.

## Summary

- An energy network defines $p_\theta(x) = e^{-E_\theta(x)}/Z_\theta$. Only energy differences matter; $Z_\theta$ is an intractable integral.
- The likelihood gradient is $\mathbb E_P\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x^+) - \mathbb E_{p_\theta}\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x^-)$: lower the energy at the data, raise it at the model's samples. The negative phase needs samples from the model, which come from Langevin dynamics on $-\nabla_x E_\theta$.
- Contrastive divergence stops the chains after $K$ steps. Short chains bias the model: it learns an energy whose $K$-step sampler reproduces the data, not the data density. Persistent chains and a replay buffer (95% old samples, 5% fresh noise) lengthen the chains at no extra cost.
- The loss sees only energy differences, so the level and scale of the energies drift; an L2 penalty $\alpha\,\text{mean}(E^2)$ pins the level near zero and caps the gap at about $1/\alpha$.
- Training is unstable; smooth activations, no normalization layers, Adam without momentum, small learning rates and more sampling steps help.
- NCE learns $E_\theta$ and $\log Z_\theta$ by classifying data against noise; score matching fits $-\nabla_x E_\theta$ to the data score. Neither samples during training.
- Energies detect outliers (with caveats), any classifier is an EBM with $E(x) = -\operatorname{logsumexp}_y f_\theta(x)[y]$ (JEM), and adding energies multiplies densities.

Notebook issues:

- **The sampler is not Langevin dynamics.** The markdown of cells 3 and 13 writes the update as $x - \eta\nabla_x E + \omega$, $\omega \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma)$, without relating $\sigma$ to $\eta$, and states that small $\eta$ and large $K$ give samples from the model. That holds only when the noise variance is $2\eta$, that is, for $x - \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla_x E + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,z$. With step 10 and noise 0.005 the chain targets $p_\theta^{1/T}$ with $T = 1.25 \cdot 10^{-6}$, as explained in the callout above.
- **Contrastive divergence or not.** Cell 3 calls the method contrastive divergence. Hinton's CD starts the chains at the data; the notebook starts them from a buffer and noise, which is persistent CD with a replay buffer ([Du and Mordatch, 2019](#references)).
- **NCE noise.** Cell 3 suggests replay buffers as a noise source for NCE. NCE needs the log-density of the noise, and a buffer of MCMC samples has none.
- **Denoising score matching** (cell 3) is described as predicting the clean image from the noisy one; the objective regresses the model score onto $-(\tilde x - x)/\sigma^2$, which is equivalent to predicting the noise, or the clean image, up to a known affine map.
- **The L2 formula** in cell 16 places the sum over $i$ on the first square only; it should cover both, $\frac1N\sum_i\big[E_\theta(x_i^+)^2 + E_\theta(x_i^-)^2\big]$, which is what the code computes.
- **Callbacks never run with current Lightning.** The three callbacks override `on_epoch_end`, a hook that PyTorch Lightning 2.x no longer has, so nothing is generated or logged during training; the hook to use is `on_train_epoch_end`.
- **`make_grid(..., range=(-1, 1))`** in cells 21, 23, 38 and 45 uses an argument that current torchvision has replaced by `value_range`; the call raises a `TypeError`, so the plots of cell 38 and the comparisons of cells 49, 51 and 53 fail until it is renamed.
- **The "flip" is a rotation.** `flip(dims=(1, 2))` on a $1 \times 28 \times 28$ image reverses both height and width, a rotation by 180°; a mirror image is `flip(dims=(2,))`.
- **Comments that do not match the code.** Cell 9 promises a split of the training set into training and validation parts and smaller batches for training; there is no split, the test set is the validation set, and training uses the same loader. Cell 13 mentions uniform or Gaussian starts; the code uses uniform noise only. Cell 7 repeats the section number 2.5.

## Next

Training an energy-based model runs biased short MCMC chains inside every parameter update, and the model it produces is shaped as much by its sampler as by its data. Diffusion models avoid this loop. They fix a noising process that turns data into Gaussian noise and learn to undo it one small step at a time, which is a regression with no sampling during training. The [next note](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process) builds that forward process. The [score matching note](/notes/generative-models/score-matching) shows later that the network a diffusion model trains is, up to a known scale factor, the score, the negative gradient of an energy, of the noised data.
