The [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans) ended with a problem. With an optimal discriminator the generator minimizes $2\,\mathrm{JS}(P\,\|\,Q) - 2\ln 2$, and when the real distribution $P$ and the generator's distribution $Q$ have disjoint supports, $\mathrm{JS} = \ln 2$ wherever $Q$ sits. The objective is flat and the generator gets no gradient. The **Wasserstein GAN** ([Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017](#references)) replaces JS with the **Wasserstein-1 distance**, which keeps decreasing as $Q$ moves towards $P$ even when they do not overlap. The price is a constraint on the discriminator: it must be 1-Lipschitz. The rest of the note is about the losses and the two standard ways to meet that constraint.

**Notation.** $P$ is the real data distribution, $Q$ the generator's distribution ($x = G(z)$ with $z \sim p(z)$), and $f$ the **critic**: the WGAN name for the discriminator, a network with a real-valued output and no sigmoid. Real samples are $x_r \sim P$ and fake ones $x_f = G(z)$. $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the Euclidean norm.

## Wasserstein-1 distance

Think of $P$ and $Q$ as two piles of earth of equal mass. A transport plan $\gamma(x, y)$ says how much mass moves from $x$ to $y$; its marginals must be $P$ and $Q$. Moving mass $m$ over a distance $\|x - y\|$ costs $m\,\|x - y\|$, and $W_1$, also called the **earth mover's distance**, is the cheapest total cost:

$$
W_1(P, Q) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(P, Q)} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y)\sim\gamma}\,\|x - y\| .
$$

A network cannot optimize directly over all transport plans. The **Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality** ([Villani, 2009](#references), Remark 6.5) rewrites the infimum as a supremum over functions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_1(P, Q) = \sup_{\|f\|_L \le 1}\; \Big[\, &\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\,f(x)\\
&- \mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\,f(x) \Big],
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\|f\|_L \le 1$ ($f$ is 1-Lipschitz) means $|f(x) - f(y)| \le \|x - y\|$ for all $x, y$. This is where the critic and its constraint come from: the critic $f$ plays the role of the function inside the supremum. It scores real samples high and fake ones low, but it may not change faster than distance. Without the constraint, and with $P \ne Q$, the supremum would be $+\infty$, since any separating $f$ could be scaled up.

**Why this helps.** Take two point masses, $P = \delta_0$ (the data) and $Q_\theta = \delta_\theta$ (the model). For every $\theta \ne 0$ the supports are disjoint, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{JS}(P\,\|\,Q_\theta) &= \tfrac12\ln\frac{1}{1/2} + \tfrac12\ln\frac{1}{1/2}\\
&= \ln 2 \approx 0.693,\\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q_\theta) &= \infty,\\
W_1(P, Q_\theta) &= |\theta| .
\end{aligned}
$$

The only plan moves all the mass a distance $|\theta|$. JS is constant in $\theta$, so its derivative is $0$; $W_1$ has slope $\pm 1$ and always points the model towards the data ([Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017](#references), Example 1). More generally, if the generator is continuous in its parameters, so is $W_1$, while JS and KL can jump.

## WGAN losses

The critic maximizes the dual objective, so its loss is the negative of it. The generator only appears in the second expectation and wants the critic's score on fakes to be high:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_D &= -\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)}\big[f(G(z))\big],\\
\mathcal L_G &= -\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)}\big[f(G(z))\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

At the optimal critic, $-\mathcal L_D$ estimates $W_1(P, Q)$, or $K\,W_1$ if the critic family is $K$-Lipschitz rather than 1-Lipschitz. Unlike a GAN discriminator, the critic should be trained close to optimality: a better critic gives a better estimate of the distance and a better gradient, not a saturated one. It is therefore updated several times per generator step, typically $n_{\text{critic}} = 5$. A useful side effect is that the critic loss tracks sample quality during training, which the GAN loss does not.

The losses alone do not keep $f$ Lipschitz. The original WGAN clipped every critic weight to $[-c, c]$ after each step. Its authors themselves called this "a clearly terrible way" to enforce the constraint, and WGAN-GP showed that it biases the critic towards overly simple functions and makes training sensitive to $c$ ([Gulrajani et al., 2017](#references)). The two methods below replace clipping.

## WGAN-GP: gradient penalty

WGAN-GP ([Gulrajani et al., 2017](#references)) keeps the WGAN losses and adds a penalty to the critic:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_D = {}& \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p(z)}\big[f(G(z))\big] - \mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big]\\
&+ \lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{\hat x}\Big[\big(\|\nabla_{\hat x} f(\hat x)\|_2 - 1\big)^2\Big].
\end{aligned}
$$

The first two terms are the WGAN critic loss written in the other order, and the generator loss $\mathcal L_G$ is unchanged.

**Why the gradient norm.** For a differentiable $f$, being 1-Lipschitz is the same as $\|\nabla f(x)\|_2 \le 1$ everywhere. Pushing $\|\nabla_{\hat x} f(\hat x)\|_2$ towards $1$ therefore keeps $f$ approximately 1-Lipschitz. The target is exactly $1$ rather than "at most 1" because the optimal critic has gradient norm $1$ almost everywhere along the straight lines between coupled real and fake points ([Gulrajani et al., 2017](#references), Proposition 1), so a two-sided penalty does not exclude it. The constraint is soft: it is only checked at the sampled points $\hat x$.

**Where $\hat x$ comes from.** Pair each real sample with a fake one, draw $\alpha \sim U(0, 1)$ and take a random point on the segment between them:

$$
\hat x = \alpha\,x_r + (1 - \alpha)\,x_f .
$$

These segments are where the proposition says the gradient norm should be $1$. In code, $\alpha$ is drawn once per image and broadcast over all pixels. The gradient of the critic's output with respect to $\hat x$ is computed with `torch.autograd.grad(..., create_graph=True)`, so that the penalty itself can be backpropagated, then flattened per sample, and its $L_2$ norm enters $(\|\cdot\| - 1)^2$. The usual coefficient is $\lambda = 10$.

## SN-GAN: spectral normalization

The second approach constrains the weights instead of the gradients ([Miyato et al., 2018](#references)). Each layer's weight matrix is divided by its **spectral norm**, the largest singular value:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\hat W = \frac{W}{\sigma_{\max}(W)},\\
\sigma_{\max}(W) = \max_{h \ne 0}\frac{\|W h\|_2}{\|h\|_2} .
\end{gathered}
$$

**What a singular value is.** A linear map stretches space. It sends the unit circle to an ellipse, and the singular values say how much it stretches in each direction: the right singular vectors $v_i$ are mapped to $\sigma_i u_i$, the semi-axes of the ellipse. The largest one, $\sigma_{\max}$, is the most any vector can be stretched, so it is the Lipschitz constant of $h \mapsto W h$. After dividing by it, no direction is stretched by more than $1$.

<SpectralNorm />

For a network that alternates linear layers and 1-Lipschitz activations (ReLU, LeakyReLU), Lipschitz constants multiply under composition, so

$$
\|f\|_L \le \prod_{l} \sigma_{\max}(W_l),
$$

which is at most $1$ after normalization.

Unlike the gradient penalty, the constraint is imposed on the weights rather than checked at sampled points, and there is no $\lambda$ to tune. In PyTorch it is a wrapper, `torch.nn.utils.spectral_norm(nn.Conv2d(...))`, which estimates $\sigma_{\max}$ with one step of power iteration per forward pass instead of a full SVD. In practice the bound is approximate, for three reasons. Power iteration only estimates $\sigma_{\max}$. For a convolution the wrapper normalizes the kernel reshaped into a matrix, which is a proxy for the convolution's operator norm, not equal to it ([Gouk et al., 2021](#references)). And normalization layers placed between the convolutions are not 1-Lipschitz in general. Spectral normalization therefore keeps the critic's Lipschitz constant under control rather than guaranteeing that it is at most $1$.

## Two dual views

Both f-GAN, from the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#f-gan), and WGAN train the discriminator as the function inside a supremum. f-GAN uses the convex-conjugate bound on an f-divergence, which compares density *ratios* and therefore inherits the support problem of JS and KL. WGAN uses the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual of a transport cost, which compares *locations* and stays informative when supports are disjoint. The Lipschitz constraint is the price of the second view: it is what ties the critic to the geometry of the data space.

The [WGAN notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/42dd64b535f5bedf1d2b3db28f2f21ce96c9f09f/seminars/03_WGAN/WGAN.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* repository (pinned commit; cell numbers count every cell from 1) trains all three variants on CelebA at $64 \times 64$.

- **Cells 4–5**: the earth mover's distance and the WGAN objective with a real-valued critic.

The baseline applies **no** Lipschitz constraint, neither clipping nor penalty, as a teaching contrast for the next two sections.
- **Cells 17–21: WGAN-GP.** `gradient_penalty` draws $\alpha \sim U(0,1)$ per sample, interpolates, differentiates with `create_graph=True` and penalizes the per-sample $L_2$ norm; `lambda_gp = 10`.
- **Cells 22–27: spectral normalization.** `DiscriminatorSN` wraps every convolution in `spectral_norm`; in its inner blocks the normalized convolution is followed by `InstanceNorm2d(affine=True)` and LeakyReLU, so the bound above holds only approximately.
