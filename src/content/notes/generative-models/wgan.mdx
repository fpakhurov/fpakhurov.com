Put all the data at one point, $x = 0$, and let the generator put all its samples at $x = \theta$. For $\theta = 5$ and $\theta = 0.01$ the Jensen–Shannon divergence is the same, $\ln 2$, because the two distributions do not overlap in either case. A generator trained on JS cannot tell that $\theta = 0.01$ is almost right, and, as the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#step-2-substitute-the-optimal-discriminator-and-recognize-js) showed, a near-optimal discriminator gives it no gradient. Real generators sit on low-dimensional sets in image space and meet this situation all the time.

What we want is a distance that measures *how far* the generated samples are from the data, and keeps shrinking as they approach. The **Wasserstein GAN** uses the Wasserstein-1 distance ([Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017](#references)). It brings a new demand: the discriminator, now called the **critic**, must not change faster than distance.

## Wasserstein-1 distance

Think of the data distribution $P$ and the generator's distribution $Q$ as two piles of earth of equal mass. A **transport plan** $\gamma(x, y)$ says how much mass moves from $x$ to $y$; its marginals must be $P$ and $Q$, and $\Pi(P, Q)$ is the set of all such plans ([Peyré and Cuturi, 2019](#references)). Moving mass $m$ a distance $\lVert x - y\rVert$ costs $m\lVert x - y\rVert$. The **Wasserstein-1 distance**, or earth mover's distance, is the cheapest total cost:

$$
W_1(P, Q) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(P, Q)} \mathbb E_{(x, y)\sim\gamma}\,\lVert x - y\rVert .
$$

For the two point masses of the opening there is one plan, moving everything a distance $\lvert\theta\rvert$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(\delta_0 \,\|\, \delta_\theta) &= \tfrac12\ln\frac{1}{1/2} + \tfrac12\ln\frac{1}{1/2} = \ln 2, \\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\delta_0 \,\|\, \delta_\theta) &= \infty, \\
W_1(\delta_0, \delta_\theta) &= \lvert\theta\rvert .
\end{aligned}
$$

The JS line uses the mixture $M = \tfrac12(\delta_0 + \delta_\theta)$, which has mass $\tfrac12$ on each atom. JS is flat in $\theta$; $W_1$ has slope $\pm 1$ and always points the model toward the data. Arjovsky et al. use two parallel line segments for the same point.

<JsVsW1 />

The example generalizes. Write $Q_\theta$ for the distribution of $G_\theta(z)$. If $G_\theta$ is a feedforward network and the prior has $\mathbb E\lVert z\rVert < \infty$, then $W_1(P, Q_\theta)$ is continuous in $\theta$ and differentiable almost everywhere; JS and KL can jump ([Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017](#references), Theorem 1 and Corollary 1). On a compact space, a sequence of distributions that converges in KL or in JS also converges in $W_1$, but not the other way round (Theorem 2); the point masses above, with $\theta \to 0$, have $W_1 = \lvert\theta\rvert \to 0$ while JS stays at $\ln 2$: $W_1$ can see progress that the others miss.

### The dual form

No network can search over transport plans. The **Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality** turns the infimum over plans into a supremum over functions. For probability distributions on a compact set, with the distance as the cost of moving,

$$
W_1(P, Q) = \sup_{\lVert f\rVert_L \le 1} \Big[\mathbb E_{x\sim P} f(x) - \mathbb E_{x\sim Q} f(x)\Big],
$$

where $\lVert f\rVert_L \le 1$ means $f$ is **1-Lipschitz**: $\lvert f(x) - f(y)\rvert \le \lVert x - y\rVert$ for all $x, y$. The function $f$ scores real samples high and fakes low, like a discriminator, but its score may not change faster than distance. Without that constraint and with $P \ne Q$, any separating $f$ could be scaled up and the supremum would be $+\infty$. The same formula holds on $\mathbb R^d$ for distributions with finite first moments, with the Lipschitz functions normalized at one point ([Peyré, 2025](#references), Section 6.5).

One direction is three lines. Take any plan $\gamma \in \Pi(P, Q)$ and any 1-Lipschitz $f$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_P f - \mathbb E_Q f &= \mathbb E_{(x, y)\sim\gamma}\big[f(x) - f(y)\big] \\
&\le \mathbb E_{(x, y)\sim\gamma}\,\lVert x - y\rVert .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line holds because the marginals of $\gamma$ are $P$ and $Q$, the second because $f$ is 1-Lipschitz. The supremum over $f$ on the left and the infimum over $\gamma$ on the right give $\sup \le W_1$. Read $f(x)$ as a price of earth at $x$: a price list that never differs by more than the distance cannot charge more than the cheapest plan. That the best price list matches the plan exactly is the theorem, proved for compact spaces in [Peyré (2025)](#references), Proposition 5.5 (Kantorovich duality) with Proposition 6.17 and Eq. (6.23), which specialize it to the cost $\lVert x - y\rVert$.

For the point masses, $f(x) = -\lvert x\rvert$ is 1-Lipschitz and gives $f(0) - f(\theta) = \lvert\theta\rvert$, so it attains the supremum. Its slope at the fake sample is $\pm 1$ whatever $\theta$ is. The optimal GAN discriminator, by contrast, is 1 at 0 and 0 at $\theta$, with nothing in between to follow.

## WGAN losses

The critic $f$ is a network with a real-valued output and no sigmoid. It maximizes the dual objective; the generator appears only in the second expectation and wants the critic's score on fakes to be high:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_D &= -\mathbb E_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big] + \mathbb E_{z\sim p_z}\big[f(G(z))\big], \\
\mathcal L_G &= -\mathbb E_{z\sim p_z}\big[f(G(z))\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

What does $-\mathcal L_D$ measure? If the critic family is $K$-Lipschitz, even its best member gives $-\mathcal L_D \le K\,W_1(P, Q)$, with equality only when the family contains $K$ times an optimal 1-Lipschitz critic; in training, $-\mathcal L_D$ is a minibatch estimate of it. Unlike a GAN discriminator, the critic should be trained close to optimality, and Theorem 3 of [Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou (2017)](#references) says why. On a compact space, with $G_\theta$ locally Lipschitz in $(\theta, z)$ with constants of finite mean over $z$, an optimal 1-Lipschitz critic $f^*$ exists and $\nabla_\theta W_1(P, Q_\theta) = -\mathbb E_z\big[\nabla_\theta f^*(G_\theta(z))\big]$ wherever both sides are defined. The generator's gradient, $\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_G$, is the gradient of $W_1$ when the critic is optimal: the derivative of a maximum is the partial derivative at the maximizer, an envelope argument. A better GAN discriminator, by contrast, saturates. The original algorithm takes $n_{\text{critic}} = 5$ critic steps per generator step.

With a constrained critic trained near its optimum, $-\mathcal L_D$ falls as samples improve during a run, which the GAN discriminator's loss does not do. $K$ depends on the critic's architecture, though, so the number cannot be compared between models; [FID](/notes/generative-models/fid) remains the metric for that.

### Where it breaks: clipping the weights

The losses alone do not keep $f$ Lipschitz. The original WGAN clipped every critic weight to $[-c, c]$, with $c = 0.01$, after each step. Bounded weights give a $K$-Lipschitz critic when it is built from linear layers and 1-Lipschitz activations. A layer's matrix $W$ of size $m \times n$ with entries in $[-c, c]$ has rows $w_i$ with $\lVert w_i\rVert^2 \le nc^2$, so by Cauchy–Schwarz on each row $\lVert Wh\rVert^2 = \sum_i (w_i^\top h)^2 \le mnc^2\lVert h\rVert^2$. A convolution on images of fixed size is such a matrix too, with kernel weights or zeros as entries. Biases do not change a Lipschitz constant, and the product rule [below](#sn-gan-spectral-normalization) gives $K \le \prod_l c\sqrt{m_l n_l}$, so the bound above holds. Its authors called clipping "a clearly terrible way" to enforce the constraint ([Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017](#references)): a large $c$ makes the critic slow to reach its optimum, a small $c$ makes gradients vanish.

The WGAN-GP paper worked out what goes wrong ([Gulrajani et al., 2017](#references)). Clipping pushes the weights toward the two ends of $[-c, c]$, and a clipped critic trained to optimality learns very simple functions that ignore all but the coarsest structure of the data. And in a 12-layer critic the gradient norm grew or decayed exponentially from layer to layer for each $c$ in $\{10^{-1}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-3}\}$: without careful tuning it explodes or vanishes. The two methods below replace clipping.

## WGAN-GP: gradient penalty

For a differentiable $f$, being 1-Lipschitz is the same as $\lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert_2 \le 1$ everywhere: one direction follows from the definition of the derivative, the other from the mean value theorem along a segment. So constrain the gradient directly. WGAN-GP keeps the WGAN losses and adds a penalty to the critic ([Gulrajani et al., 2017](#references)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_D ={}& \mathbb E_{z}\big[f(G(z))\big] - \mathbb E_{x\sim P}\big[f(x)\big] \\
&+ \lambda\,\mathbb E_{\hat x}\Big[\big(\lVert\nabla_{\hat x} f(\hat x)\rVert_2 - 1\big)^2\Big],
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\lambda = 10$ in all the paper's experiments. The generator's loss is unchanged.

**Why two-sided.** The literal constraint would penalize only norms above 1, $\max(0, \lVert\nabla f\rVert_2 - 1)^2$. The penalty pushes the norm *toward* 1 from both sides, and this does not exclude the optimal critic. If the space is compact, the optimal critic is differentiable and the optimal plan moves every point, it has gradient norm 1 along the segments between optimally coupled points, and almost everywhere under $P$ and $Q$ ([Gulrajani et al., 2017](#references), Proposition 1). For the point masses above, $f = -\lvert x\rvert$ has $\lvert f'\rvert = 1$ everywhere except at 0.

**Where $\hat x$ comes from.** The proposition is about segments between coupled points, and the optimal coupling is unknown. WGAN-GP pairs each real sample with a random fake, draws $\alpha \sim U(0, 1)$ and takes the point

$$
\hat x = \alpha\,x_r + (1 - \alpha)\,x_f
$$

on the segment between them.

This is a heuristic: enforcing the norm everywhere is intractable, and enforcing it on these segments works. In code, $\alpha$ is drawn once per image and broadcast over its pixels. The gradient of the critic at $\hat x$ is computed with `torch.autograd.grad(..., create_graph=True)`, so that the penalty itself can be back-propagated, and flattened per image before taking the norm.

<GradientPenalty />

**No batch normalization in the critic.** The penalty treats the critic as a function of one image. With batch normalization each output depends on the whole minibatch, and the per-input gradient no longer describes the function being constrained. WGAN-GP therefore drops batch normalization from the critic and recommends layer normalization. [Instance normalization](/notes/generative-models/training-background#instance-normalization), which normalizes each image's channels separately, also qualifies.

With the penalty, $-\mathcal L_D$ is the score difference minus $\lambda$ times the penalty. Log the difference $\mathbb E_P f - \mathbb E_Q f$ and the penalty separately. The constraint is soft, so the difference is only approximately a lower bound on $W_1$.

## SN-GAN: spectral normalization

The second approach constrains the weights instead of the gradients ([Miyato et al., 2018](#references)). Divide each layer's weight matrix by its **spectral norm**, its largest singular value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat W &= \frac{W}{\sigma_{\max}(W)}, \\
\sigma_{\max}(W) &= \max_{h \ne 0}\frac{\lVert Wh\rVert_2}{\lVert h\rVert_2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

A linear map sends the unit circle to an ellipse. The [singular values](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) are its semi-axes, and the largest one is the most any vector can be stretched: the Lipschitz constant of $h \mapsto Wh$. After the division no direction is stretched by more than 1.

<SpectralNorm />

Activations such as ReLU and LeakyReLU are 1-Lipschitz, and Lipschitz constants multiply under composition: $\lvert f(g(x)) - f(g(y))\rvert \le L_f\lVert g(x) - g(y)\rVert \le L_f L_g\lVert x - y\rVert$. For a network alternating linear layers and such activations,

$$
\lVert f\rVert_L \le \prod_{l} \sigma_{\max}(W_l),
$$

which is at most 1 after normalization. Unlike the penalty, the constraint holds at every input, and there is no $\lambda$ to tune.

**Computing $\sigma_{\max}$.** A full SVD per step would be expensive. Spectral normalization runs one step of the [power method](/notes/math/iterative-methods#the-power-method) per forward pass:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u &\leftarrow \frac{Wv}{\lVert Wv\rVert_2}, \\
v &\leftarrow \frac{W^\top u}{\lVert W^\top u\rVert_2}, \\
\sigma_{\max} &\approx u^\top W v .
\end{aligned}
$$

For the matrix of the figure, $W = \begin{pmatrix} 1.3 & 0.7 \\ 0.3 & 0.9 \end{pmatrix}$ with $\sigma_{\max} = 1.657$, starting from $v = (1, 0)$:

| Step | Estimate of $\sigma_{\max}$ |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 1.601 |
| 2 | 1.656 |
| 3 | 1.657 |

The gap $\sigma_2/\sigma_1 = 0.35$ is wide, so two steps already land within $0.001$. A network's weights change little between training steps, and the vectors $u$, $v$ are kept from one step to the next, so one step per forward pass keeps the estimate close.

The bound is approximate in practice. Power iteration only estimates $\sigma_{\max}$, and from below, since $\lvert u^\top W v\rvert \le \lVert u\rVert\,\lVert Wv\rVert \le \sigma_{\max}$ for unit $u$, $v$ (the table's 1.601 and 1.656 lie below 1.657); dividing by an underestimate leaves the layer stretching some direction by slightly more than 1. For a convolution, the kernel is reshaped into a matrix and normalized, which is a proxy for the convolution's operator norm; the true norm also depends on stride and padding ([Gouk et al., 2021](#references)). And the product bound need not be tight. With exact norms the network would be $K$-Lipschitz with $K \le 1$, often well below 1; with the estimates $K$ is close to that but not guaranteed to be at most 1. A WGAN critic with spectral normalization therefore estimates roughly $K\,W_1$.

Miyato et al. trained their SN-GANs with the standard GAN loss and the **hinge** loss, with WGAN-GP as a baseline. Spectral normalization is a Lipschitz constraint and works with any of these losses. In PyTorch it is a wrapper, `torch.nn.utils.parametrizations.spectral_norm(nn.Conv2d(...))`; the [scaling note](/notes/generative-models/gan-scaling#sagan-self-attention-for-long-range-structure) applies it to both networks.

## What changed

The f-GAN of the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#f-gan) and the WGAN both train the discriminator as the function inside a supremum. f-GAN's supremum bounds an f-divergence, which compares density *ratios* and inherits the support problem of JS and KL. WGAN's supremum is the dual of a transport cost, which compares *locations* and stays informative when supports are disjoint. The price of the second view is the Lipschitz constraint, which ties the critic to the geometry of the data space.

## What you should remember

- $W_1$ is the cheapest cost of moving $P$ onto $Q$; for point masses at 0 and $\theta$ it is $\lvert\theta\rvert$, while JS stays at $\ln 2$.
- Dual: $W_1 = \sup_{\lVert f\rVert_L \le 1} \mathbb E_P f - \mathbb E_Q f$; the critic plays $f$ and is trained close to optimal.
- Weight clipping enforces the constraint badly: simple critics, vanishing or exploding gradients.
- WGAN-GP: $\lambda(\lVert\nabla f(\hat x)\rVert_2 - 1)^2$ on random real–fake interpolates, $\lambda = 10$, no batch norm in the critic.
- Spectral normalization: $W/\sigma_{\max}(W)$ per layer, one power-method step per forward pass.
