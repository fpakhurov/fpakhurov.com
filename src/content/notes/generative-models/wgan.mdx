**Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)** changes the quantity that the adversarial game estimates. A real-valued **critic** compares real and generated samples through the Wasserstein-1 distance. The handwritten notes then introduce two ways to control the critic: gradient penalty and spectral normalization.

## Distance as transport cost

Let $P_r$ denote the real distribution and $P_g$ the distribution of $G_\theta(z)$, with $z\sim p_z$. The Wasserstein-1 distance is

$$
W_1(P_r,P_g)
=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(P_r,P_g)}
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\gamma}\left[\|x-y\|_2\right].
$$

A **coupling** $\gamma$ describes a transport plan whose first marginal is $P_r$ and second marginal is $P_g$. The cost is the expected distance that probability mass travels; $W_1$ chooses the cheapest plan. Assume the distributions have finite first moments so that this distance is finite.

For two point masses, $P_r=\delta_0$ and $P_g=\delta_t$, the only plan moves all the mass from $0$ to $t$, giving $W_1(P_r,P_g)=|t|$. With the symmetric JS convention from [Entropy and divergences](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences), using natural logarithms, their JS divergence is $\log 2$ whenever $t\ne0$, and zero at $t=0$.

This example separates **how far apart** the supports are from **whether** they overlap. It adapts the motivation in the [original WGAN paper, Section 2](https://arxiv.org/html/1701.07875v3#S2). It illustrates a useful distance gradient, rather than promising that every trained neural critic supplies the exact gradient or that adversarial optimization always converges.

## The critic and its constraint

The Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual expresses the same distance through scalar functions:

$$
W_1(P_r,P_g)
=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\le1}
\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_r}[f(x)]
-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}[f(G_\theta(z))]\right).
$$

The critic $f$ must be **1-Lipschitz**:

$$
|f(x)-f(y)|\le\|x-y\|_2
\qquad\text{for all }x,y.
$$

It can assign higher scores to real samples, but cannot increase their score difference arbitrarily relative to their separation. Unlike a probability discriminator, its output is a real number without a sigmoid. Adding a constant to every score cancels in the difference; an individual critic score has no interpretation as a probability of being real.

A neural critic searches a restricted function family and is trained for a finite number of steps. Its score gap therefore approximates the dual objective. Without Lipschitz control, even scaling a useful critic by an arbitrarily large constant can make the objective grow without bound. The constraint is part of the distance definition, rather than an optional training trick. See [Arjovsky et al., Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/1701.07875v3#S3).

## Losses to minimize

The ideal game minimizes the distance over the generator and maximizes the score gap over admissible critics:

$$
\min_\theta\max_{\|f_\phi\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\le1}
\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_r}[f_\phi(x)]
-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}[f_\phi(G_\theta(z))]\right).
$$

The handwritten objectives on page 25 can be expressed consistently as losses for gradient descent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_D
&=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}[f_\phi(G_\theta(z))]
-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_r}[f_\phi(x)],\\
L_G
&=-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}[f_\phi(G_\theta(z))].
\end{aligned}
$$

Minimizing $L_D$ raises the real–fake score gap. Minimizing $L_G$ raises the generated samples' scores. The real-data term is absent from $L_G$ because it does not depend on the generator parameters while the critic is fixed.

During a critic update, hold the generator fixed and detach generated samples. During a generator update, keep the critic parameters fixed while differentiating through its input back into the generator. The notebook uses five critic steps per generator step; this is a training choice, not a theorem about the required ratio.

## Gradient penalty

For a differentiable critic, a global bound $\|\nabla_x f(x)\|_2\le1$ controls its rate of change on a convex input domain. WGAN-GP encourages unit gradient norm at sampled interpolations between real and generated points:

$$
\hat{x}=\alpha x+(1-\alpha)\tilde{x},
\qquad
\alpha\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1),
\quad \tilde{x}=G_\theta(z).
$$

The minimized critic loss becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_D^{\mathrm{GP}}
&=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x}\sim P_g}[f_\phi(\tilde{x})]
-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P_r}[f_\phi(x)]\\
&\quad+\lambda\mathbb{E}_{\hat{x}}\left[
\left(\|\nabla_{\hat{x}}f_\phi(\hat{x})\|_2-1\right)^2
\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The gradient is taken with respect to the **input sample**, not the critic's weights. Flatten each sample's image dimensions before computing its Euclidean norm, then average the penalty over the batch. The generator loss stays $L_G$ above.

The scan's shorthand “gradient norm approximately one, so 1-Lipschitz” needs a qualification. This is a **soft, sampled penalty**, not proof of a global bound. It penalizes norms below one as well as above one, whereas the exact dual constraint only requires a bound of at most one. Randomly paired endpoints also need not form an optimal transport coupling. This distinction follows [Gulrajani et al., Section 4](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2017/file/892c3b1c6dccd52936e27cbd0ff683d6-Paper.pdf).

## Spectral normalization

The scan describes a linear map as stretching space in different directions. Its largest singular value measures the maximum stretch:

$$
\sigma_{\max}(W)
=\sup_{\|v\|_2=1}\|Wv\|_2.
$$

For a nonzero matrix, spectral normalization uses

$$
\widehat{W}=\frac{W}{\sigma_{\max}(W)}.
$$

For an affine layer with this exact matrix norm, the Lipschitz constant is one. Composing such layers with activations whose Lipschitz constants are at most one gives a network bound of at most one. This explains the layerwise argument in [Miyato et al., Section 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/1802.05957v1#S2.SS1).

An implementation still requires care: power iteration approximates the norm; a reshaped convolution kernel is not the full spatial convolution operator; and other layers can change the bound. In particular, the seminar's spectrally normalized critic also contains affine `InstanceNorm2d` layers. Normalizing its convolution weights alone does not establish that the complete critic is globally 1-Lipschitz.

Spectral normalization can be used with several adversarial losses. The notebook combines it with the WGAN score losses; the name **SN-GAN** alone does not specify a Wasserstein objective.
