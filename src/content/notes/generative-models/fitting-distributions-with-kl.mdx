The data have two clusters, one around $-3$ and one around $-8$, and the model is a single Gaussian. Minimize the KL divergence from the data to the model, and the Gaussian settles at $-5.5$ with a standard deviation of $2.55$: its densest part sits in the empty gap, where no data point lies. Swap the two arguments of the divergence, and the Gaussian sits on one cluster and ignores the other.

Real models never represent the data exactly, so which of these failures a loss prefers is a property of every generative model trained with it. To see it we fit a Gaussian $Q$ with trainable parameters to a target $P$ by gradient descent, with the KL divergence of the [entropy note](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences) as the loss and the [trainable Gaussian](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions#gaussians-in-pytorch) as the model.

## How the parameters are trained

In one dimension the model is $Q = \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with parameters $\theta = (\mu, \log\sigma)$; storing $\sigma$ through its logarithm keeps it positive whatever the optimizer does. In two dimensions the parameters are the mean and the three raw entries of the Cholesky factor.

One iteration of the fitting loop rebuilds $Q$ from the current parameters, evaluates its log-density at fixed samples $x_i \sim P$, and computes the loss, a sample estimate of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$. Backpropagation then computes the gradient of the loss with respect to each parameter, and the optimizer moves the parameters against it. For plain gradient descent with learning rate $\eta$ the step is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mu &\leftarrow \mu - \eta\,\frac{\partial\,\text{loss}}{\partial \mu}, \\
\log\sigma &\leftarrow \log\sigma - \eta\,\frac{\partial\,\text{loss}}{\partial \log\sigma}.
\end{aligned}
$$

[Adam](/notes/ml/stochastic-and-adaptive-optimizers#adam) ([Kingma and Ba, 2015](#references)) moves in a similar direction but rescales each coordinate's step by running estimates of the gradient's mean and magnitude; the PyTorch fits quoted below use it.

<TrainingLoop />

In PyTorch the parameters are tensors created with `requires_grad=True`, `loss.backward()` fills their `.grad`, and `optimizer.step()` takes the step; the target's parameters carry no gradient and stay fixed. The Code section runs the same loop in NumPy with the gradients written out by hand.

### The loss is a Monte Carlo estimate of KL

KL is an expectation under $P$, so we can estimate it by averaging over samples $x_1, \dots, x_N \sim P$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) &= \mathbb E_{x\sim P}\big[\log p(x) - \log q(x)\big] \\
&\approx \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[\log p(x_i) - \log q(x_i)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

By the law of large numbers the average converges to the expectation as $N$ grows. Averaging $\log_2$ instead of $\ln$ reports the loss in bits; it rescales the loss and its gradients by $1/\ln 2 \approx 1.443$.

<MonteCarloKL />

If the samples come from another distribution $R$ with density $r$, each term is reweighted by $w_i = p(x_i)/r(x_i)$, the [importance weight](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#importance-sampling); this needs $r > 0$ wherever $p > 0$.

The estimate can be negative although KL never is. Fit a full-covariance 2-D Gaussian, five parameters, to 1000 fixed samples of $\mathcal N\big((-5,-5), \mathbf I\big)$: after 4800 Adam steps the loss reads $-0.0020$ bits. The model is fitted to the same samples on which the loss is evaluated. The target is one of the candidate Gaussians, so at the maximum-likelihood fit the average of $\log q$ over the samples is at least the average of $\log p$. How far below zero follows from Wilks' theorem ([Wilks, 1938](#references)). When the family contains $P$, its log-likelihood is smooth and the true parameters lie inside the parameter set, twice the gain in total log-likelihood of the fit over the true parameters is approximately $\chi^2$ with $k$ degrees of freedom for $k$ fitted parameters. The intuition: near its maximum the log-likelihood is a quadratic bowl, and the noise of the sample shifts the maximum by $k$ independent, roughly normal amounts, each of which adds half a squared standard normal to the gain. The loss is minus the gain divided by $N$, and a $\chi^2_k$ variable has mean $k$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[\text{loss}] &\approx -\frac{\mathbb E[\chi^2_k]}{2N} = -\frac{k}{2N} \\
&= -\frac{5}{2000} = -0.0025 \text{ nats} \\
&= -0.0036 \text{ bits}.
\end{aligned}
$$

In 2000 simulated fits the estimate averages $-0.0036$ bits with a standard deviation of $0.0023$, so $-0.0020$ is a typical value. An honest estimate of the fitted model's divergence needs fresh samples, as held-out data does for any model.

### What the gradients are

For a 1-D Gaussian, $\log q(x) = -\log\sigma - \tfrac12\log 2\pi - \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$. The term $\log p(x_i)$ does not depend on the parameters, so only $-\log q$ contributes (natural logarithms):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\,\text{loss}}{\partial \mu} &= -\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{x_i-\mu}{\sigma^2}, \\
\frac{\partial\,\text{loss}}{\partial \log\sigma} &= \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[1 - \frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line uses $\sigma^{-2} = e^{-2\log\sigma}$, whose derivative in $\log\sigma$ is $-2\sigma^{-2}$, and the derivative $1$ of $\log\sigma$ itself. Both vanish when $\mu$ is the sample mean and $\sigma^2 = \frac1N\sum_i (x_i-\mu)^2$ the biased sample variance: the maximum-likelihood estimates, as the entropy note predicted. The gradient never needs $p$; the values $p(x_i)$ only make the reported number equal to the KL. With real data, where $p$ is unknown, we drop that constant and minimize the negative log-likelihood $-\frac1N\sum_i \log q(x_i)$.

The exact KL between two 1-D Gaussians shows where the steps lead. Take $D_{\mathrm{KL}} = \mathbb E_P[\log p(x) - \log q(x)]$, write both log-densities as above, and use $\mathbb E_P[(x-\mu_P)^2] = \sigma_P^2$ and $\mathbb E_P[(x-\mu)^2] = \sigma_P^2 + (\mu_P-\mu)^2$. The constants $\tfrac12\log 2\pi$ cancel, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
&D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal N(\mu_P, \sigma_P^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\big) \\
&\quad= \log\frac{\sigma}{\sigma_P} + \frac{\sigma_P^2 + (\mu_P - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac12 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last term is $-\sigma_P^2/(2\sigma_P^2)$, from $\log p$.

<FittingTrajectory />

With plain gradient descent on $(\mu, \log\sigma)$ the trajectory shows the priorities of the forward KL. While $Q$ is narrow and far away, most of $P$'s mass sits where $q \approx 0$, and the cheapest fix is to widen $Q$: $\sigma$ jumps up in the first steps while $\mu$ only creeps. Once $Q$ covers $P$, $\mu$ moves across and $\sigma$ shrinks back. The order comes from the gradient sizes. From the closed form, $\partial/\partial\log\sigma = 1 - \big(\sigma_P^2 + (\mu_P-\mu)^2\big)/\sigma^2$ grows like $(\mu_P-\mu)^2/\sigma^2$, while $\partial/\partial\mu = (\mu-\mu_P)/\sigma^2$ grows only like $\lvert\mu_P-\mu\rvert/\sigma^2$. Adam, which normalizes each coordinate's step, moves both at a similar rate and reaches the same optimum by a different path.

## What KL assumes

The formula for continuous distributions ([Kullback and Leibler, 1951](#references)),

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = \int p(x)\,\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx ,
$$

is a meaningful, finite number only under conditions (a)–(c) below; (d) and (e) are choices that must be made consistently ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), §8.5; [Murphy, 2023](#references), §5.1).

### (a) Common support

The **support** of a distribution is the set of its possible values, $\operatorname{supp} p = \lbrace x : p(x) > 0\rbrace$. If $q(x) = 0$ on a region to which $P$ gives positive probability, $\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \infty$ there and the divergence is infinite. Formally, $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$ is finite only if $P$ is **absolutely continuous** with respect to $Q$, written $P \ll Q$: wherever $P$ has mass, $Q$ must have mass too.

This is one reason models trained by maximum likelihood use densities that are positive everywhere, such as Gaussians: the per-sample loss $-\log q(x_i)$ stays finite at every observed point. Positivity alone does not make a model blurry. Combined with the forward KL, which punishes any missed mass, it makes a model that cannot represent the data spread probability between and around the modes, which is one explanation offered for the blurry samples of likelihood-trained image models ([Theis, van den Oord and Bethge, 2016](#references)).

<SupportMismatch />

The condition is one-sided. Points where $q(x) > 0$ but $p(x) = 0$ contribute $0$ to $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$; they matter only for the reverse direction.

### (b) Integrability

The integral must converge, and matching supports are not enough. Let $P$ be a standard Cauchy distribution and $Q = \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)$. Both are positive everywhere, and with $p(x) = 1/\big(\pi(1 + x^2)\big)$

$$
\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2} - \log(1 + x^2) + \log\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}{\pi} .
$$

Under $P$ the middle term has the finite mean $2\ln 2$, while $\mathbb E_P[x^2] = \infty$, so $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = \infty$. A model with light tails cannot fit heavy-tailed data in KL.

### (c) Normalized densities

$p$ and $q$ must be probability densities or mass functions: non-negative and integrating to 1. Raw scores such as a classifier's logits go through a softmax first, and the `log_prob` of a `torch.distributions` object is already normalized.

### (d) Direction

KL is not symmetric, so we must decide which distribution plays the truth. The **forward** KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$ averages over $P$ and penalizes missed modes: $Q$ must cover everything where $P$ lives. It is the direction of maximum likelihood. The **reverse** KL $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,P)$ averages over $Q$ and penalizes extra mass: $Q$ must not spread to where $P \approx 0$. It is the direction of [variational inference](/notes/bayesian-methods/variational-inference#the-evidence-lower-bound-and-the-reverse-kl), including the [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae). The two are also called inclusive and exclusive KL, and their behaviour zero-avoiding, or mode-covering, and zero-forcing, or mode-seeking ([Murphy, 2023](#references), §5.1.4).

<DirectionPenalties />

Only the forward direction can be estimated from data alone. It needs samples from $P$ and $\log q$ at them; the reverse direction needs $\log p$, which real data never provides.

### (e) Continuous versus discrete

For discrete distributions the integral becomes a sum. Mixing the two gives an infinite divergence: if $P$ has an atom at $x$, any $Q$ with a density gives $\lbrace x\rbrace$ probability zero, so $P \not\ll Q$ and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = \infty$ by condition (a). For continuous data, maximum likelihood is therefore read as a sample estimate of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$ for the underlying density $p$. Reading it as the KL of the empirical distribution of the samples works only for [discrete outcomes](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#why-cross-entropy-is-the-loss-we-minimize).

### In code

A Gaussian `Q` is positive everywhere, so (a) holds mathematically whatever the target, and it can still fail numerically. Computing `qx = Q.log_prob(x).exp()` and then `qx.log2()` underflows in [32-bit floating point](/notes/acos/floating-point#special-values) once $\log q(x)$ drops below about $-103$: for $\sigma \approx 1$, about 14 standard deviations from $\mu$. The loss is then infinite exactly when $Q$ starts far from $P$. Working with `log_prob` directly avoids it.

The two-mode targets below need the same care. Their log-density $\log\big(\tfrac12 p_1(x) + \tfrac12 p_2(x)\big)$ is computed from the component log-densities $a = \log p_1(x) + \log\tfrac12$ and $b = \log p_2(x) + \log\tfrac12$ as

$$
\operatorname{logaddexp}(a, b) = \max(a, b) + \log\big(1 + e^{-|a - b|}\big),
$$

the two-term case of the [stable log-sum-exp](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability#log-sum-exp-and-the-stable-softmax), which stays finite far from both components.

## One Gaussian, two modes

With a single Gaussian target, KL fitting recovers the target. The informative case is a target the family **cannot represent**. In 2-D it is an equal mixture of $\mathcal N\big((-5,-5), \mathbf I\big)$ and $\mathcal N\big((4,4), \mathbf I\big)$; in 1-D it is

$$
P = \tfrac12\,\mathcal N(-3,\, 0.5^2) + \tfrac12\,\mathcal N(-8,\, 0.5^2),
$$

and the model starts from $Q = \mathcal N(0, 1)$. We compare four losses: forward KL, reverse KL, JS and a least-squares loss on density values. None is satisfactory, each for its own reason, and the same trade-offs reappear across generative models ([Huszár, 2015](#references); [Theis, van den Oord and Bethge, 2016](#references); [Isachenko, lecture 5](#references)).

### Far from the target, the missed mode dominates

The initial $Q = \mathcal N(0, 1)$ barely reaches the far mode at $-8$, eight standard deviations from its mean. There $\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ is huge, and that mode dominates the whole divergence, $17.9$ nats. For a Gaussian $Q$ the forward KL stays finite, and its gradient in $\mu$, $(\mu - \mathbb E_P[x])/\sigma^2 = 5.5$, is large. Overlap matters for the bounded losses, JS and least squares, whose gradients nearly vanish without it.

### Forward KL covers both modes; reverse KL picks one

Minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q)$ makes a single Gaussian cover both modes with a wide spread. Write the forward KL as

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = -H(P) - \mathbb E_P[\log q(x)] .
$$

Only the second term depends on $Q$, and for a Gaussian $\log q$ depends on $x$ only through $x$ and $xx^{\top}$. Minimizing it over $\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ gives exact moment matching ([Bishop, 2006](#references), §10.7), derived below:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol\mu^\ast &= \mathbb E_P[x], \\
\boldsymbol\Sigma^\ast &= \operatorname{Cov}_P[x] .
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

Write $\mathbf m = \mathbb E_P[x]$ and $\mathbf C = \operatorname{Cov}_P[x]$, and assume $\mathbf C$ is positive definite. For $q = \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)$ in $d$ dimensions,

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\mathbb E_P[\log q(x)] ={}& \tfrac12\log\det(2\pi\boldsymbol\Sigma) \\
&+ \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_P\big[(x - \boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(x - \boldsymbol\mu)\big] .
\end{aligned}
$$

Split $x - \boldsymbol\mu = (x - \mathbf m) + (\mathbf m - \boldsymbol\mu)$. The cross term averages to zero because $\mathbb E_P[x - \mathbf m] = 0$, and a scalar equals its trace, $\mathbb E[v^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}v] = \operatorname{tr}\big(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbb E[vv^{\top}]\big)$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb E_P\big[(x - \boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(x - \boldsymbol\mu)\big] \\
&\quad= \operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf C) + (\mathbf m - \boldsymbol\mu)^{\top}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf m - \boldsymbol\mu) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last term is $\ge 0$ and zero only at $\boldsymbol\mu = \mathbf m$, which fixes the mean. For the covariance let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d > 0$ be the eigenvalues of $\mathbf A = \mathbf C^{-1/2}\boldsymbol\Sigma\,\mathbf C^{-1/2}$. The cyclic property of the trace gives $\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf C) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf A^{-1}) = \sum_i 1/\lambda_i$, and $\det\boldsymbol\Sigma = \det\mathbf C \cdot \prod_i \lambda_i$, so the part that depends on $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is

$$
\tfrac12\log\det\mathbf C + \tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^d \Big(\log\lambda_i + \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\Big) .
$$

Each term $\log\lambda + 1/\lambda$ has derivative $(\lambda - 1)/\lambda^2$, negative for $\lambda < 1$ and positive for $\lambda > 1$, so it is smallest at $\lambda = 1$. All eigenvalues equal to 1 means $\mathbf A = \mathbf I$, that is $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \mathbf C$.

</details>

For the 1-D target, $\mu^\ast = -5.5$ and, by the [law of total variance](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation#the-law-of-total-variance), $\sigma^{\ast2} = 0.25 + 2.5^2 = 6.5$, so $\sigma^\ast \approx 2.55$: the average variance within a component is $0.25$, and the spread of the two means around $-5.5$ adds $2.5^2$. For the 2-D target the optimum has mean $(-0.5, -0.5)$. The same law adds the within-component covariance $\mathbf I$ to the covariance of the two means, which sit at $\pm(4.5, 4.5)$ from it and contribute $4.5^2 = 20.25$ to every entry:

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma^\ast = \begin{pmatrix} 21.25 & 20.25 \\ 20.25 & 21.25\end{pmatrix},
$$

one elongated Gaussian along the diagonal through both modes, densest in the empty gap. Its divergence is $1.170$ nats, or $1.688$ bits. An Adam fit (learning rate $0.1$, 2000 fixed samples, started from $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$) reaches this picture at an estimated $1.669$ bits after 4800 steps; the gap of $0.019$ bits is below the standard deviation of a 2000-sample estimate, about $0.023$ bits.

The reverse divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,P)$ averages over samples of $Q$. It punishes mass where $P$ is small and ignores parts of $P$ that $Q$ misses, so $Q$ locks onto one mode ([Bishop, 2006](#references), §10.1.2; [Minka, 2005](#references)). For two equal, well-separated modes, sitting exactly on one costs only $\ln 2$. If $Q$ stays near the first component $p_1$, where the second is negligible, then $p \approx p_1/2$ there and

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,P) &\approx \mathbb E_Q\Big[\log\frac{q(x)}{p_1(x)/2}\Big] \\
&= \ln 2 + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,p_1) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second term is zero for $Q = p_1$ and positive otherwise, so the best Gaussian is the mode itself, at $\ln 2$.

### JS does not cover the modes either

JS never blows up, and it still drops a mode. For the 1-D target the best single Gaussian under the symmetric JS, found by grid search, sits exactly on one mode, at $0.216$ nats against $0.255$ for the moment-matched Gaussian. Exactly on one mode, $Q = p_1$, the mixture is $M = \tfrac12(P + Q) = \tfrac34 p_1 + \tfrac14 p_2$. The modes are ten standard deviations apart, so wherever one component has mass the other is negligible, and each log ratio is a constant on its mode:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,M) &\approx \log_2\tfrac{1}{3/4} = \log_2\tfrac43, \\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,M) &\approx \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac{1/2}{3/4} + \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac{1/2}{1/4} \\
&= \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac43 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Their average is

$$
D_{\mathrm{JS}} \approx \tfrac34\log_2\tfrac43 \approx 0.311 \text{ bits}.
$$

Trained runs agree. A fit of the 2-D mixture with Adam (learning rate $0.1$, 5000 steps, 5000 fresh samples per step), started from $\boldsymbol\mu = (-2,-2)$, ends near $(-4.99, -5.06)$ with a nearly unit covariance, on the mode at $(-5,-5)$. In 1-D, plain gradient descent from $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ ends at $\mu = -2.995$, $\sigma = 0.495$ after 1800 steps, on the nearer mode; $Q$'s peak density there, $0.81$, is twice the target's $0.40$. Both runs minimized a weighted variant, $0.7\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,M) + 0.3\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\,\|\,M)$. With the two terms above its one-mode value is $(0.7 \cdot \tfrac12 + 0.3)\log_2\tfrac43 = 0.65\log_2\tfrac43 \approx 0.270$ bits. The final 1-D Gaussian has exactly this value, $0.270$ bits by numerical integration. The 2-D run's last estimate, $0.2734$ bits, is a sample estimate whose standard deviation at 5000 samples is about $0.008$ bits, so it agrees with $0.270$ up to noise.

Each half of JS is an expectation under its own distribution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{JS}}(P\,\|\,Q) &= \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{x\sim P}\Big[\log\frac{p(x)}{m(x)}\Big] \\
&\quad + \tfrac12\,\mathbb E_{y\sim Q}\Big[\log\frac{q(y)}{m(y)}\Big],
\end{aligned}
$$

with $m = \tfrac12(p + q)$ evaluated by `logaddexp`. The estimate needs samples of both $P$ and $Q$. The second term depends on $Q$'s parameters also through the samples $y_j$, so they must be drawn with the [reparameterization trick](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions#the-reparameterization-view), $y = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon$; the runs above drew them without it, so their gradients miss that dependence.

Boundedness has a second cost. When $Q$ and $P$ barely overlap, JS is already close to its maximum $\ln 2$ and almost flat. At the initial $\mathcal N(0, 1)$ it is $0.645$ nats, $93\%$ of $\ln 2$, and its gradient in $\mu$ is $0.070$ against $5.5$ for the forward KL. The same saturation gives the original minimax GAN its vanishing generator gradients ([Arjovsky and Bottou, 2017](#references), Theorem 2.4; see the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#step-2-substitute-the-optimal-discriminator-and-recognize-js) and [Wasserstein GANs](/notes/generative-models/wgan)).

### A least-squares loss on densities is sensitive to scale

A naive alternative compares density values directly, at samples $x_i \sim P$:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{LS}} = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl(q(x_i) - p(x_i)\bigr)^2 .
$$

Density values depend on the units of the data. Stretching every coordinate by $c$ divides all densities by $c^{d}$ in $d$ dimensions, so the loss shrinks by $c^{2d}$. Adam absorbs a constant factor, but not the second problem: when $Q$ does not overlap $P$, $q(x_i) \approx 0$ at the data points, and the loss sits near the constant $\mathbb E_P[p(x)^2]$ with almost no gradient. KL has neither problem. The ratio $p/q$ does not change under rescaling, and $-\log q(x_i)$ grows like $(x_i-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2$ far from $\mu$, so the pull grows as $Q$ moves away.

The least-squares loss also has several minima. In 1-D its global minimum, $0.0470$, is a wide Gaussian with $\sigma \approx 2.53$, close to the moment-matched one, and a Gaussian on one mode, $\mathcal N(-3.01, 1.21^2)$, is a local minimum at $0.0495$. In 2-D a Gaussian on one mode, $\mathcal N\big((4,4), 1.58^2\,\mathbf I\big)$, is a local minimum at $0.00113$, while the global one, $0.00102$, is centred at $(-0.5,-0.5)$ and stretched along the diagonal (standard deviation $6.5$ along it, $0.43$ across). An Adam fit started from $\mathcal N\big((1,1), \mathbf I\big)$, which overlaps only the mode at $(4,4)$, ends on that mode, wider than it; the other mode contributes almost no gradient, because $q \approx 0$ there.

Bold marks the best candidate for each loss; for the two KLs and JS it is also the global optimum over all $(\mu, \sigma)$. Forward KL and least squares prefer the wide Gaussian, reverse KL and JS one mode. The last row shows how hard each loss pulls the initial $Q$: KL strongly, JS and least squares hardly at all.

A forward-KL fit with Adam (learning rate $0.01$, 1000 fixed samples) lands on the moment-matched Gaussian, $\mu = -5.494$ and $\sigma = 2.566$, with an estimated $1.300$ bits against the exact $0.936$ nats, or $1.350$ bits. The difference, $0.050$ bits, is 1.6 times the standard deviation of a 1000-sample estimate, which is $0.032$ bits.

The families of the course inherit these preferences. Flows and autoregressive models are trained by maximum likelihood, the forward KL, and spread mass rather than drop a mode. The VAE's encoder is fitted in the reverse direction and tends to under-cover its posterior. The original GAN minimizes JS with an optimal discriminator, and its samples are sharp while modes go missing; in GANs the alternating optimization adds [mode collapse](/notes/generative-models/gans#mode-collapse-and-how-to-fight-it) of its own.

## What you should remember

- The loss is a Monte Carlo average of $\log p - \log q$ over samples of $P$; its gradient needs only $\log q$, which makes it maximum likelihood.
- An estimate on the training samples is biased low, by about $k/(2N)$ nats for $k$ parameters.
- KL is finite only if $Q$ has mass wherever $P$ does, the tails are integrable and both are normalized.
- Forward KL covers every mode and blurs; reverse KL and JS pick one mode and stay sharp.
- JS saturates at $\ln 2$ and least squares depends on units; far from the data both give almost no gradient.

## Code

The 1-D fit of this note in NumPy, with the gradients of "What the gradients are" in place of autograd, followed by the table of the four losses by numerical integration. It uses NumPy and SciPy and runs in a few seconds.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

# target P: equal mixture of N(-3, 0.5^2) and N(-8, 0.5^2); half of the samples from each
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = np.concatenate([rng.normal(-3, 0.5, 500), rng.normal(-8, 0.5, 500)])
log_p = lambda t: np.logaddexp(np.log(0.5) + norm.logpdf(t, -3, 0.5),
                               np.log(0.5) + norm.logpdf(t, -8, 0.5))

# forward KL on fixed samples, plain gradient descent on (mu, log sigma), from Q = N(0, 1)
mu, log_s, lr = 0.0, 0.0, 0.05
for step in range(3000):
    s = np.exp(log_s)
    g_mu = -np.mean(x - mu) / s**2                 # d loss / d mu
    g_log_s = np.mean(1 - (x - mu)**2 / s**2)      # d loss / d log sigma
    mu, log_s = mu - lr * g_mu, log_s - lr * g_log_s
s = np.exp(log_s)
kl = np.mean(log_p(x) - norm.logpdf(x, mu, s))     # the loss, in nats
print(round(mu, 3), round(s, 3), round(x.mean(), 3), round(x.std(), 3), round(kl, 3))

# the four losses for three candidate Gaussians, by numerical integration
grid = np.linspace(-40, 30, 400_001)
dx = grid[1] - grid[0]
lp = log_p(grid)
p = np.exp(lp)
def losses(m, s):
    lq = norm.logpdf(grid, m, s)
    q = np.exp(lq)
    lm = np.logaddexp(lp, lq) - np.log(2)          # log of the mixture M = (P + Q) / 2
    fwd = np.sum(p * (lp - lq)) * dx
    rev = np.sum(q * (lq - lp)) * dx
    js = 0.5 * np.sum(p * (lp - lm)) * dx + 0.5 * np.sum(q * (lq - lm)) * dx
    ls = np.sum(p * (q - p)**2) * dx
    return np.round([fwd, rev, js, ls], 4)
for m, sd in [(0, 1), (-5.5, np.sqrt(6.5)), (-3, 0.5)]:
    print(losses(m, sd))
```
