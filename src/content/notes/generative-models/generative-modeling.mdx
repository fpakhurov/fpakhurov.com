A classifier answers a question about an object: is this image a cat? A generative model produces the object itself: a new image of a cat that was not in the training set. The two tasks differ in shape. A classifier has one right answer per input. A request for "a cat on a sofa" has endless right answers, some more plausible than others, and the model has to produce them with the right frequencies.

## The problem: examples of an unknown distribution

We treat a data set as independent draws from some distribution:

$$
x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)} \sim p_{\text{data}}(x).
$$

For images, $x$ is a vector of pixel values; for text, a sequence of tokens; for audio, a waveform. Nobody can write down the density of "photographs of cats", so $p_{\text{data}}$ is unknown. All we have is the sample.

The goal is a model $p_\theta(x)$ with parameters $\theta$ such that $p_\theta \approx p_{\text{data}}$, and from which we can **draw new samples** $x \sim p_\theta$. If the approximation is good, the new samples look like the data without copying it.

<DataToModel />

Why not simply store the data and replay it? A model that puts probability $1/N$ on each training point is the best possible fit to the sample: on a finite set of outcomes the average log-likelihood of $Q$ is minus the cross-entropy from the empirical distribution to $Q$, which [Gibbs' inequality](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#cross-entropy-surprise-under-a-wrong-belief) maximizes at that distribution itself. Every one of its samples is a copy.

For continuous data it is worse. Put a Gaussian bump of width $s$ on each of the $N$ training points in $d$ dimensions, $q_s(x) = \frac1N \sum_j \mathcal N\big(x;\, x^{(j)}, s^2\mathbf I\big)$. At a training point the sum is at least its own term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_s\big(x^{(i)}\big)
&\ge \tfrac1N\, \mathcal N\big(x^{(i)};\, x^{(i)}, s^2\mathbf I\big) \\
&= \tfrac1N\, (2\pi s^2)^{-d/2} \;\xrightarrow{\,s \to 0\,}\; \infty ,
\end{aligned}
$$

so the likelihood of the sample grows without bound as the bumps shrink, the [unbounded-likelihood failure](/notes/probability/maximum-likelihood#when-maximum-likelihood-goes-wrong) of mixtures. A useful model is restricted to a family that cannot memorize, and its quality is judged on data it has not seen ([Kaiming He, MIT 6.S978, lecture 1](#references); [Stanford CS236 notes](#references)).

The classical guarantees of maximum likelihood, consistency and asymptotic efficiency, need conditions: the model family contains the true distribution and is identifiable, the log-likelihood is smooth, and the sample grows while the number of parameters stays fixed. The Probability course states them with the [Fisher information](/notes/probability/maximum-likelihood#fisher-information). A network with more parameters than training images is far from that regime ([BMML lecture notes, 2026](#references), lecture 1).

Most applications want a conditional model $p(x \mid c)$: an image for a text prompt $c$, speech for a sentence, a translation for a source sentence, a high-resolution image for a small one. The unconditional case is the same problem with an empty condition, and the course treats it first.

## What a generative model is for

Sampling new objects is the most visible use. A model that can also evaluate $p_\theta(x)$ gives a **density estimate**: one possible anomaly score, since low density can flag unusual objects. The score depends on the representation, because a density changes under a change of variables, and a high density at a point does not mean that the point looks typical; the [anomaly detection note](/notes/ml/anomaly-detection#density-scores) works through these limits. A good density model is also a good compressor. A prefix code can give each discrete $x$ a codeword of $\lceil -\log_2 p(x) \rceil$ bits, because these lengths satisfy the Kraft inequality, $\sum_x 2^{-\lceil -\log_2 p(x) \rceil} \le \sum_x p(x) = 1$, which is exactly the condition for such a code to exist. Each $x$ then costs less than $-\log_2 p(x) + 1$ bits, and no prefix code has a shorter average length than the [entropy](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#entropy-expected-surprise) ([Cover and Thomas, 2006](#references), chapter 5).

Models with a latent variable, such as the VAE, also learn a **representation**: they map each object to a low-dimensional code whose directions often carry meaning, without labels. Not every family offers all three uses, and the trade-offs between them shape the rest of the course ([Stanford CS236 notes](#references)).

## What "approximate" means: a divergence to minimize

To train $p_\theta$ we need a number that says how far it is from $p_{\text{data}}$. The standard choice is the Kullback–Leibler divergence ([Entropy, cross-entropy and KL divergence](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta)
&= \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log p_{\text{data}}(x)\big] \\
&\quad - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log p_\theta(x)\big].
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term does not depend on $\theta$. Minimizing the divergence therefore means maximizing the expected log-likelihood of the data under the model. With the sample in place of the expectation this is **maximum likelihood**:

$$
\theta^\star = \arg\max_\theta \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log p_\theta\big(x^{(i)}\big).
$$

The catch is that this needs $p_\theta(x)$ as a number. For flexible models it is often impossible to compute, and the families in this course differ mainly in how they get around that: an exact formula, a lower bound, or a different objective altogether ([Isachenko, lecture 1](#references)). The [fitting note](/notes/generative-models/fitting-distributions-with-kl) runs this objective on the simplest case, a Gaussian fitted by gradient descent.

## How a computer samples at all

A model is only generative if we can draw from it. Computers do not draw from arbitrary distributions directly. Everything starts from one primitive, uniform random numbers, and every later step transforms the samples of an easy distribution into samples of a harder one. The [Probability course](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#producing-samples) surveys these techniques; here we follow the chain to the point where neural networks take over.

### Step 1: uniform numbers

A processor is deterministic, so programs get their random numbers from a **pseudo-random generator**. It keeps an internal state $s$, updates it by a fixed rule $s_{n+1} = T(s_n)$ and returns a number computed from the state. The starting state is set from a **seed**. The simplest rule is the linear congruential generator ([Knuth, 1997](#references), §3.2.1). A toy version with 16 states is

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_{n+1} &= (5\,s_n + 3) \bmod 16, \\
u_n &= s_n / 16 .
\end{aligned}
$$

From the seed $s_0 = 7$ it visits $7, 6, 1, 8, 11, 10, 5, 12, 15, 14, 9, 0, 3, 2, 13, 4$ and returns to 7. Over one cycle each of the 16 values $0, \tfrac1{16}, \dots, \tfrac{15}{16}$ appears exactly once, which is what "uniform" means for a generator.

<PrngToy />

The toy has two defects. It repeats after 16 numbers. And consecutive pairs fall on a few lines: dividing the rule by 16 gives $u_{n+1} = 5u_n + \tfrac3{16} - k$, where the integer $k$ is what the $\bmod$ removes, and $0 \le 5s_n + 3 \le 78$ leaves only $k = 0, \dots, 4$. So every pair $(u_n, u_{n+1})$ lies on one of five parallel lines of slope 5. Real generators keep the state-and-rule design and remove both. NumPy's default, PCG64, has a 128-bit state and scrambles it before output ([O'Neill, 2014](#references)); on GPUs, PyTorch uses Philox, which computes the $n$-th number directly from $n$ and the seed so that thousands of threads can draw at once ([Salmon et al., 2011](#references)).

The seed is what makes an experiment repeatable, and the guarantee is narrow. The same seed reproduces the same numbers only with the same generator, the same calls in the same order and the same library build. NumPy keeps its raw streams stable across versions, but samplers built on them, such as `normal`, may change between releases ([NumPy documentation](#references)).

### Step 2: any one-dimensional distribution, by inverting the CDF

Suppose we need an outcome $a$, $b$ or $c$ with probabilities $0.2$, $0.5$ and $0.3$. Cut $[0, 1)$ into pieces of exactly these lengths, $[0, 0.2)$, $[0.2, 0.7)$ and $[0.7, 1)$, draw $u$ and return the outcome whose piece contains it. A uniform number falls into a piece with probability equal to its length. The cut points are the running totals of the probabilities, the cumulative distribution function $F$, so sampling is a lookup: the first outcome whose running total exceeds $u$.

<DiscreteInverse />

For a continuous, strictly increasing CDF $F$ the same lookup asks which $x$ has accumulated exactly $u$ of the probability. With $U \sim \mathcal U(0, 1)$,

$$
\boxed{\,X = F^{-1}(U) \sim F\,}
$$

This is **inverse transform sampling** ([Devroye, 1986](#references), §II.2). It works because $F^{-1}(U) \le x$ exactly when $U \le F(x)$, which has probability $F(x)$; the [functions of random variables note](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables#inverse-transform-sampling) gives the proof and the version for CDFs with jumps. For the exponential distribution with rate $\lambda$, solving $1 - e^{-\lambda x} = u$ gives

$$
x = -\frac{\ln(1 - u)}{\lambda} .
$$

With $\lambda = 1$, the uniform numbers $0.1$, $0.5$, $0.9$ and $0.99$ become $0.105$, $0.693$, $2.303$ and $4.605$. Half of all samples land in the short stretch below $0.693$, and the last 1% spread over everything beyond $4.6$: the long tail appears by itself.

For the standard normal, $F$ is $\Phi$, whose inverse has no formula in elementary functions. Libraries evaluate $\Phi^{-1}$ with rational approximations accurate to about 16 digits ([Wichura, 1988](#references)). The figure shows why the recipe produces a bell.

### Step 3: the standard normal, by Box–Muller

Box and Muller found an exact shortcut that turns two independent uniforms into two independent standard normals ([Box and Muller, 1958](#references)):

$$
\begin{aligned}
R &= \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}, \\
\Theta &= 2\pi U_2, \\
Z_1 &= R\cos\Theta, \\
Z_2 &= R\sin\Theta .
\end{aligned}
$$

It is step 2 in polar coordinates. Take two independent standard normals, with joint density $\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$, and write them in polar coordinates $(R, \Theta)$. The map from $(r, \theta)$ to $(z_1, z_2)$ has Jacobian determinant $r$, so by the [densities under a transform](/notes/math/multiple-integrals#densities-under-a-transform) section of the Mathematics course

$$
p_{R,\Theta}(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot r\,e^{-r^2/2} .
$$

The density is a function of $\theta$ alone times a function of $r$ alone, so $\Theta$ is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$ and independent of $R$. The squared radius has the distribution function

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(R^2 \le t) &= \int_0^{\sqrt t} r\,e^{-r^2/2}\, dr \\
&= \Big[-e^{-r^2/2}\Big]_0^{\sqrt t} \\
&= 1 - e^{-t/2},
\end{aligned}
$$

an exponential with rate $\tfrac12$. Step 2 samples it as $-2\ln(1 - U_1)$, and since $1 - U_1$ is uniform too, $-2\ln U_1$ works as well. The constructed $(R, \Theta)$ then has the joint distribution of the polar coordinates of a normal pair, and so $(R\cos\Theta, R\sin\Theta)$ has the distribution of the pair itself.

For $u_1 = 0.3$ and $u_2 = 0.8$ the distance is $R = \sqrt{-2\ln 0.3} = 1.552$, the angle is $288^\circ$, and the point is $z = (0.480, -1.476)$.

<BoxMullerSteps />

A floating-point generator on $[0, 1)$ can return exactly 0, where $\ln U_1 = -\infty$, so code feeds the logarithm $1 - U_1$ instead. Libraries use faster variants of the same idea; NumPy's normal sampler uses the ziggurat method.

### Step 4: any Gaussian, by an affine map

Steps 1–3 give standard normal noise $Z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$, a round cloud centred at zero. A shift and a linear map turn it into any Gaussian: for any matrix $\mathbf A$ with $\mathbf A\mathbf A^{\top} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$,

$$
\boxed{\,X = \boldsymbol\mu + \mathbf A Z \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \boldsymbol\Sigma)\,}
$$

The [next note](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions#sampling-from-standard-noise-to-any-gaussian) chooses $\mathbf A$ and turns this map into a model with trainable parameters. The first four steps fit in a few lines:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)               # the seed fixes the whole stream
u = rng.random((100_000, 3))                 # step 1: U(0, 1), three columns
print(u[0].round(4))                         # [0.637  0.2698 0.041 ]

x = -np.log1p(-u[:, 0])                      # step 2: exponential, x = -ln(1 - u)
print(x.mean().round(3), np.median(x).round(3))          # theory: 1 and ln 2 = 0.693

r = np.sqrt(-2 * np.log1p(-u[:, 1]))         # step 3: Box-Muller, 1 - u is never 0
phi = 2 * np.pi * u[:, 2]
z = np.stack([r * np.cos(phi), r * np.sin(phi)], axis=1)
print(z.std(axis=0).round(3), np.corrcoef(z.T)[0, 1].round(3))

mu, Sigma = np.array([1.0, 2.0]), np.array([[2.0, 0.8], [0.8, 1.0]])
L = np.linalg.cholesky(Sigma)                # step 4: any A with A A^T = Sigma
xg = mu + z @ L.T
print(xg.mean(axis=0).round(2), np.cov(xg.T).round(2).ravel())
```

```text
[0.637  0.2698 0.041 ]
1.005 0.694
[0.997 0.994] -0.001
[0.99 2.  ] [1.99 0.79 0.79 0.99]
```

The exponential sample has mean $1.005$ and median $0.694$ against the exact $1$ and $\ln 2$. The normal pair has unit spread and no correlation, and the affine map lands on the requested mean and covariance up to sampling noise.

### Step 5: anything else, by a learned nonlinear map

Gaussians are ellipsoids. Images, speech and text have distributions of far more complicated shape, and to reach them we push the noise through a nonlinear map:

$$
\boxed{\,Z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I) \;\longrightarrow\; X = g_\theta(Z)\,}
$$

The distribution of $X$ is the **pushforward** of the noise distribution through $g_\theta$. In a generative model $g_\theta$ is a neural network, and training chooses $\theta$ so that the pushforward matches the data.

<SamplingLadder />

This is the central picture of the course: a generative model is simple noise plus a learned transformation. Some of the transformation is designed by hand, such as the order in which an autoregressive model generates pixels or the noising schedule of a diffusion model, and the rest is learned ([Kaiming He, MIT 6.S978, lecture 1](#references)). For a one-pass generator, sampling is easy by construction. The hard part is choosing $\theta$, which needs some way to compare the pushforward with the data.

When $g$ is invertible and smooth, the density of $X$ has a formula, the change of variables:

$$
p_X(x) = p_Z\big(g^{-1}(x)\big)\,\left|\det \frac{\partial g^{-1}(x)}{\partial x}\right| .
$$

It is derived in [Multiple integrals](/notes/math/multiple-integrals#densities-under-a-transform) and is the starting point of [normalizing flows](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows#change-of-variables). For a general network there is no such formula, and that is where the families diverge.

## Why this is hard

Inverting a CDF solves sampling in one dimension, and a Gaussian has a formula in any dimension. Real data has neither. A $256 \times 256$ colour image has $196{,}608$ coordinates. Even a binary $28 \times 28$ image has $2^{784}$ possible values, so a table of their probabilities needs $2^{784} - 1$ numbers ([Isachenko, lecture 1](#references)). A joint CDF exists, but sampling coordinate by coordinate would need every conditional CDF, which is exactly what we do not know.

Natural images also fill a tiny part of that space. An array of independent random pixels looks like noise, because neighbouring pixels of a photograph are strongly correlated and these are not. Measurements agree: nearest-neighbour estimates put the intrinsic dimension of ImageNet, whose $224 \times 224$ colour images have $150{,}528$ coordinates, between 26 and 43 ([Pope et al., 2021](#references), Table 1). Real images lie close to a much lower-dimensional surface, and the model has to put nearly all its probability there.

Finally, a flexible function $f_\theta(x) \ge 0$ becomes a density only after division by $\int f_\theta(x)\,dx$, an integral over the whole space. For a neural network it has no closed form, and numerical integration does not rescue it. A grid with only 10 points per coordinate has $10^{784}$ nodes for a $28 \times 28$ image ([why grids fail](/notes/math/multiple-integrals#high-dimensions-why-grids-fail)), and uniform random points almost never land on the thin region where $f_\theta$ is large. Without the integral the likelihood cannot be evaluated.

The two basic rules of probability show where the difficulty sits ([BMML lecture notes, 2026](#references), lecture 1). The product rule factorizes any joint distribution into one-dimensional conditionals,

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = p(x_1) \prod_{k=2}^{n} p(x_k \mid x_1, \dots, x_{k-1}),
$$

which is exact and is the whole idea of autoregressive models; the price is $n$ sequential steps per sample. The sum rule gives the density of a model with a latent code $z$ as an integral,

$$
p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz ,
$$

which has no closed form once the decoder $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ is a nonlinear network. With a linear decoder and Gaussian noise it does: that model is probabilistic PCA, the starting point of the [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#the-latent-variable-model-and-its-posterior). The VAE, diffusion models and GANs are three ways of living with this integral.

Only the latent code is integrated out. In the VAE and in diffusion models the network weights $\theta$ are point estimates, one value found by optimizing an objective; the latent variables $z$, or the noisy images of a diffusion chain, are what is integrated over or sampled.

## Six ways around it

Each family keeps the "noise plus transformation" picture and differs in what it gives up to make training possible. Two groups stand out: likelihood-based models, which compute $\log p_\theta(x)$ exactly or bound it, and likelihood-free models such as GANs, which compare samples instead. The cost of a sample is a second axis. A VAE or a GAN needs one network pass; an autoregressive model needs one pass per element, and energy-based and diffusion models need many steps of a chain ([Murphy, 2023](#references), Table 20.1).

<FamiliesMap />

| Family | How a sample is drawn | What can be computed | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Variational autoencoder (VAE) | $z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$, then a learned decoder | a lower bound on $\log p_\theta(x)$, the ELBO | [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae), [variants](/notes/generative-models/vae-variants) |
| Generative adversarial network (GAN) | $z \sim p(z)$, then $x = G_\theta(z)$ in one pass | no density, only samples | [GAN](/notes/generative-models/gans), [WGAN](/notes/generative-models/wgan) and six branch notes |
| Normalizing flow | $z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf I)$, then a chain of invertible maps | the exact $\log p_\theta(x)$ | [Flows](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows), [architectures](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures) |
| Energy-based model (EBM) | a noisy Markov chain started from noise, many steps | $\log p_\theta(x)$ up to the unknown $\log Z_\theta$ | [MCMC samplers](/notes/generative-models/mcmc-samplers), [EBMs](/notes/generative-models/energy-based-models) |
| Diffusion model | start from noise $x_T$, denoise step by step to $x_0$ | a lower bound on $\log p_\theta(x)$ | [Forward process](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-forward-process), [objective](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective), [score matching](/notes/generative-models/score-matching), [continuous time](/notes/generative-models/continuous-time-diffusion), [latent diffusion](/notes/generative-models/latent-diffusion) |
| Autoregressive model | one value or token at a time, each given the previous ones | the exact $\log p_\theta(x)$ | [PixelRNN and PixelCNN](/notes/generative-models/autoregressive-models), [transformers](/notes/generative-models/autoregressive-transformers) |

Not every model gives a likelihood, and a likelihood does not always reflect how good samples look. Mix a good model $p$ with a model $q$ of white noise and sample from $0.01\,p + 0.99\,q$: 99% of the samples are noise. Since $q(x) \ge 0$ and the logarithm is increasing,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\big(0.01\,p(x) + 0.99\,q(x)\big)
&\ge \log\big(0.01\,p(x)\big) \\
&= \log p(x) - \ln 100 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The log-likelihood drops by at most $\ln 100 \approx 4.61$ nats per image, while the log-likelihoods of different models of $32 \times 32$ images can differ by thousands of nats ([Theis, van den Oord and Bethge, 2016](#references), §3.2). The course therefore also measures models from their samples alone, before it needs those measurements to compare GANs.

## What you should remember

- Data is a sample from an unknown $p_{\text{data}}$; a generative model is a $p_\theta$ we can sample from, fitted so that $p_\theta \approx p_{\text{data}}$.
- Minimizing $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta)$ is maximum likelihood, which needs $p_\theta(x)$ as a number.
- A family that can memorize the sample wins the likelihood and generalizes nothing; judge on held-out data.
- Sampling ladder: uniform numbers, inverse CDF, Box–Muller, an affine map, a learned nonlinear map.
- The families differ in what they can compute: a bound (VAE, diffusion), nothing (GAN), the likelihood up to a constant (EBM) or exactly (flows, autoregressive).
