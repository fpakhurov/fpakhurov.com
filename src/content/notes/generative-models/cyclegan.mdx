You have a thousand photos of horses and a thousand photos of zebras, and you want a network that repaints a given horse as a zebra: same pose, same field, stripes added. No photo shows the same horse as a zebra, so there is no target to compare an output with. The [previous note](/notes/generative-models/alpha-gan) mapped images to codes and back; here both sides are images.

This task is **image-to-image translation**. With paired examples it is supervised learning with a conditional GAN. **CycleGAN** ([Zhu et al., 2017](#references)) learns it from two unpaired collections, and it adds one idea to the GAN: translating an image to the other domain and back should return the original.

## Paired translation: pix2pix

Start with the easy case. Each training example is a pair $(x, y)$: an edge map $x$ and the photo $y$ it was drawn from, or a label map and its street scene. A generator $G$ maps $x$ to an image, and we need a loss that compares $G(x)$ with $y$.

A pixel loss alone produces blur. Where several outputs are plausible for one input, the minimizer of an expected $L_2$ loss is their per-pixel mean and that of an $L_1$ loss their per-pixel median ([conditional expectation note](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation#other-losses-other-targets)). Neither looks like any of the plausible outputs. **pix2pix** ([Isola et al., 2017](#references)) adds a [conditional](/notes/generative-models/gans#conditional-gan) adversarial loss. Its discriminator sees the pair $(x, y)$ or $(x, G(x))$ and decides whether the output is a real photo *for this input*:

$$
\begin{aligned}
G^* &= \arg\min_G \max_D\; \mathcal L_{\text{cGAN}}(G, D) + \lambda\, \mathcal L_{1}(G), \\
\mathcal L_{1}(G) &= \mathbb E_{x, y}\, \|y - G(x)\|_1 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\lambda = 100$. Here $\|a - b\|_1$ is the sum of absolute pixel differences; implementations average it over pixels, which only rescales $\lambda$. The adversarial term makes the output sharp, and the $L_1$ term keeps it near the true target.

The generator is a [U-Net](/notes/deep-learning/semantic-segmentation#u-net-an-encoder-a-decoder-and-skip-connections). The discriminator is a **PatchGAN**: it outputs a grid of verdicts, one per overlapping $70 \times 70$ patch, and the loss averages them. CycleGAN keeps the PatchGAN and replaces the U-Net by a residual generator.

## Without pairs, the adversarial loss is not enough

Now take two domains $X$ and $Y$ with data distributions $P_X$ and $P_Y$, and a generator $G: X \to Y$. Without pairs, the only signal is adversarial: a discriminator $D_Y$ tells real $y \sim P_Y$ from translated $G(x)$. That loss depends on $G$ only through the distribution of its outputs. Every $G$ that pushes $P_X$ onto $P_Y$ is optimal, whatever it does to an individual image; a network with enough capacity can send the horse photos to any permutation of zebra photos. In practice the adversarial loss alone also tends to collapse, mapping all inputs to the same few outputs ([Zhu et al., 2017](#references)).

A one-dimensional model makes this concrete. Let "horses" be $x \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and "zebras" $y \sim \mathcal N(3, 0.5^2)$. Three maps give outputs with exactly the zebra distribution: the increasing line $G(x) = 3 + 0.5x$, the decreasing line $G(x) = 3 - 0.5x$, and, for contrast, a sampler $G(x, \varepsilon) = 3 + 0.5\,\varepsilon$ that draws fresh noise $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ and ignores $x$.

<CycleToy />

A discriminator cannot tell the three apart. Only the first is the translation we want; the third has thrown away everything about its input. CycleGAN's generators are deterministic, so the sampler is outside its class, but a deterministic $G$ can also forget its input, by mapping many inputs to one output. The cycle loss punishes both in the same way.

## Cycle consistency

Add a second generator $F: Y \to X$ and a second discriminator $D_X$ that tells real $x$ from $F(y)$. If $G$ and $F$ translate in opposite directions, each should undo the other: $F(G(x)) \approx x$, the **forward cycle**, and $G(F(y)) \approx y$, the **backward cycle**. The **cycle-consistency loss** measures both:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\text{cyc}}(G, F) = \;&\mathbb E_{x \sim P_X}\, \|F(G(x)) - x\|_1 \\
+\;&\mathbb E_{y \sim P_Y}\, \|G(F(y)) - y\|_1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

What does it rule out? A zero forward-cycle loss means $F(G(x)) = x$ for every $x$. Then $G$ is injective: if two inputs had the same output, $F$ could return only one of them. A translation that forgets its input cannot be undone.

In the toy, the sampler's output is independent of $x$, so $F$ sees nothing about $x$ and its best constant guess under the $L_1$ loss is the median of $x$, zero. The cycle error is then

$$
\mathbb E\,|x - 0| = \sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.80 .
$$

The two lines are invertible, with $F(y) = 2(y - 3)$ and $F(y) = -2(y - 3)$, and their cycle error is zero.

What does it not rule out? The decreasing line reverses the order of its inputs and is as cycle-consistent as the increasing one. The cycle requires that the translation keep enough information to be undone; it does not say which invertible correspondence is the right one. For images, the architecture makes that choice. A generator built from residual blocks (below) represents the identity map easily and changes images locally, so the solution it reaches keeps the layout and changes texture and colour: stripes on the same horse in the same pose. When two domains admit several such correspondences, the network can pick a wrong one; on photo → label maps, CycleGAN sometimes swaps the labels of trees and buildings ([Zhu et al., 2017](#references)).

<aside class="callout"><strong>Two autoencoders.</strong> $F \circ G$ maps $X$ to $X$ through an image of $Y$, so CycleGAN trains two autoencoders whose codes are images of the other domain. The cycle loss is their reconstruction loss, and the adversarial loss makes the codes look like real images of $Y$, as the code discriminator of an <a href="/notes/generative-models/alpha-gan">adversarial autoencoder</a> matches a prior.</aside>

## Identity loss

Painting → photo translation showed one more freedom. The generators changed the tint of an image when nothing required it: a daytime Monet often came out as a sunset photo, and both the adversarial and the cycle loss were satisfied. The **identity loss** ([Taigman, Polyak and Wolf, 2017](#references)) asks each generator to leave an image of its own target domain unchanged:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L_{\text{id}}(G, F) = \;&\mathbb E_{y \sim P_Y}\, \|G(y) - y\|_1 \\
+\;&\mathbb E_{x \sim P_X}\, \|F(x) - x\|_1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

A zebra fed to the horse → zebra generator is already a zebra, so the output should be the input. This removes global colour shifts that neither domain asks for. The paper uses the term for paintings and photo enhancement; common implementations use it everywhere.

## The full objective

<CycleFlow />

The losses combine into one objective,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(G, F, D_X, D_Y) = \;&\mathcal L_{\text{GAN}}(G, D_Y) + \mathcal L_{\text{GAN}}(F, D_X) \\
+\;&\lambda_{\text{cyc}}\, \mathcal L_{\text{cyc}}(G, F) + \lambda_{\text{id}}\, \mathcal L_{\text{id}}(G, F),
\end{aligned}
$$

minimized over the generators and maximized over the discriminators, with $\lambda_{\text{cyc}} = 10$ and $\lambda_{\text{id}} = 0.5\,\lambda_{\text{cyc}} = 5$.

Both cycles are needed. With only the forward cycle or only the backward one, training was often unstable and collapsed in the direction whose cycle was removed ([Zhu et al., 2017](#references)).

## The networks

The generator follows the style-transfer network of [Johnson, Alahi and Fei-Fei (2016)](#references). For $256 \times 256$ images it has a $7 \times 7$ convolution to 64 channels, two stride-2 convolutions down to $64 \times 64 \times 256$, nine residual blocks at that size, two transposed convolutions back to $256 \times 256 \times 64$, and a $7 \times 7$ convolution to 3 channels with $\tanh$. Images of $128 \times 128$ use six residual blocks.

Each residual block computes $h + r(h)$, where $r$ is two $3 \times 3$ convolutions. A block whose $r$ is near zero passes its input through, which is the bias towards small, local changes described above. Convolutions are followed by [instance normalization](/notes/generative-models/training-background#instance-normalization), the natural choice with one image per batch, and reflection padding avoids dark borders.

The discriminator is a $70 \times 70$ PatchGAN: four $4 \times 4$ convolutions with 64, 128, 256 and 512 channels (strides 2, 2, 2, 1), then a $4 \times 4$ convolution to one channel. On a $256 \times 256$ image it outputs a $30 \times 30$ map of scores. Each score sees a $70 \times 70$ window of the input, its [receptive field](/notes/deep-learning/convolution#how-far-a-unit-sees-the-receptive-field). With kernel sizes $k_\ell = 4$ and strides $s = (2, 2, 2, 1, 1)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= 1 + \sum_\ell (k_\ell - 1) \prod_{m < \ell} s_m \\
&= 1 + 3\,(1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 8) = 70 .
\end{aligned}
$$

A patch discriminator judges local texture, which is what most translations change, and it runs on images of any size. Global structure is left mostly to the generator and the cycle loss. The 900 scores are not independent verdicts: they overlap, and the normalization layers share image-wide statistics.

## Training

The adversarial terms use the [least-squares loss](/notes/generative-models/gans#least-squares-gan) of [Mao et al. (2017)](#references) instead of the log-likelihood. $D_Y$ minimizes $\mathbb E\,(D_Y(y) - 1)^2 + \mathbb E\,D_Y(G(x))^2$, and $G$ minimizes $\mathbb E\,(D_Y(G(x)) - 1)^2$. The squared loss is flat only at $D_Y = 1$, so the generator still gets a gradient for outputs that the discriminator scores beyond 1, on the real side but away from the data; the [GAN note](/notes/generative-models/gans#least-squares-gan) compares this with the vanishing slope of the cross-entropy. Mao et al. report that training is also more stable.

Each discriminator is updated on fakes drawn from a buffer of the 50 most recent generated images ([Shrivastava et al., 2017](#references)). Once the buffer is full, each new fake replaces a random stored one with probability $\tfrac12$, and the stored one is shown instead. A discriminator that sees only the current generator can chase it in circles; one that remembers earlier generators damps the oscillation.

The optimizer is Adam with learning rate $2\cdot10^{-4}$ and batch size 1: 100 epochs at a constant rate, then 100 with the rate decaying linearly to zero. The paper also halves the discriminator's objective to slow it down. Adam divides each step by a running estimate of the gradient's scale, so a constant factor on a loss largely cancels in the update.

## Results

Two evaluations from the paper show what the cycle buys and what it leaves out. In a perceptual study on maps ↔ aerial photos, raters took CycleGAN's outputs for real images in 26.8% of trials for map → photo; earlier unpaired methods fooled them in at most 2.1% ([Zhu et al., 2017](#references), Table 1).

Faithfulness is measured on Cityscapes labels → photo: a pretrained segmentation network is run on the generated photos and compared with the input labels. CycleGAN reaches a class IoU of 0.11 against 0.18 for pix2pix trained on pairs. The adversarial loss alone gives 0.08, and the cycle loss alone 0.02 ([Zhu et al., 2017](#references), Tables 2 and 4).

## Where it breaks \{#limitations\}

The cycle asks that $x$ be recoverable from $G(x)$. It does not ask that the information be *visible*. When one domain carries more detail than the other, the generator must store the surplus somewhere, and it hides it.

Photo → map translation shows this. A map draws a roof as a flat grey area; the aerial photo shows dots, vents and shadows on it. Many photos have the same map, so a faithful map cannot be undone, and the forward cycle $F(G(x)) = x$ demands that $G$ encode the roof's details in its map anyway. Chu, Zhmoginov and Sandler (2017) found exactly that: the generated maps carry a low-amplitude, high-frequency signal, invisible until the contrast is equalized, and $F$ rebuilds the roof details from it.

A toy version, with pixels in $[0, 1]$, shows why such a solution is both invisible and fragile. Let the roof be grey, 0.5, on the true map, and let the photo carry one detail $d \in [-1, 1]$. The generator writes it at the amplitude $a$ of one 8-bit grey level, and $F$ reads it back:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a &= 1/256, \\
G(x) &= 0.5 + a\, d, \\
\hat d &= \big(G(x) - 0.5\big) / a = d .
\end{aligned}
$$

The cycle loss is zero, and the map differs from a real one by at most one grey level. Now add noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ to the map, as JPEG compression or a camera would. The decoded detail becomes $d + \varepsilon/a$, with an error of standard deviation $\sigma/a$. Chu et al. saw the reconstruction change almost completely once $\sigma$ reached about $3/256$; in the toy that is an error of standard deviation 3 on a detail whose whole range is 2. The cycle is consistent, the visible map has lost the detail, and the inverse holds only on the exact outputs of $G$.

Other failures come from the same objective. Changes of shape mostly fail: on dog → cat the learned map makes minimal changes to the input. Trained on horse and zebra photos without riders, the model paints stripes on the rider too. And the faithfulness scores above stay well below those of paired training. Both generators are deterministic, so a horse has exactly one zebra version, and covering $k$ domains takes $k(k-1)/2$ separate CycleGANs.

## What changed

| | pix2pix | CycleGAN |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Training data | aligned pairs $(x, y)$ | two unpaired collections |
| Networks | $G$; $D(x, y)$ sees the input | $G$, $F$, $D_X$, $D_Y$; each $D$ sees one domain |
| Tie to the input | $L_1$ to the true target, $\lambda = 100$ | two cycles, $\lambda_{\text{cyc}} = 10$; identity loss |
| Adversarial loss | conditional, log-likelihood | one per domain, least squares |
| Generator | U-Net | ResNet with 9 residual blocks |

## What you should remember

- Without pairs, an adversarial loss fixes only the distribution of outputs; a map that ignores its input can be optimal.
- $\|F(G(x)) - x\|_1 + \|G(F(y)) - y\|_1$ makes each generator injective, so it keeps the input's information.
- The cycle does not choose among invertible maps; residual generators prefer small local changes.
- The identity loss $\|G(y) - y\|_1$ stops gratuitous colour shifts; $\lambda_{\text{cyc}} = 10$, $\lambda_{\text{id}} = 5$.
- A $70 \times 70$ PatchGAN judges texture patch by patch; global layout comes from the generator.
- A domain with less detail forces the generator to hide the surplus in invisible, fragile signals.

## Notebook

The [CycleGAN notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/spring-2025-long/seminars/04_Image-to-Image/CycleGAN.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* course runs and trains CycleGAN on horse2zebra with the networks of the [CycleGAN-PyTorch](https://github.com/Lornatang/CycleGAN-PyTorch) package, pinned to one commit. Cell numbers count every cell, markdown included, from 1. Only cell 16 has a saved output, five zebra → horse translations by the pretrained generator; the training loop was not run.

- The method (cells 1–2): the losses and the full objective, written with $\log D$, while the code uses the least-squares form.
- Setup and data (cells 3–11): downloads the dataset and pretrained weights for all four networks; images are resized to 286, randomly cropped to 256 and flipped, and `unaligned=True` pairs each horse with a random zebra. `args.cuda = False` keeps everything on the CPU even on a GPU machine.
- Models and inference (cells 12–18): the generator and PatchGAN described above, with the pretrained weights loaded, translating five images each way. `Discriminator.forward` averages the $30 \times 30$ score map before the loss, which gives $(\bar s - 1)^2$ instead of the per-patch mean $(\bar s - 1)^2 + \operatorname{Var}_p(s_p)$, so only the average patch must look real.
- Losses and training (cells 19–22): $L_1$ cycle and identity losses with weights 10 and 5, `MSELoss` for the adversarial terms, Adam with betas $(0.5, 0.999)$, linear decay after epoch 100 and two buffers of 50 fakes. The loop fine-tunes the pretrained weights loaded in cell 13, not a fresh model, and its loss lists are never filled.
