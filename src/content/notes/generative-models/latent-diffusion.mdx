The [DDPM](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective) and [score matching](/notes/generative-models/score-matching) notes describe one model that denoises images step by step. Every step runs a network over every pixel, and a sample needs tens to hundreds of steps. Most of that work is spent on details nobody sees: the exact texture of grass or the noise of a camera sensor. **Latent diffusion** ([Rombach et al., 2022](#references)) splits the problem in two. An autoencoder removes the imperceptible detail once; the diffusion model works only on the small latent that remains and spends its capacity on what an image shows. Stable Diffusion is this design with a text encoder attached.

**Which Stable Diffusion.** The constants below belong to named checkpoints, not to latent diffusion in general. The $512 \times 512$ numbers, the latent scale 0.18215 and the 77-token text encoders are those of Stable Diffusion 1.x and 2.x, and the guidance scale 7.5 is the default of their Diffusers pipeline. SDXL, a later model, has a larger U-Net and two text encoders ([Podell et al., 2023](#references)); its released autoencoder uses a latent scale of 0.13025 and its Diffusers pipeline a default guidance of 5.0.

**Notation.** An image $x \in \mathbb R^{H \times W \times 3}$, an encoder $\mathcal E$ and a decoder $\mathcal D$ with $z = \mathcal E(x) \in \mathbb R^{h \times w \times c}$, a downsampling factor $f = H/h = W/w$. A condition $y$ (a prompt) and its encoder $\tau(y)$. The diffusion quantities $z_t$, $\bar\alpha_t$, $\varepsilon_\theta$ are those of the DDPM notes, applied to $z$ instead of $x$.

## Two kinds of compression

A trained image model compresses in two senses. **Perceptual compression** throws away high-frequency detail that changes many bits of the image and almost nothing of what a person perceives. **Semantic compression** captures the layout and content of the image: which objects, where, in what style. A likelihood-based model in pixel space is charged for every bit, and the paper shows that most of those bits belong to the perceptual part. Latent diffusion assigns each part to a model suited to it: an autoencoder trained with perceptual and adversarial losses for the first, a diffusion model for the second.

The savings are large. Stable Diffusion at $512 \times 512$ uses $f = 8$ and $c = 4$: the image has $512 \cdot 512 \cdot 3 = 786{,}432$ numbers and the latent $64 \cdot 64 \cdot 4 = 16{,}384$, 48 times fewer. Attention grows faster still. Self-attention over a $64 \times 64$ grid compares $4096^2 \approx 1.7 \cdot 10^7$ pairs; over the $512 \times 512$ pixels it would compare $262{,}144^2 \approx 6.9 \cdot 10^{10}$, 4096 times more, which is why pixel-space models attend only at coarse resolutions. Memory matters as much as time: one $4096 \times 4096$ attention map already has $1.7 \cdot 10^7$ entries, 67 MB in 32-bit floats, for every head and every image of a batch. Libraries can compute attention in slices, a few heads or images at a time, which gives the same result with a lower peak memory at some cost in speed.

## The autoencoder

The first stage is trained once and then frozen. Its loss combines a reconstruction term, a **perceptual loss** that compares deep features of the reconstruction and the image, as [LPIPS](/notes/generative-models/lpips) does, and a **patch-based adversarial loss** whose discriminator judges small patches, so that reconstructions stay sharp instead of averaging to blur. The latent is kept close to a simple distribution in one of two ways: a slight KL penalty toward $\mathcal N(0, I)$, as in a [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) with a very small weight on the KL term, or a vector-quantization layer, as in [VQ-VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae-variants#vq-vae-a-discrete-latent-code). Unlike a VAE that is used as a generator, this autoencoder is not asked to make prior samples look good: the diffusion model provides the prior.

The downsampling factor sets the split. With $f = 1$ the diffusion model still does all the perceptual work and trains slowly; with $f = 32$ the autoencoder loses information the decoder cannot restore, and quality stops improving. The paper finds $f$ between 4 and 16 a good balance and $f = 4$ and $f = 8$ best. Because the KL penalty is weak, the latents of Stable Diffusion have a variance different from 1; the code multiplies them by a fixed scale, 0.18215 for versions 1.x and 2.x, so that the diffusion starts from data of roughly unit variance.

## Diffusion in the latent

Training is the DDPM objective with $z$ in place of $x$:

$$
\mathcal L = \mathbb E_{z_0 = \mathcal E(x),\ \varepsilon,\ t}\Big[\big\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, z_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t,\ \tau(y)\big)\big\rVert^2\Big] .
$$

Generation draws $z_T \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, runs the reverse chain in the latent space and decodes once at the end, $\hat x = \mathcal D(z_0)$. The network is a [U-Net](/notes/deep-learning/semantic-segmentation#u-net-an-encoder-a-decoder-and-skip-connections), convolutional with attention layers at the lower resolutions.

### Conditioning through cross-attention

The condition enters through **cross-attention** ([attention note](/notes/nlp/attention)): the queries come from the U-Net features of $z_t$, the keys and values from the encoded condition,

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\Big(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt d}\Big) V, \qquad Q = W_Q\, \varphi(z_t), \quad K = W_K\, \tau(y), \quad V = W_V\, \tau(y),
$$

where $\varphi(z_t)$ are flattened intermediate features. Every spatial position of the latent attends over the tokens of the prompt, so different regions can follow different words. In Stable Diffusion $\tau$ is a frozen text encoder that turns a prompt into 77 token vectors: [CLIP](/notes/deep-learning/vision-transformers#clip-contrastive-pretraining-on-images-and-captions) ViT-L/14 with 768 dimensions in versions 1.x, OpenCLIP ViT-H/14 with 1024 in versions 2.x. The same mechanism accepts other conditions, such as class labels, segmentation maps or low-resolution images.

**Prompt weighting.** Every token reaches the U-Net as its own vector $\tau(y)_i$, so a concept can be emphasised or toned down after encoding, without retraining. A common rule moves the token's vector away from, or toward, the vector at the same position for an empty prompt,

$$
\tau(y)_i \;\leftarrow\; \tau(\varnothing)_i + w_i\,\big(\tau(y)_i - \tau(\varnothing)_i\big),
$$

with $w_i > 1$ to boost the concept and $w_i < 1$ to suppress it. The change acts through cross-attention: the token's key changes how much the latent attends to it, and its value what the latent reads from it. The [Compel](#references) library writes weights as suffixes, each `+` a factor $1.1$ and each `-` a factor $0.9$: `ball++` has weight $1.1^2 = 1.21$, `ball--` $0.9^2 = 0.81$, and `forest---` $0.9^3 = 0.729$. For weights below $1$ it also blends the result with the encoding of the prompt in which those tokens are masked out.

## Classifier-free guidance

A conditional model sampled as it is follows the prompt only loosely. **Classifier-free guidance** ([Ho and Salimans, 2022](#references)) strengthens it without a separate classifier. During training the condition is replaced by an empty one in a fraction of the steps, 10 or 20 % in the paper, so one network learns both $\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, y)$ and $\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \varnothing)$. At sampling time the two predictions are extrapolated:

$$
\tilde\varepsilon = \varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \varnothing) + s\,\big(\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, y) - \varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \varnothing)\big) .
$$

$s = 0$ is unconditional, $s = 1$ the plain conditional model, and $s > 1$ moves beyond it; the Diffusers pipeline for Stable Diffusion 1.x and 2.x uses $s = 7.5$ by default. Since the noise prediction is a scaled score, $s_\theta = -\varepsilon_\theta / \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ ([score matching note](/notes/generative-models/score-matching#the-same-model-as-ddpm)), the guided prediction is the score

$$
(1 - s)\, \nabla \log p(z) + s\, \nabla \log p(z \mid y) = \nabla \log\big[p(z)^{1 - s}\, p(z \mid y)^{s}\big],
$$

and by Bayes' rule $p(z \mid y) \propto p(y \mid z)\, p(z)$ this is $\nabla \log p(z) + s\, \nabla \log p(y \mid z)$: the unconditional score plus $s$ times the gradient of an implicit classifier.

**A score at each noise level, not a target distribution.** The identity holds separately at every step, with the noised marginals $q_t$ in place of $p$: the guided prediction is the score of $r_t(z) \propto q_t(z)^{1-s}\, q_t(z \mid y)^{s}$. For $s = 0$ and $s = 1$ the family $r_t$ is the noising of one distribution. For other $s$ it is in general not: noising $p^{1-s}\, p(\cdot \mid y)^{s}$ does not give $r_t$, and no other single distribution does either. In the worked example below, every $r_t$ is a Gaussian, and the clean distribution it would have to come from has mean $2.40$ at the last step but $2.58$ at $t = 0.2$, with time running from $0$ (data) to $1$ (noise). A sampler driven by the guided score therefore does not in general end at $p^{1-s}\, p(\cdot \mid y)^{s}$, and the stochastic DDPM sampler and the deterministic DDIM sampler of the [next section](#fewer-steps) end at different distributions with the same network ([Bradley and Nakkiran, 2024](#references)). What the identity gives is the direction of each step: toward regions that the implicit classifier at that noise level favours.

**Worked example.** Take an unconditional $p = \mathcal N(0, 4)$ and a conditional $p(\cdot \mid y) = \mathcal N(2, 1)$. At the last step the guided score is the score of $p^{1-s}\, p(\cdot \mid y)^{s}$, a Gaussian with precision $(1 - s)/4 + s$ and mean $2s$ divided by that precision. At $s = 3$ the precision is $2.5$, so the standard deviation is $0.63$ and the mean $2.4$; at $s = 7.5$ the precision is $5.875$, the standard deviation $0.41$ and the mean $2.55$. The samplers end elsewhere. With exact Gaussian scores, so that no network error enters, and the continuous-time version of DDPM's linear schedule ($\beta$ from $0.1$ to $20$), guided sampling at $s = 3$ ends at mean $2.63$ and standard deviation $0.52$ with the stochastic sampler and at mean $3.00$ and standard deviation $0.25$ with the deterministic one; at $s = 7.5$ at $2.67$ and $0.31$, and at $2.88$ and $0.01$. The direction agrees with the tilted density, more strongly: guidance narrows the distribution and pushes it away from the unconditional mean, past the conditional one. Samples become more typical of the prompt and less diverse, the precision side of the trade-off from the [precision and recall note](/notes/generative-models/precision-recall#where-the-trade-off-appears). Each step costs two network evaluations, one with the prompt and one without.

## Fewer steps

DDPM samples with as many steps as it was trained with, 1000, because each step undoes one small Gaussian step. Fast samplers start from a deterministic description of the same model. In continuous time the forward process is a stochastic differential equation, and [Song et al. (2021)](#references) show that the **probability flow ODE**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dz}{dt} &= -\tfrac12\,\beta(t)\,\big[z + \nabla_z \log q_t(z)\big] \\
&= -\tfrac12\,\beta(t)\,\Big[z - \frac{\varepsilon_\theta(z, t)}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\Big]
\end{aligned}
$$

has the same marginal $q_t$ as the noising process at every time. Here $\beta(t)$ is the noise rate in continuous time, with $\beta_t \approx \beta(t)\,\Delta t$ for one discrete step and $d\bar\alpha_t/dt = -\beta(t)\,\bar\alpha_t$, and the score is replaced by the scaled noise predictor ([score matching note](/notes/generative-models/score-matching#the-same-model-as-ddpm)). Integrating the ODE from $z_T \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ down to $t = 0$ maps every noise latent to one sample, and a solver that takes large steps needs few network evaluations.

**DDIM is Euler's method in the right coordinates.** Divide the latent by its signal coefficient and measure time by the noise-to-signal ratio,

$$
\bar z = \frac{z}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}, \qquad \sigma_t = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_t}},
$$

so that $\bar z = z_0 + \sigma_t\,\varepsilon$. Differentiating $\bar z$ with $d\bar\alpha_t/dt = -\beta(t)\,\bar\alpha_t$, the terms in $z$ cancel, and $d\bar z/dt$ and $d\sigma_t/dt$ both carry the factor $\beta(t)/\big(2\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_t)}\big)$. The ODE becomes $d\bar z/d\sigma = \varepsilon_\theta$ ([Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021](#references), §4.3, Eq. 14), with the exact solution from $\sigma_t$ to an earlier, smaller $\sigma_s$

$$
\bar z_s = \bar z_t + \int_{\sigma_t}^{\sigma_s} \varepsilon_\theta\, d\sigma .
$$

Euler's method holds $\varepsilon_\theta$ at its value at $\sigma_t$ for the whole step. Multiplying back by $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}$ gives the **DDIM** update (their Eq. 12 with no added noise):

$$
\begin{gathered}
z_s = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}\;\hat z_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_s}\;\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t), \\
\hat z_0 = \frac{z_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}} .
\end{gathered}
$$

Predict the clean latent, then noise it again to the level of step $s$ with the same predicted noise. The step $s$ can be any earlier step, so a 50-step schedule simply visits every twentieth $t$. The Euler sampler of diffusion libraries, after [Karras et al. (2022)](#references), makes this same update with its own choice of noise levels; its "ancestral" variant adds fresh noise after each step. DDIM is deterministic and maps each noise latent to one image. Running its update in the other direction, from the image toward noise, gives an approximate **inversion**: the ideal ODE can be integrated both ways, but every Euler step evaluates $\varepsilon_\theta$ at the start of the step, so the backward pass does not exactly undo the forward one. The error shrinks with more steps and grows with model error and with guidance.

**Worked example.** One-dimensional data $\mathcal N(0, 0.5^2)$, the exact noise predictor, DDPM's linear schedule, $z_T$ drawn from its exact distribution and DDIM steps evenly spaced in $t$. The samples should have standard deviation $0.5$. With 1 step they have $0.002$, with 10 steps $0.370$, with 50 steps $0.471$ and with 1000 steps $0.4985$. A single step from pure noise returns $\hat z_0$, the posterior mean $\mathbb E[z_0 \mid z_T]$, which is close to the average of all data; too few first-order steps shrink the samples toward that average. The same setting shows that inversion is approximate even with an exact noise predictor: inverting the point $0.8$ with DDIM steps and generating back returns $0.42$ with 10 steps, $0.71$ with 50 and $0.80$ with 1000.

**DPM-Solver: integrate the known part exactly.** In the variable $\lambda = \log\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha}/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha}\big) = -\log\sigma$, half the log signal-to-noise ratio, the same exact solution reads $\bar z_s = \bar z_t - \int_{\lambda_t}^{\lambda_s} e^{-\lambda}\,\hat\varepsilon_\theta(\lambda)\,d\lambda$ ([Lu et al., 2022a](#references), Eq. 3.4). DPM-Solver expands $\hat\varepsilon_\theta$ in a Taylor series in $\lambda$ around the start of each step and integrates the exponential weight exactly. Its first-order version is DDIM (their §4.1); the second- and third-order versions spend two or three network evaluations per step but need far fewer steps. **DPM-Solver++** ([Lu et al., 2022b](#references)) applies the same idea to the predicted clean latent $\hat z_0$ instead of the noise and uses a multistep second-order update that reuses the evaluation of the previous step. Earlier high-order solvers became unstable at the large guidance scales of text-to-image models, even slower than DDIM; the multistep form reduces the effective step size and keeps them stable.

**How many steps.** The numbers depend on guidance. Without it, DPM-Solver produces high-quality samples in 10 to 20 network evaluations, with FID 4.70 at 10 and 2.87 at 20 on CIFAR-10 (Lu et al., 2022a). With guidance, Lu et al. (2022b) find that DDIM generally needs 100 to 250 steps to converge, while DPM-Solver++ gives high-quality samples in 15 to 20 steps, for pixel-space and latent-space models alike. On Stable Diffusion with guidance scale 7.5, DPM-Solver++ reaches with 15 to 20 steps the latent error that the default 50-step sampler (PNDM) has; in the same test every solver already gave an FID of about 15 to 16 at 10 steps, because the decoder turns a latent that has not quite converged into a good image (their §7.2). The network does not change; only the update rule between its evaluations does, which is why diffusion libraries let the sampler (the **scheduler**) be swapped after training.

## Editing with the same model

- **Image-to-image** (SDEdit, [Meng et al., 2022](#references)). Encode an existing image, add noise to an intermediate step, $z_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, z_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$, and run the remaining denoising steps with a new prompt. The **strength** $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ chooses the step: with 40 sampling steps and $\lambda = 0.6$, the last 24 are run. Low strength keeps the composition; strength 1 ignores the input.
- **Inpainting.** To change only a masked region, at every step the latent outside the mask is replaced by the original latent noised to the same step, and only the masked part follows the model: $z_{t-1} \leftarrow (1 - m) \odot z^{\text{orig}}_{t-1} + m \odot z^*_{t-1}$, where $m$ is the mask downsampled to the latent grid (1 where the image may change), $z^{\text{orig}}_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, z_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\varepsilon$ and $z^*_{t-1}$ is the sampler's update. With $f = 8$ the mask is applied on $8 \times 8$ pixel blocks, so its edge is blended by the decoder rather than followed exactly. Dedicated inpainting models instead receive the mask and the masked image as extra input channels and are trained for the task.
- **LoRA.** Fine-tune the attention layers with low-rank updates $W + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A$ ([PEFT note](/notes/nlp/peft#lora-a-low-rank-update)) to teach a style or a subject from a small dataset; the update can be fused into the weights for inference. For a $d \times k$ weight it trains $r(d + k)$ numbers instead of $dk$: with $d = k = 1280$ that is $0.6\%$ of the layer for $r = 4$ and $5\%$ for $r = 32$.
- **Textual inversion** ([Gal et al., 2023](#references)). Freeze everything and learn only the embedding $v$ of a new token, such as `<S*>`, by minimizing the diffusion loss on three to five images of a concept, $v^* = \arg\min_v \mathbb E_{z_0, y, \varepsilon, t}\, \lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \tau(y_v))\rVert^2$ (their Eq. 2), where $y_v$ is a template prompt such as "a photo of `<S*>`" with $v$ in place of the token's embedding. The token can then be used in any prompt.

## What changed

| | Pixel-space diffusion | Latent diffusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Space of the diffusion | pixels, $512 \cdot 512 \cdot 3$ | latent, $64 \cdot 64 \cdot 4$ |
| Who removes imperceptible detail | the diffusion model, slowly | the autoencoder, once |
| Attention | only at coarse resolutions | affordable over the whole latent grid |
| Conditioning | possible, costly | cross-attention to a text encoder |
| Number of steps (set by the sampler, not the space) | same samplers: about 1000 for DDPM, about 50 with DDIM in common use (100 to 250 to converge with guidance), 15 to 20 with DPM-Solver++ | same |
| Cost of one step | a U-Net pass over $512 \cdot 512 \cdot 3$ numbers, twice with guidance | a pass over $64 \cdot 64 \cdot 4$ numbers, twice with guidance, plus one decoder pass per image |
| Ceiling on detail | the network | the decoder of the autoencoder |

## Limitations

- **The decoder bounds the detail.** Fine text, faces at small scale and exact textures depend on what the autoencoder can reconstruct; the diffusion model cannot add what $\mathcal D$ cannot express.
- **Guidance is a trade-off.** Large $s$ improves prompt adherence and typicality at the cost of diversity and, beyond some value, of image quality.
- **The text encoder limits understanding.** A frozen CLIP-like encoder with 77 tokens struggles with counting, spatial relations and long prompts.
- **Still iterative.** Tens of network evaluations per image remain, twice that with guidance, and too few steps of a first-order sampler shrink the samples toward their average.
- **Memory.** Attention over the latent grid needs memory quadratic in the number of positions; slicing trades speed for memory.
- **Data.** The models are trained on large scraped image–text collections, with the questions of consent, copyright and bias that come with them.

## Summary

- An autoencoder removes perceptual detail once; diffusion runs in its latent, 48 times smaller than the image for Stable Diffusion at $512 \times 512$.
- The autoencoder is trained with reconstruction, perceptual and patch-adversarial losses and a weak KL or VQ regularization; $f$ between 4 and 16 balances speed and fidelity.
- Text enters the U-Net through cross-attention: queries from the latent, keys and values from the text encoder. Per-token weights move token vectors away from or toward those of an empty prompt.
- Classifier-free guidance extrapolates from the unconditional to the conditional prediction. At each noise level this is the score of $q_t^{1-s}\, q_t(\cdot \mid y)^s$, but the samples do not in general follow $p^{1-s}\, p(\cdot \mid y)^s$, and DDPM and DDIM sampling end at different distributions; both narrow the distribution toward the prompt.
- Samplers integrate the probability flow ODE of the same model: DDIM is Euler's method in $(\bar z, \sigma)$ coordinates, DPM-Solver integrates the linear part exactly and expands the network in $\lambda$; with guidance DPM-Solver++ needs 15 to 20 steps. Image-to-image, inpainting, LoRA and textual inversion reuse the model for editing.
