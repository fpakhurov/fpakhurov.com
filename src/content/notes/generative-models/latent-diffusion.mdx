The [DDPM](/notes/generative-models/ddpm-training-objective) and [score matching](/notes/generative-models/score-matching) notes describe one model that denoises images step by step. Every step runs a network over every pixel, and a sample needs tens to hundreds of steps. Most of that work is spent on details nobody sees: the exact texture of grass or the noise of a camera sensor. **Latent diffusion** ([Rombach et al., 2022](#references)) splits the problem in two. An autoencoder removes the imperceptible detail once; the diffusion model works only on the small latent that remains and spends its capacity on what an image shows. Stable Diffusion is this design with a text encoder attached.

**Notation.** An image $x \in \mathbb R^{H \times W \times 3}$, an encoder $\mathcal E$ and a decoder $\mathcal D$ with $z = \mathcal E(x) \in \mathbb R^{h \times w \times c}$, a downsampling factor $f = H/h = W/w$. A condition $y$ (a prompt) and its encoder $\tau(y)$. The diffusion quantities $z_t$, $\bar\alpha_t$, $\varepsilon_\theta$ are those of the DDPM notes, applied to $z$ instead of $x$.

## Two kinds of compression

A trained image model compresses in two senses. **Perceptual compression** throws away high-frequency detail that changes many bits of the image and almost nothing of what a person perceives. **Semantic compression** captures the layout and content of the image: which objects, where, in what style. A likelihood-based model in pixel space is charged for every bit, and the paper shows that most of those bits belong to the perceptual part. Latent diffusion assigns each part to a model suited to it: an autoencoder trained with perceptual and adversarial losses for the first, a diffusion model for the second.

The savings are large. Stable Diffusion at $512 \times 512$ uses $f = 8$ and $c = 4$: the image has $512 \cdot 512 \cdot 3 = 786{,}432$ numbers and the latent $64 \cdot 64 \cdot 4 = 16{,}384$, 48 times fewer. Attention grows faster still. Self-attention over a $64 \times 64$ grid compares $4096^2 \approx 1.7 \cdot 10^7$ pairs; over the $512 \times 512$ pixels it would compare $262{,}144^2 \approx 6.9 \cdot 10^{10}$, 4096 times more, which is why pixel-space models attend only at coarse resolutions.

## The autoencoder

The first stage is trained once and then frozen. Its loss combines a reconstruction term, a **perceptual loss** that compares deep features of the reconstruction and the image, as [LPIPS](/notes/generative-models/lpips) does, and a **patch-based adversarial loss** whose discriminator judges small patches, so that reconstructions stay sharp instead of averaging to blur. The latent is kept close to a simple distribution in one of two ways: a slight KL penalty toward $\mathcal N(0, I)$, as in a [VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae) with a very small weight on the KL term, or a vector-quantization layer, as in [VQ-VAE](/notes/generative-models/vae-variants#vq-vae-a-discrete-latent-code). Unlike a VAE that is used as a generator, this autoencoder is not asked to make prior samples look good: the diffusion model provides the prior.

The downsampling factor sets the split. With $f = 1$ the diffusion model still does all the perceptual work and trains slowly; with $f = 32$ the autoencoder loses information the decoder cannot restore, and quality stops improving. The paper finds $f$ between 4 and 16 a good balance and $f = 4$ and $f = 8$ best. Because the KL penalty is weak, the latents of Stable Diffusion have a variance different from 1; the code multiplies them by a fixed scale, 0.18215, so that the diffusion starts from data of roughly unit variance.

## Diffusion in the latent

Training is the DDPM objective with $z$ in place of $x$:

$$
\mathcal L = \mathbb E_{z_0 = \mathcal E(x),\ \varepsilon,\ t}\Big[\big\lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, z_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t,\ \tau(y)\big)\big\rVert^2\Big] .
$$

Generation draws $z_T \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, runs the reverse chain in the latent space and decodes once at the end, $\hat x = \mathcal D(z_0)$. The network is a U-Net, convolutional with attention layers at the lower resolutions.

### Conditioning through cross-attention

The condition enters through **cross-attention** ([attention note](/notes/nlp/attention)): the queries come from the U-Net features of $z_t$, the keys and values from the encoded condition,

$$
\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\Big(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt d}\Big) V, \qquad Q = W_Q\, \varphi(z_t), \quad K = W_K\, \tau(y), \quad V = W_V\, \tau(y),
$$

where $\varphi(z_t)$ are flattened intermediate features. Every spatial position of the latent attends over the tokens of the prompt, so different regions can follow different words. In Stable Diffusion $\tau$ is a frozen text encoder that turns a prompt into 77 token vectors: CLIP ViT-L/14 with 768 dimensions in versions 1.x, OpenCLIP ViT-H/14 with 1024 in versions 2.x. The same mechanism accepts other conditions, such as class labels, segmentation maps or low-resolution images.

## Classifier-free guidance

A conditional model sampled as it is follows the prompt only loosely. **Classifier-free guidance** ([Ho and Salimans, 2022](#references)) strengthens it without a separate classifier. During training the condition is replaced by an empty one in a fraction of the steps, 10 or 20 % in the paper, so one network learns both $\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, y)$ and $\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \varnothing)$. At sampling time the two predictions are extrapolated:

$$
\tilde\varepsilon = \varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \varnothing) + s\,\big(\varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, y) - \varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \varnothing)\big) .
$$

$s = 0$ is unconditional, $s = 1$ the plain conditional model, and $s > 1$ moves beyond it; Stable Diffusion uses $s = 7.5$ by default. Since the noise prediction is a scaled score, $s_\theta = -\varepsilon_\theta / \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ ([score matching note](/notes/generative-models/score-matching#the-same-model-as-ddpm)), the guided prediction is the score

$$
(1 - s)\, \nabla \log p(z) + s\, \nabla \log p(z \mid y) = \nabla \log\big[p(z)^{1 - s}\, p(z \mid y)^{s}\big],
$$

and by Bayes' rule $p(z \mid y) \propto p(y \mid z)\, p(z)$ this is $\nabla \log p(z) + s\, \nabla \log p(y \mid z)$: the unconditional score plus $s$ times the gradient of an implicit classifier.

**Worked example.** Take an unconditional $p = \mathcal N(0, 4)$ and a conditional $p(\cdot \mid y) = \mathcal N(2, 1)$. The guided density $p^{1-s}\, p(\cdot \mid y)^{s}$ is a Gaussian with precision $(1 - s)/4 + s$ and mean $2s$ divided by that precision. At $s = 3$ the precision is $2.5$, so the standard deviation is $0.63$ and the mean $2.4$; at $s = 7.5$ the precision is $5.875$, the standard deviation $0.41$ and the mean $2.55$. Guidance narrows the distribution and pushes it away from the unconditional mean, past the conditional one: samples become more typical of the prompt and less diverse, the precision side of the trade-off from the [precision and recall note](/notes/generative-models/precision-recall#where-the-trade-off-appears). Each step costs two network evaluations, one with the prompt and one without.

## Fewer steps

DDPM samples with as many steps as it was trained with, 1000. Practical samplers treat the reverse process as a differential equation, the probability flow ODE of [Song et al. (2021)](#references), and integrate it with large steps. **DDIM** ([Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021](#references)) is a deterministic sampler for the same trained network that works with 50 steps or fewer and maps each noise latent to one image, which also makes an image invertible back to its noise. **DPM-Solver++** ([Lu et al., 2022](#references)) uses the semi-linear structure of the equation for higher-order steps and needs about 15 to 25. The Euler sampler of [Karras et al. (2022)](#references) is the first-order method on the same equation. The network does not change; only the update rule between its evaluations does, which is why diffusion libraries let the sampler (the **scheduler**) be swapped after training.

## Editing with the same model

- **Image-to-image** (SDEdit, [Meng et al., 2022](#references)). Encode an existing image, add noise to an intermediate step, $z_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, z_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$, and run the remaining denoising steps with a new prompt. The **strength** $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ chooses the step: with 40 sampling steps and $\lambda = 0.6$, the last 24 are run. Low strength keeps the composition; strength 1 ignores the input.
- **Inpainting.** To change only a masked region, at every step the latent outside the mask is replaced by the original latent noised to the same step, and only the masked part follows the model. Dedicated inpainting models instead receive the mask and the masked image as extra input channels and are trained for the task.
- **LoRA.** Fine-tune the attention layers with low-rank updates $W + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A$ ([PEFT note](/notes/nlp/peft#lora-a-low-rank-update)) to teach a style or a subject from a small dataset; the update can be fused into the weights for inference.
- **Textual inversion** ([Gal et al., 2023](#references)). Freeze everything and learn only the embedding of a new token, such as `<S*>`, by minimizing the diffusion loss on a few images of a concept; the token can then be used in any prompt.

## What changed

| | Pixel-space diffusion | Latent diffusion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Space of the diffusion | pixels, $512 \cdot 512 \cdot 3$ | latent, $64 \cdot 64 \cdot 4$ |
| Who removes imperceptible detail | the diffusion model, slowly | the autoencoder, once |
| Attention | only at coarse resolutions | affordable over the whole latent grid |
| Conditioning | possible, costly | cross-attention to a text encoder |
| Sampling | about 1000 steps | 20 to 50 steps with DDIM or DPM-Solver, two evaluations each with guidance |
| Ceiling on detail | the network | the decoder of the autoencoder |

## Limitations

- **The decoder bounds the detail.** Fine text, faces at small scale and exact textures depend on what the autoencoder can reconstruct; the diffusion model cannot add what $\mathcal D$ cannot express.
- **Guidance is a trade-off.** Large $s$ improves prompt adherence and typicality at the cost of diversity and, beyond some value, of image quality.
- **The text encoder limits understanding.** A frozen CLIP-like encoder with 77 tokens struggles with counting, spatial relations and long prompts.
- **Still iterative.** Tens of network evaluations per image remain, twice that with guidance.
- **Data.** The models are trained on large scraped image–text collections, with the questions of consent, copyright and bias that come with them.

## Summary

- An autoencoder removes perceptual detail once; diffusion runs in its latent, 48 times smaller than the image for Stable Diffusion at $512 \times 512$.
- The autoencoder is trained with reconstruction, perceptual and patch-adversarial losses and a weak KL or VQ regularization; $f$ between 4 and 16 balances speed and fidelity.
- Text enters the U-Net through cross-attention: queries from the latent, keys and values from the text encoder.
- Classifier-free guidance extrapolates from the unconditional to the conditional prediction; it samples from $p^{1-s}\, p(\cdot \mid y)^s$, which narrows the distribution toward the prompt.
- Samplers such as DDIM and DPM-Solver integrate the same model in 20 to 50 steps; image-to-image, inpainting, LoRA and textual inversion reuse it for editing.

The [advanced diffusion notebook](https://github.com/HSE-LAMBDA/DeepGenerativeModels/blob/spring-2025-long/seminars/14_Advanced-Diffusion-Models/Advanced_Diffusion.ipynb) of the HSE-LAMBDA *Deep Generative Models* repository uses the Hugging Face Diffusers library (cell numbers count every cell from 1). It has no saved outputs, so it documents the procedure rather than results.

- **Cells 5–9**: a recap of DDPM and the Stable Diffusion pipeline, then text-to-image with `stabilityai/stable-diffusion-2-1-base` in float32, 25 steps and guidance scale 7.5. The markdown of cell 6 gives the text embedding as $77 \times 768$, the size for Stable Diffusion 1.x; the 2.1 model used here has an OpenCLIP encoder with 1024 dimensions.
- **Cells 10–12**: schedulers. The ODE in the markdown keeps only the noise term: the probability flow ODE of the variance-preserving process also has a term proportional to $z_t$ and a factor $\frac12$. The Euler update is written with added noise, while `EulerDiscreteScheduler` is deterministic by default (the stochastic variant is `EulerAncestralDiscreteScheduler`), and DDIM is said to allow parallel sampling, which it does not; it allows fewer steps and exact inversion. In cell 11 `swap_scheduler` replaces the scheduler of the same pipeline object and returns it, so `pipe_euler` and `pipe_dpm` are one pipeline: both images of the side-by-side are produced with DPM-Solver++.
- **Cells 13–16**: batch generation of three prompts and prompt weighting with Compel (`ball++`, `ball--`, `forest---`). The markdown describes the `(token:weight)` syntax of another tool and a normalized weighted sum of token embeddings; Compel's syntax is the one used in the code, and its weighting acts on the text-encoder output.
- **Cells 17–20**: image-to-image on a $768 \times 768$ picture, with strength 0.6 (24 of 40 steps) and 1.0.
- **Cells 21–22**: inpainting with the same base checkpoint. Its U-Net has 4 input channels, so the pipeline uses the latent replacement described in the markdown rather than the mask-conditioned inpainting models.
- **Cells 23–24**: a pixel-art LoRA loaded into Stable Diffusion XL and fused with scale 0.8.
- **Cells 25–26**: textual inversion with a concept token from the `sd-concepts-library`, loaded into `runwayml/stable-diffusion-v1-5`. That repository has been removed from the Hugging Face Hub; the same weights are published as `stable-diffusion-v1-5/stable-diffusion-v1-5`.
