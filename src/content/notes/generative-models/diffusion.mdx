A diffusion model starts with a process we can specify: gradually destroy structure in data by adding noise. A learned reverse process then generates new data by starting from noise and taking many small steps in the opposite direction.

The scan introduces [Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, Ho et al. (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239), following [Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585). Its opening history labels GANs “SotA as of 2015” and points back to variational inference. This is the seminar's historical framing; the variational bound returns in the reverse-process derivation. Here the mathematical question is how to turn an unknown data distribution into a simple terminal distribution whose samples are easy to draw.

This first note constructs the forward process. The next notes derive the [reverse process and variational objective](/notes/generative-models/diffusion-reverse), then [noise prediction and sampling](/notes/generative-models/diffusion-noise-prediction).

## Two directions, two jobs

Let $x_0\sim q_{\mathrm{data}}$ be a data point, with $x_t\in\mathbb R^d$ at every timestep. A fixed **forward** chain moves from $x_0$ toward noise:

$$
q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}).
$$

We choose these transitions rather than learn them in the setup below. The **reverse** model will learn distributions $p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)$ and generate from a Gaussian prior at time $T$. The same number of coordinates is retained throughout; this chain does not use a smaller latent bottleneck.

The goal is a terminal distribution close to $\mathcal N(0,I)$, so generation can begin with ordinary Gaussian noise. Finite noising does not automatically make the terminal distribution exactly standard normal.

## Why adding noise alone is insufficient

Pages 38–39 first try a random walk:

$$
x_t=x_{t-1}+\sqrt\beta\,\epsilon_t,
\qquad \epsilon_t\overset{\mathrm{iid}}\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

The noises at different steps are independent. Conditioned on a fixed $x_0$, after $t$ steps

$$
x_t=x_0+\sqrt\beta\sum_{s=1}^{t}\epsilon_s,
\qquad
q(x_t\mid x_0)=\mathcal N(x_0,t\beta I).
$$

An equivalent direct draw is $x_t=x_0+\sqrt{t\beta}\,\epsilon$ with one standard-normal $\epsilon$. It is not $x_0+t\sqrt\beta\,\epsilon$: that would have variance $t^2\beta I$.

The conditional mean stays at $x_0$, while the variance grows without bound. This does not approach $\mathcal N(0,I)$. The red annotation in the scan identifies the variance-exploding trial and rejects it for this goal.

## Contract the signal and add noise

DDPM uses a variance schedule $0<\beta_t<1$ and defines

$$
\alpha_t=1-\beta_t.
$$

The transition is

$$
\boxed{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})=
\mathcal N\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\beta_t I\bigr).}
$$

To draw from it,

$$
x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\epsilon_t,
\qquad \epsilon_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
$$

The old signal contracts, and independent noise is added. Here **$\beta_t$ is a variance**; its square root is the noise amplitude. The Gaussian's second argument is a covariance matrix.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Editorial correction.</strong> The scan alternates between $\beta$ and $\sqrt\beta$ as a noise coefficient, and some direct-sampling lines omit a square root. This note uses the variance convention consistently, matching the notebook and the original DDPM formulation.</aside>

The coefficients are chosen so that standard Gaussian noise is stationary: if $x_{t-1}\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and is independent of $\epsilon_t$, then $x_t$ also has mean zero and covariance $\alpha_t I+\beta_t I=I$. For non-Gaussian data, individual forward marginals need not yet be Gaussian.

## Sample any timestep directly

Define the accumulated signal factor

$$
\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s,
\qquad \bar\alpha_0=1.
$$

Expanding the recursion gives

$$
x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+
\sum_{s=1}^{t}
\sqrt{\beta_s\prod_{r=s+1}^{t}\alpha_r}\,\epsilon_s.
$$

The independent Gaussian terms in that sum have total covariance

$$
\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t}\beta_s\prod_{r=s+1}^{t}\alpha_r\right)I
=(1-\bar\alpha_t)I.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{q(x_t\mid x_0)=
\mathcal N\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,(1-\bar\alpha_t)I\bigr),}
$$

and a direct sample is

$$
\boxed{x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+
\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,
\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).}
$$

We can select a timestep and construct its noisy input without simulating every preceding state. This is what makes random-timestep training practical. A single direct draw has the correct marginal at that timestep; independently drawing several noisy levels this way does not reproduce the correlations of one forward trajectory.

For constant $\beta$, $\bar\alpha_t=(1-\beta)^t$. With a varying schedule, use the product above. Both $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and $\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}$ are amplitudes; their squares describe retained signal and injected-noise variance factors.

## From the data distribution toward Gaussian noise

For a fixed starting point,

$$
\mathbb E[x_t\mid x_0]=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(x_t\mid x_0)=(1-\bar\alpha_t)I.
$$

If $\bar\alpha_t\to0$, the conditional distribution tends to $\mathcal N(0,I)$. Constant $0<\beta<1$ gives this limit as $t\to\infty$. In a finite DDPM, the schedule must make $\bar\alpha_T$ sufficiently small; having each $\beta_t$ in $(0,1)$ alone does not establish a good finite terminal approximation.

The data marginal also averages over starting points:

$$
q_t(x_t)=\int q(x_t\mid x_0)q_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)\,dx_0.
$$

Although $q(x_t\mid x_0)$ is Gaussian for a fixed $x_0$, this mixture generally is not. The sketch on page 40 shows a structured distribution approaching a single noise distribution.

<DdpmForwardDensities />

For data with finite mean $m_0$ and covariance $C_0$, the corresponding marginal moments are

$$
m_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,m_0,
\qquad
C_t=\bar\alpha_t C_0+(1-\bar\alpha_t)I.
$$

These formulas separate conditional noise variance from the full data covariance. They also explain the phrase “variance preserving”: standard-normal covariance is preserved by a step, while arbitrary data covariance moves toward $I$ rather than remaining unchanged.

The forward schedule now gives us known noisy distributions and a tractable Gaussian conditional posterior. The [reverse-process note](/notes/generative-models/diffusion-reverse) uses that posterior to construct the training objective.
