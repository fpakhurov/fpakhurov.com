The shop wants a dashboard that usually stays within ten minutes of sales, with a daily total that can be corrected after a late arrival. Its current hourly job finishes in two minutes. Faster execution alone cannot make an event that arrived just after the job started appear within ten minutes. It first has to wait for the next job.

## Write down the result and its deadline

Measure freshness at the dashboard, from source readiness to visibility. A separate completeness measure states which store inputs have been accounted for. Recent results can be provisional. The daily report can be finalized under an explicit cutoff, then corrected by a versioned backfill. This separates an early answer from the later handling of out-of-order input. The Dataflow model describes this tension among correctness, latency and cost. [Akidau et al., 2015](#references)

Compare three designs on a toy workload. Assume data are ready on arrival, there is no queue in the normal case, and publication time is included in the execution time. An hourly batch can wait almost 60 minutes and then run for two. A five-minute incremental batch waits at most five and runs for two. A continuously running job has a modeled processing and publication budget of three minutes. These numbers are design assumptions, so they do not establish a latency percentile.

| Design | Normal-case bound in the model | Freshness objective |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hourly batch | 62 minutes | Misses 10 minutes |
| Five-minute incremental batch | 7 minutes | Fits |
| Continuous processing | 3 minutes | Fits |

## Pay for the work you actually do

At five-minute intervals there are $24 \cdot 60 / 5 = 288$ runs a day. If a run uses four vCPUs for two minutes, the modeled daily compute is

$$
288 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 / 60 = 38.4\ \mathrm{vCPU\ hours}.
$$

A continuously allocated two-vCPU job uses $24 \cdot 2 = 48$ vCPU hours a day. The first design does less modeled CPU time. It could still cost more if it repeatedly reads large files, keeps cluster capacity allocated between runs or writes many small outputs that need compaction. The continuous job pays for state, checkpoint traffic and idle capacity. Spark Structured Streaming is one example of a system that can run a streaming query as a series of micro-batches. [Apache Spark, Structured Streaming](#references)

Keep storage, transferred bytes, compute time and operational effort as separate terms. An input retention window also buys recovery options. If the log retains a day, a three-day replay needs another source. If recoverable input is available, the repair still needs spare throughput beyond the current arrival rate.

## Check the assumptions under load

The seven-minute bound assumes every batch finishes before the next interval. If batches that arrive every five minutes take six minutes each on the same worker, backlog grows by one minute per interval until conditions change. Under parallel execution a slow partition can hold up publication even if the average task is fast. Systems at scale have to measure the tail and its effect on the complete response. [Dean and Barroso, 2013](#references)

For this modeled shop, five-minute incremental batches meet the objective without continuously allocated compute. Verify the actual publication delay, data coverage and time to replay a missed interval before committing to that design. A tighter objective, unpredictable input or a long recovery can change the choice.
