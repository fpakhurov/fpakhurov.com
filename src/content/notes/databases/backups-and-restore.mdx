An operator accidentally deletes reservations and commits. Restarting the server recovers the deletion along with all other durable changes. If the disk itself has failed, a restart may have neither the pages nor the local WAL. The first incident needs a prior state. The second needs another copy.

## What a backup must contain

A **logical backup** records database objects and their contents in a form the database can recreate. PostgreSQL's `pg_dump` takes a consistent snapshot of one database while normal reads and writes continue. Restoring that dump can rebuild its schema and rows. Cluster-wide roles and tablespaces require separate handling, for example through `pg_dumpall --globals-only`. A dump supplies the data at its snapshot. The subsequent physical WAL cannot be replayed on top of it to recover a later PostgreSQL state. [PostgreSQL 18, §§25.1 and 25.3](#references)

A **physical base backup** contains the cluster's files in a form that the engine can recover. For an online PostgreSQL base backup, recovery also needs the continuous WAL sequence beginning no later than the start of the backup. The files may be copied at different times while writes continue. The backup procedure and required WAL let recovery reach a consistent point. Simply copying open files at arbitrary times gives no such boundary. SQLite's Online Backup API is another engine-specific way to obtain a consistent copy of a live database. [PostgreSQL 18, §25.3](#references) [SQLite, Online Backup API](#references)

The copy and its required WAL must be accessible after the failure we are planning for. A backup on the same lost volume will not help with loss of that volume. A replica can improve availability, but a replica that replays the mistaken deletion also loses the rows. A retained backup with the appropriate history serves a different recovery objective.

## Choose how far to replay

Suppose a PostgreSQL base backup completed at 08:00 and its required WAL is available. The archive has an unbroken sequence through a recoverable commit at 11:55. The primary storage fails at 12:00 and no later WAL can be recovered. Restoring the base and replaying that sequence reaches the committed state at 11:55. The five-minute interval is the portion of history outside the available archive. The number of lost transactions depends on the workload in that interval. [PostgreSQL 18, §25.3](#references)

A missing WAL segment breaks the chain. Later segments cannot reconstruct the changes that were omitted. The usable restore point can move earlier, or the base backup can become unusable if the gap is within the WAL needed to make it consistent. Archiving must cover the relevant timeline as well. When recovery starts a new history, later log records on that branch cannot be treated as a continuation of a different branch. [PostgreSQL 18, §25.3](#references)

For the mistaken deletion, suppose the transaction committed at 11:50. Replay all the way to 11:55 and the deletion is present. **Point-in-time recovery** (PITR) stops replay at a chosen target. In PostgreSQL, when the bad transaction ID is known and the target can be reached, `recovery_target_xid` with `recovery_target_inclusive = off` stops before that transaction commits. The restored cluster also excludes later legitimate commits. Restoring into a separate environment allows an operator to extract the needed rows and reconcile them with the current state. A PostgreSQL physical restore operates at cluster scope even when the mistake affected only one table. [PostgreSQL 18, §§19.5.6 and 25.3](#references)

## Measure two different times

The **recovery point objective** (RPO) specifies how recent the recovered data must be. A target of ten minutes allows recovery to a point no more than ten minutes before the outage in this media-loss scenario. The **recovery time objective** (RTO) specifies the target time to restore service. If service is ready at 12:42, the actual recovery took 42 minutes from 12:00. With a ten-minute RPO and a one-hour RTO, this exercise meets both objectives. The objectives are requirements. The five-minute gap and the 42-minute restore are outcomes of the assumed exercise. [NIST SP 800-34 Rev. 1, §3.2.1](#references)

The time to restore includes getting a copy and the needed log, replaying the log, checking the data and making the service ready. More frequent backup snapshots or a shorter archive delay can reduce the age of the recovered state. They do not by themselves make the service available sooner.

## Restore and check the result

A restore exercise runs against a separate destination. Check that the backup and WAL can be read, the server reaches the intended point and the application can execute representative operations. PostgreSQL's `pg_verifybackup` can verify a base backup against its manifest and check required WAL when applicable. Its successful result is useful evidence about the files. A restore exercise also checks the environment, the recovery target and what the service actually sees. [PostgreSQL 18, pg_verifybackup](#references)

For the reservation example, a read-only check can compare the restored totals with the expected state at the chosen point. It uses the [stock and reservation tables](/notes/databases/transactions-and-invariants#make-both-writes-use-the-same-quantity) and the six-pack invariant from the toy model.

```sql
SELECT s.product, s.available, COALESCE(r.reserved, 0) AS reserved,
       s.available + COALESCE(r.reserved, 0) AS accounted
FROM stock AS s
LEFT JOIN (
    SELECT product, SUM(quantity) AS reserved
    FROM reservations
    GROUP BY product
) AS r ON r.product = s.product
WHERE s.product = 'tea';
```

At the point before reservation 701 this returns five available, one reserved and six accounted for. After reservation 701 it gives three, three and six. The same total is consistent with both points, so check the individual reservation and the expected commit boundary as well. A real exercise needs similarly specific checks of the restored data and the time at which the service can resume.
