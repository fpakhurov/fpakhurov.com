The preview service is healthy, but editors report that its queue is less useful. Request success and CPU load cannot establish whether the uploaded cards resemble the evaluation data. Conversely, a changed input distribution does not by itself establish that decisions became worse. Monitoring must distinguish these observations.

## Observe the decision as well as the endpoint \{#baseline-monitor-the-service-not-the-model\}

Keep service indicators such as errors, queue age and complete-request delay. Add schema failures, missing features, score distributions, action rates and label coverage. Once labels mature, compute quality on the corresponding prediction cohort. A label received today may evaluate a prediction from last week and a different release.

## Define the population before comparing it \{#what-to-monitor\}

Record the time window, input filters, feature version, model version and grouping unit. Comparing one scanner family in the reference window with all scanners today can manufacture a change through population selection. Keep a stable reference when testing for a change; use a rolling reference only with a stated reason, since gradual drift can disappear when the reference moves with it.

A useful alert has an owner and a follow-up measurement. A feature shift can trigger investigation of a decoder update. A growing label gap can trigger inspection of the review workflow. Neither observation alone authorizes automatic retraining or deletion of user data.

## Separate kinds of distribution change \{#kinds-of-shift\}

Write $P(X,Y)=P(Y\mid X)P(X)=P(X\mid Y)P(Y)$. Here $X$ is the observed feature vector and $Y$ the later repair label.

- **Covariate shift:** $P(X)$ changes while $P(Y\mid X)$ remains fixed. More blurry uploads arrive, but the relationship between blur and repair need stays the same.
- **Label shift:** $P(Y)$ changes while $P(X\mid Y)$ remains fixed. The mixture of good and bad previews changes, while each class retains its feature distribution.
- **Concept change:** $P(Y\mid X)$ changes. A new publication standard can change whether the same feature pattern requires repair.

These are assumptions about a joint distribution. Several can fail at once. Unlabelled inputs can reveal a marginal change in $X$ but cannot establish that the conditional label rule stayed fixed. Even an unchanged input marginal can accompany a changed label rule.

## Compute a discrepancy and state its calibration \{#detecting-drift-statistics-on-numbers\}

The following samples and bin counts are original fictional fixtures. They illustrate arithmetic and small-sample limits; they are not records from learners or a trained model.

### Fixed-bin proportions \{#psi-the-population-stability-index\}

For three fixed bins, let reference fractions be $p=(0.6,0.3,0.1)$ and current fractions $q=(0.2,0.3,0.5)$. With every fraction positive, define the population stability index

$$
\mathrm{PSI}=\sum_i(q_i-p_i)\log\frac{q_i}{p_i}.
$$

Expanding the product gives $D_{KL}(q\|p)+D_{KL}(p\|q)$, so PSI is a symmetric sum of two discrete KL divergences. For these fractions it is $1.083220$. The middle bin contributes zero; the first and third account for the change. The value depends on binning, and it is not a p-value. A zero bin needs an explicit policy: report a newly observed category or record a smoothing rule and its influence. A conventional fixed cutoff does not calibrate every sample size and workload.

### Empirical cumulative distributions \{#the-kolmogorovsmirnov-test\}

For samples $A=[0,1,1,2,3]$ and $B=[1,2,3,4,5]$, define $F_A(t)$ as the fraction of A at most $t$, and similarly $F_B(t)$. The two-sided KS statistic is $D=\sup_t|F_A(t)-F_B(t)|$. At $t=1$, the fractions are $3/5$ and $1/5$, giving 0.4; checking all observed values finds no larger difference.

The usual continuous-distribution KS calibration has assumptions that matter when values tie ([SciPy](#references)). Here we instead enumerate all $\binom{10}{5}=252$ assignments of the pooled observations to two labelled groups. Under the null that observations are exchangeable between groups, 204 assignments have a statistic at least 0.4. The exact permutation p-value is $204/252\approx0.809524$. A visible discrepancy in five observations per group is weak evidence under this test. Failure to reject does not establish equality.

### Category counts \{#chi-square-for-categories\}

If $p$ is a fixed reference distribution and current counts are $O_i$ with total $n$, Pearson's discrepancy is $\sum_i(O_i-np_i)^2/(np_i)$. For counts $(2,3,5)$ and expected counts $(6,3,1)$, it is $18.666667$. The expected count of one is too small to casually apply the usual large-sample chi-square approximation. If the reference proportions were themselves estimated from a small sample, a two-sample procedure must also account for that uncertainty. A large discrepancy and a valid calibrated test are different objects.

### Compare feature representations \{#mmd-many-features-at-once\}

Maximum mean discrepancy compares average feature representations in a kernel space ([Gretton et al.](#references)). If $k(x,z)=\langle\phi(x),\phi(z)\rangle$, the squared difference between sample means is

$$
\left\|\frac1n\sum_i\phi(x_i)-\frac1m\sum_j\phi(z_j)\right\|^2
=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,i'}k(x_i,x_{i'})+\frac1{m^2}\sum_{j,j'}k(z_j,z_{j'})-\frac2{nm}\sum_{i,j}k(x_i,z_j).
$$

This is the nonnegative, biased empirical squared MMD, obtained by expanding a squared norm. For the linear kernel $k(x,z)=xz$, it becomes the squared difference of ordinary means. The samples above have means 1.4 and 3, so it equals $2.56$. But $[-1,1]$ and $[-2,2]$ have the same mean and yield zero despite different spread. Kernel choice determines which differences are visible; calibration still requires a suitable sampling assumption.

### Evidence from a broader study \{#what-failing-loudly-found\}

Rabanser, Günnemann and Lipton compared shift detection approaches over defined datasets and perturbations. Their results support combining a representation with two-sample testing in the evaluated settings, and investigating whether a detected shift is harmful. They do not establish one detector as best for every service. No benchmark data, plots or measured scores from that study are used in our fixtures.

## A repeated test is an alerting policy \{#alert-thresholds-and-false-alarms\}

If twenty independent tests each have a 5% false-alarm probability, the chance of at least one false alarm is $1-0.95^{20}\approx64.15\%$. Independence is required for that calculation. A Bonferroni threshold of $0.05/20=0.0025$ bounds the family-wise error for twenty valid tests without requiring independence, but can reduce detection power.

Monitoring also repeats through time. Overlapping windows are dependent, and observing a metric every minute creates more opportunities to alert. Choose windows and follow-up actions before inspecting a suspected incident, calibrate on appropriate stable periods, and measure detection delay as well as false alarms. A useful operational policy may require persistence or corroboration, but those changes must be evaluated as part of the policy.

## What changed \{#what-changed\}

The service-health dashboard now has a separate view of population change and delayed decision quality. Every discrepancy carries its reference, sample size and calibration method. The tiny KS example explains why a large-looking difference can still have a high p-value.

## Limits \{#limitations\}

A drift detector can miss a change in an unobserved feature or in the label relationship. A large sample can flag a negligible shift. Reviewer labels can be selective or inconsistent. Repeated correlated observations can make an independent-sample test overconfident. A detector's output begins an investigation; real harm needs outcome evidence and context.

## What to retain \{#what-you-should-remember\}

Align windows, versions and populations. Separate input shift from quality loss. Report label maturity. Distinguish a discrepancy from its p-value. Calibrate the repeated alerting policy. Keep an owner and a concrete follow-up action.
