The [previous note](/notes/mlops/testing-ml-systems) tested models small enough to train in a second. The models that dominate current practice are not: a language model with seven billion parameters cannot even hold its training state in the 80 GB of an H100, and training it on one device would take years. This note works out why, on numbers, and then builds up the ways a run is spread across many GPUs: copying the model and splitting the data, splitting the optimizer state, splitting every matrix, splitting the stack of layers, and trading computation for memory. Each comes with a cost model in bytes and seconds, which is what decides how they are combined.

**Notation.** $\Psi$ is the number of parameters, $N$ the number of GPUs that share data-parallel work, $S$ the size in bytes of the gradient being exchanged, $B$ the bandwidth of one link in bytes per second, $p$ the number of pipeline stages and $m$ the number of micro-batches. A GB is $10^9$ bytes.

**Conventions.** The ring all-reduce of the Example is a real simulation in NumPy 2.5.4 on Python 3.14.7 (23 October 2026). Nothing here was run on GPUs: memory and time are computed from formulas, with the hardware figures of NVIDIA's H100 SXM datasheet (80 GB of memory, 989 dense bf16 TFLOP/s, 900 GB/s of NVLink bandwidth in total per GPU) and an assumed sustained rate of 400 TFLOP/s, about 40% of peak. Library versions are those of October 2026: PyTorch 2.14.1 and DeepSpeed 0.19.8.

## Baseline: one GPU

Training a model of $\Psi$ parameters on $D$ tokens costs about $6 \Psi D$ floating-point operations, two per parameter per token forward and four backward, as the [scaling-laws note](/notes/nlp/scaling-laws#what-is-counted-parameters-tokens-and-compute) derives. For $\Psi = 7 \times 10^9$ and $D = 10^{12}$ tokens:

$$
\begin{aligned}
6 \cdot 7 \times 10^{9} \cdot 10^{12} &= 4.2 \times 10^{22}\ \text{FLOPs}, \\
\frac{4.2 \times 10^{22}}{4 \times 10^{14}\ \text{FLOP/s}} &= 1.05 \times 10^{8}\ \text{s} \approx 3.3\ \text{years}.
\end{aligned}
$$

On 1,024 GPUs with perfect scaling it would take 1.2 days. And it cannot start on one GPU at all, because, as the next section shows, the training state of a 7B model needs 112 GB before a single activation is stored, against 80 GB of memory. Distribution is needed for memory first and for time second.

## Where the memory goes

Mixed-precision training with Adam, as the [Deep Learning course](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#mixed-precision-fp16-bf16-and-loss-scaling) describes it, keeps for every parameter:

| | precision | bytes per parameter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| weights used in the forward and backward pass | fp16 or bf16 | 2 |
| gradients | fp16 or bf16 | 2 |
| master copy of the weights | fp32 | 4 |
| Adam's first moment $m$ | fp32 | 4 |
| Adam's second moment $v$ | fp32 | 4 |
| **total** | | **16** |

The last three rows are the **optimizer states**, $K = 12$ bytes per parameter in the notation of the ZeRO paper, so the model states take $(2 + 2 + K)\Psi = 16\Psi$ bytes ([Rajbhandari et al., 2020](#references), section 3.1). For $\Psi = 7 \times 10^9$ that is $16 \cdot 7 \times 10^9 = 112$ GB.

**Activations** come on top: the outputs of every layer that the backward pass needs. For a Transformer layer with hidden size $h$, $a$ attention heads, sequence length $s$ and micro-batch $b$, in 16-bit precision, Korthikanti et al. count

$$
\text{activations per layer} = s\, b\, h \left(34 + \frac{5\, a\, s}{h}\right)\ \text{bytes}
$$

([Korthikanti et al., 2023](#references), section 4.1). For the shape of most 7B models, $h = 4096$, $a = 32$ and 32 layers, with one sequence of $s = 4096$ tokens: $34\, s b h = 0.57$ GB per layer, and the attention-score term $5\, a\, s^2 b = 2.68$ GB per layer, so $32 \cdot 3.25 = 104$ GB for the whole model. [FlashAttention](/notes/nlp/efficient-transformers#flashattention) never stores the $s \times s$ scores, which removes the second term and leaves $32 \cdot 0.57 = 18.3$ GB per sequence: still a quarter of the GPU for one sequence.

## Data parallelism

The simplest scheme copies the whole model to each of $N$ GPUs and gives each a different part of the batch. Every GPU computes gradients on its own examples; the gradients are then **averaged** across GPUs, every GPU applies the same update, and the replicas stay identical. Since the average of the per-GPU mean gradients is the mean gradient of the whole batch, $N$ GPUs with micro-batches of $b$ compute exactly what one GPU would compute with a batch of $Nb$, and the [rules for large batches](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#batch-size-and-the-learning-rate) apply.

The averaging is a collective operation called **all-reduce**: every participant contributes a vector, and every participant receives the sum. PyTorch's `DistributedDataParallel` (DDP) performs it during the backward pass rather than after it: gradients are grouped into **buckets** of about 25 MB in the order the backward pass produces them, and each bucket is all-reduced as soon as it is complete, while the backward pass continues on earlier layers ([Li et al., 2020](#references)). Communication is thereby hidden behind computation, as long as it is not slower than it. An older design, the parameter server, sends all gradients to a set of central servers that hold the weights; it has the bottleneck of the naive scheme below.

### The cost of all-reduce

How long does it take to all-reduce a gradient of $S$ bytes over $N$ GPUs whose links each move $B$ bytes per second in each direction?

**Naive: reduce to one, then broadcast.** Every GPU sends its gradient to GPU 0, which adds them and sends the sum back. GPU 0 receives $(N - 1)S$ bytes and sends $(N - 1)S$, all through its own link, while the other links are idle, so

$$
t_{\text{naive}} \approx \frac{2 (N - 1)\, S}{B},
$$

which grows linearly with the number of GPUs.

**Ring.** Arrange the GPUs in a ring, each sending only to its right neighbour, and cut every gradient into $N$ chunks of $S/N$ bytes ([Patarasuk and Yuan, 2009](#references)).

<RingAllReduce />

1. At the start, each GPU holds its own gradient, cut into $N = 4$ chunks.
2. **Reduce-scatter**, step 1: GPU $i$ sends chunk $i$ to GPU $i + 1$, which adds it to its own chunk $i$.
3. Step 2: GPU $i$ sends chunk $i - 1$, which now holds two terms, on to GPU $i + 1$, which adds its own.
4. After $N - 1 = 3$ steps, GPU $i$ holds chunk $i + 1$ summed over all four GPUs.
5. **All-gather**: in each step, every GPU sends its most recently completed chunk to its right neighbour, which stores it.
6. And again,
7. until after $N - 1$ more steps every GPU holds every chunk of the full sum.

In every step, every GPU sends exactly one chunk and receives exactly one, so all $N$ links work at the same time. Over the $2(N - 1)$ steps, each GPU sends

$$
\begin{aligned}
2 (N - 1) \cdot \frac{S}{N} &= \frac{2 (N - 1)}{N}\, S \ \text{bytes}, \\
t_{\text{ring}} &\approx \frac{2 (N - 1)}{N} \cdot \frac{S}{B} + 2 (N - 1)\, \alpha ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\alpha$ is the fixed latency of one message. The bandwidth term approaches $2S/B$ as $N$ grows: **the time of a ring all-reduce hardly depends on the number of GPUs.** It is also the best possible: Patarasuk and Yuan show that no all-reduce algorithm has a smaller bandwidth term ([2009](#references)). Intuitively, each GPU's data must leave it for the $N - 1$ chunks it does not finish itself, $(N - 1)S/N$ bytes, and the finished sums of those chunks must come back, another $(N - 1)S/N$. The latency term does grow with $N$, which is why libraries such as NCCL also use tree algorithms for small messages and very large rings, and why DDP sends buckets rather than single tensors.

**On numbers.** A 7B model has a 14 GB gradient in bf16. On $N = 8$ GPUs, each sends $2 \cdot 7/8 \cdot 14 = 24.5$ GB per step. The time depends on the link:

| link | bandwidth per direction | ring all-reduce of 14 GB |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NVLink inside an H100 server | about 450 GB/s | 0.054 s |
| InfiniBand, 400 Gb/s per GPU | 50 GB/s | 0.49 s |
| Ethernet, 100 Gb/s | 12.5 GB/s | 1.96 s |

The computation it must hide behind: a micro-batch of 8 sequences of 2,048 tokens per GPU costs $6 \cdot 7 \times 10^9 \cdot 16{,}384 = 6.9 \times 10^{14}$ FLOPs, 1.72 s at 400 TFLOP/s. Inside one server, or over InfiniBand, the all-reduce fits behind the backward pass; over ordinary 100 Gb/s Ethernet it takes longer than the computation. The ratio is the [roofline](/notes/acos/data-level-parallelism#the-roofline-model-arithmetic-or-memory) argument again with the network in place of memory: communication per step is fixed by the model size, computation grows with the tokens per GPU, so larger micro-batches make data parallelism cheaper. The Example simulates the ring, checks that it computes the same sum as the naive scheme, and counts the bytes.

Data parallelism leaves one problem untouched: every GPU still holds all 112 GB of model states.

## Sharding the model states: ZeRO and FSDP

Under data parallelism, all $N$ GPUs hold identical copies of the optimizer states, the gradients and the weights. ZeRO, the Zero Redundancy Optimizer, removes the copies in three stages, each partitioning one more kind of state into $N$ shards, one per GPU ([Rajbhandari et al., 2020](#references)):

- **Stage 1, optimizer states.** GPU $j$ keeps the fp32 master weights and Adam moments only for its $1/N$ of the parameters, and updates only those. After the update, the GPUs all-gather the new 16-bit weights. Memory per GPU: $2\Psi + 2\Psi + 12\Psi/N$.
- **Stage 2, gradients as well.** Instead of an all-reduce, the gradients are **reduce-scattered**: each GPU receives the summed gradient of its own shard only, which is all its optimizer needs. Memory: $2\Psi + 14\Psi/N$.
- **Stage 3, the weights as well.** Each GPU stores only its shard of the 16-bit weights. Before a layer runs, forward or backward, its full weights are all-gathered from the shards, used, and freed. Memory: $16\Psi/N$.

For a 7B model on 8 GPUs:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{DDP:}\quad & 16 \cdot 7 = 112\ \text{GB}, \\
\text{stage 1:}\quad & 4 \cdot 7 + 12 \cdot 7/8 = 28 + 10.5 = 38.5\ \text{GB}, \\
\text{stage 2:}\quad & 2 \cdot 7 + 14 \cdot 7/8 = 14 + 12.25 = 26.25\ \text{GB}, \\
\text{stage 3:}\quad & 16 \cdot 7/8 = 14\ \text{GB}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The same formulas give the ZeRO paper's own example, 7.5 billion parameters on 64 GPUs: 120, 31.4, 16.6 and 1.9 GB (figure 1).

<ZeroMemory />

**What it costs in communication.** An all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather, each moving $(N - 1)S/N$ per GPU. Stages 1 and 2 replace the all-reduce of the gradients by a reduce-scatter of the gradients and an all-gather of the updated weights, the same two halves in a different place, so they communicate exactly as much as plain data parallelism. Stage 3 all-gathers the weights twice, once for the forward pass and once for the backward pass, and reduce-scatters the gradients once: three halves instead of two, **1.5 times** the communication of data parallelism ([Rajbhandari et al., 2020](#references), section 7). In exchange, memory falls linearly with the number of GPUs.

**FSDP**, Fully Sharded Data Parallel, is PyTorch's implementation of stage-3 sharding ([Zhao et al., 2023](#references)); its current form, `fully_shard`, shards each parameter tensor across the GPUs. Two of its options show the trade-offs: **hybrid sharding** shards within a server and replicates across servers, so that the frequent all-gathers run over NVLink and only the gradient reduction crosses the slower network; **CPU offloading** keeps shards in host memory, which saves GPU memory at the cost of PCIe transfers. DeepSpeed implements all three ZeRO stages, with offloading to CPU memory and NVMe.

## Tensor parallelism

ZeRO shards storage but still runs every layer whole on every GPU. When one layer's matrices or activations are too large, or one step is too slow, the matrices themselves are split. **Tensor parallelism**, as in Megatron-LM, splits each Transformer layer across $t$ GPUs so that only two collective operations per layer are needed in each direction ([Shoeybi et al., 2019](#references)).

Take the MLP of a layer, $Z = \mathrm{GeLU}(XA)\, B$, with $X$ of shape $(bs, h)$, $A$ of shape $(h, 4h)$ and $B$ of shape $(4h, h)$, on $t = 2$ GPUs.

1. Split $A$ **by columns**, $A = [A_1, A_2]$. Then $XA = [XA_1, XA_2]$, and because GeLU acts elementwise, $\mathrm{GeLU}(XA) = [\mathrm{GeLU}(XA_1), \mathrm{GeLU}(XA_2)] = [Y_1, Y_2]$: each GPU computes its half with no communication. Splitting $A$ by rows instead would give two partial sums, $XA = X_1 A_1 + X_2 A_2$, which must be added before the nonlinearity, a synchronization in the middle of the block.
2. Split $B$ **by rows**, $B = \begin{bmatrix} B_1 \\ B_2 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $Z = [Y_1, Y_2] \begin{bmatrix} B_1 \\ B_2 \end{bmatrix} = Y_1 B_1 + Y_2 B_2$: each GPU computes one term from the half it already has.
3. One **all-reduce** adds the two terms, and every GPU holds $Z$.

Attention is split the same way, by heads: each GPU computes its own heads, and the output projection, split by rows, ends with one all-reduce. In the backward pass the same pattern runs in reverse, so a layer costs two all-reduces of activations forward and two backward. Each GPU stores $1/t$ of the layer's weights and does $1/t$ of its arithmetic.

The price is that these all-reduces carry activations, $b \cdot s \cdot h$ values, in every layer, and the next operation waits for them. With $b = 8$, $s = 2048$, $h = 4096$ in bf16 an activation is 134 MB; on $t = 8$ GPUs each all-reduce sends $2 \cdot 7/8 \cdot 134 = 235$ MB per GPU, and four per layer over 32 layers come to 30 GB per step. Over NVLink that is 0.067 s against 0.22 s of computation per GPU, about a third more; over 50 GB/s InfiniBand it would be 0.60 s, almost three times the computation. This is why tensor parallelism stays **inside a server**, with $t$ at most the 8 GPUs that share NVLink ([Narayanan et al., 2021](#references)).

## Pipeline parallelism

The other way to split a model is by depth: layers $1$ to $L/p$ on GPU 1, the next $L/p$ on GPU 2, and so on for $p$ **stages**. Only activations at the boundaries travel between neighbouring stages, $b \cdot s \cdot h$ values per micro-batch, point to point, which a network between servers handles well. The problem is time. A naive schedule runs one batch through stage 1, then stage 2, and so on, and only one GPU works at a time.

**GPipe** splits the batch into $m$ **micro-batches** and sends them through the stages one after another, so that stage 2 works on micro-batch 1 while stage 1 works on micro-batch 2; all forward passes come first, then all backward passes, then one optimizer step ([Huang et al., 2019](#references)).

<PipelineBubble />

1. Forward passes: micro-batch $j$ reaches stage $i$ at time $(i + j - 2)\, t_f$, so the stages start one after another.
2. Backward passes run in the opposite order, here twice as long as forward ones.
3. The grey slots are the **bubble**, the time a stage waits.
4. With twice as many micro-batches the same idle slots remain at the edges, but they are a smaller share.

**The bubble fraction.** Let one micro-batch take $t_f$ forward and $t_b$ backward on one stage. The forward phase ends when the last micro-batch leaves the last stage. Micro-batch $m$ enters stage 1 at time $(m - 1)\, t_f$ and needs $p$ more slots to cross the $p$ stages, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_{\text{forward}} &= (m - 1)\, t_f + p\, t_f = (m + p - 1)\, t_f, \\
T_{\text{backward}} &= (m + p - 1)\, t_b \quad \text{(the same argument in reverse)}, \\
T_{\text{step}} &= (m + p - 1)(t_f + t_b).
\end{aligned}
$$

Each stage is busy only for its own work, $m\,(t_f + t_b)$, so the fraction of the step it is idle is

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{T_{\text{step}} - m (t_f + t_b)}{T_{\text{step}}} = \frac{(p - 1)(t_f + t_b)}{(m + p - 1)(t_f + t_b)} = \frac{p - 1}{m + p - 1}.\;}
$$

With $p = 4$ stages: one micro-batch, $m = 1$, leaves a stage idle $3/4$ of the time, the naive schedule; $m = 4$ gives $3/7 = 43\%$, $m = 8$ gives $3/11 = 27\%$, $m = 16$ gives $3/19 = 16\%$ and $m = 32$ gives $3/35 = 8.6\%$. The ratio of bubble to useful work is $(p - 1)/m$, the form used by Narayanan et al.; GPipe found the bubble negligible when $m \ge 4p$, partly because recomputation, described next, can be scheduled into it. More micro-batches mean smaller ones, though, and a micro-batch too small no longer keeps a GPU's matrix units busy.

GPipe holds the activations of all $m$ micro-batches until their backward passes, so memory grows with $m$. The **1F1B** schedule starts each micro-batch's backward pass as soon as possible and alternates one forward and one backward pass per stage; it has the same bubble but keeps at most $p$ micro-batches in flight. The **interleaved** schedule assigns each GPU several non-adjacent chunks of layers, which divides the bubble by the number of chunks at the cost of more point-to-point communication ([Narayanan et al., 2021](#references)).

## Gradient checkpointing

The activations are stored only because the backward pass needs them. **Gradient checkpointing**, also called activation recomputation, stores only some of them, the **checkpoints**, typically the input of each Transformer layer, and recomputes the rest of a layer's activations from its checkpoint when the backward pass reaches it ([Chen et al., 2016](#references)). For the 7B shape above, storing only layer inputs costs $2\, s b h = 33.5$ MB per layer, 1.07 GB for 32 layers, plus the 0.57 GB of the one layer being recomputed, instead of 18.3 GB. The price is one extra forward pass: since a forward pass is about a third of the work of forward and backward together ($2\Psi$ of $6\Psi$ FLOPs per token), training becomes about 33% slower. With $n$ layers and checkpoints every $\sqrt n$ layers, memory grows as $O(\sqrt n)$ for the same one extra forward pass. **Selective** recomputation saves most of the memory for a small part of the cost by recomputing only the attention operations whose activations grow with $s^2$ ([Korthikanti et al., 2023](#references)).

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> A training run is limited by memory before it is limited by time: mixed-precision Adam needs 16 bytes per parameter, plus activations. Data parallelism splits the batch and pays one all-reduce per step, which a ring makes independent of the number of GPUs. ZeRO and FSDP split the model states across the data-parallel GPUs for at most 1.5 times that traffic. Tensor parallelism splits each layer and pays activation all-reduces in every layer, so it stays inside a server. Pipeline parallelism splits by depth and pays a bubble of $(p - 1)/(m + p - 1)$. Checkpointing trades a third more computation for most of the activation memory.</aside>

## Choosing a combination

The schemes compose: with $t$-way tensor, $p$-way pipeline and $d$-way data parallelism, a run uses $N = t \cdot p \cdot d$ GPUs, the **3D parallelism** of Megatron-LM ([Narayanan et al., 2021](#references)). The choice follows from the costs above.

1. **If the model states and activations fit on one GPU**, use data parallelism: one overlapped all-reduce per step, nothing else to tune.
2. **If they do not fit but the model is moderate**, up to some tens of billions of parameters on a fast network, shard with ZeRO stage 2 or 3 or FSDP and add checkpointing. The 7B model of this note on one server of 8 GPUs needs 14 GB of model states per GPU under stage 3, plus about 1.6 GB of activations per sequence with checkpointing; it fits with room for a micro-batch of many sequences.
3. **For larger models**, put tensor parallelism inside each server, where NVLink can carry its per-layer all-reduces; pipeline parallelism across servers, whose point-to-point traffic tolerates a slower network; and data parallelism, possibly sharded, over everything else. A 70B model needs $16 \cdot 70 = 1{,}120$ GB of model states. On 8 servers of 8 GPUs, with $t = 8$ inside each server and ZeRO stage 1 across the $d = 8$ servers, each GPU holds $4 \cdot 70/8 = 35$ GB of 16-bit weights and gradients and $12 \cdot 70/64 = 13.1$ GB of optimizer states, 48.1 GB in all, leaving room for activations.
4. **Keep the global batch in mind.** Data parallelism multiplies the batch by $d$ and pipelining needs $m$ micro-batches per step; both make the global batch larger, and beyond some size larger batches stop helping optimization.

With such combinations, Narayanan et al. trained a model of one trillion parameters on 3,072 A100 GPUs at 502 petaFLOP/s, 52% of the GPUs' peak. In October 2026 these schemes are packaged in PyTorch (DDP, FSDP2, tensor and pipeline parallelism through `DeviceMesh`), DeepSpeed and Megatron-Core; the **sequence** and **context** parallelism that split long sequences across GPUs, and the **expert** parallelism of [mixtures of experts](/notes/nlp/efficient-transformers#mixture-of-experts), are further dimensions of the same mesh.

At this scale, failure is routine. During a 54-day period of training Llama 3 405B on 16,384 H100 GPUs, Meta counted 466 job interruptions, 419 of them unexpected, about one every three hours, with faulty GPUs the largest single cause ([Dubey et al., 2024](#references), section 3.3.4). A distributed run therefore checkpoints its full sharded state to storage regularly and restarts automatically from the last checkpoint, and the time lost per failure, the checkpoint interval plus the restart, is a design quantity of its own. For reproducibility, a run on a different number of GPUs is a different computation: the order of the sums changes, as the Example shows, and so do the batch size and the data order unless they are fixed independently of $N$.

## What changed

| | One GPU (the baseline) | Distributed training |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Memory limit | 80 GB for 16 bytes per parameter plus activations | states sharded over $N$ GPUs, $16\Psi/N$ with ZeRO-3 or FSDP |
| Time for $6\Psi D$ FLOPs | years for a 7B model | days, with near-linear scaling |
| Gradient exchange | none | ring all-reduce, $2(N - 1)/N \cdot S$ per GPU, overlapped with backward |
| Splitting one layer | impossible | tensor parallelism, two all-reduces per layer each way, inside a server |
| Splitting the depth | impossible | pipeline stages, idle share $(p - 1)/(m + p - 1)$ |
| Activations | all stored | layer inputs stored, the rest recomputed |
| Failures | rare | every few hours at scale; sharded checkpoints and automatic restarts |

## Limitations

- **The cost models are idealized.** They ignore network topology, congestion, the latency of small messages, imperfect overlap of communication with computation, and kernels that run below the assumed 40% of peak. Measured throughput, from a profiler, decides.
- **Memory is more than model states and activations.** Communication buffers, temporary tensors and fragmentation of the allocator take several more gigabytes.
- **Bigger batches are not free.** Every form of data parallelism enlarges the global batch, and past a critical batch size the extra examples per step stop reducing the number of steps.
- **Every dimension adds failure modes.** Pipeline stages must be balanced, tensor-parallel layers must be written for it, and a sharded checkpoint must be resharded to resume on a different number of GPUs.
- **Tools move.** FSDP2, DeepSpeed and Megatron-Core change their interfaces between releases; the versions here are those of October 2026.

## What you should remember

- Mixed-precision Adam needs 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for the 16-bit weights, 2 for the gradients, 12 for the fp32 master weights and two moments. A 7B model needs 112 GB before activations.
- Data parallelism copies the model and splits the batch; gradients are averaged by an all-reduce, overlapped with the backward pass.
- A ring all-reduce sends $2(N - 1)/N \cdot S$ bytes per GPU, the minimum possible, in a time that hardly grows with $N$; a reduce to one GPU grows linearly.
- ZeRO shards optimizer states (stage 1), gradients (stage 2) and weights (stage 3, FSDP): $2\Psi + 2\Psi + 12\Psi/N$, $2\Psi + 14\Psi/N$ and $16\Psi/N$, for 38.5, 26.25 and 14 GB in the 7B, 8-GPU example; stages 1 and 2 cost no extra communication, stage 3 costs 1.5 times.
- Tensor parallelism splits matrices (columns, then rows) with all-reduces of activations in every layer, so it stays inside a server.
- Pipeline parallelism splits layers into stages; with $m$ micro-batches on $p$ stages the idle share is $(p - 1)/(m + p - 1)$.
- Gradient checkpointing keeps layer inputs and recomputes the rest, for about a third more computation.
- Combine them as tensor inside servers, pipeline across servers, data (sharded) over the rest; plan for failures and checkpoints.

## Example

The program below simulates both all-reduce algorithms on 8 workers with NumPy, checks that they produce the same sum, and counts the bytes each worker sends. The ring follows the figure: in reduce-scatter step $s$, worker $i$ sends chunk $i - s$ to worker $i + 1$, which adds it; in all-gather step $s$, worker $i$ sends its finished chunk $i + 1 - s$, which the neighbour stores.

```python
# Ring all-reduce simulated with NumPy: the same sum as a naive reduce to one worker, and the bytes each worker sends.
import numpy as np

def ring_allreduce(grads):
    n = len(grads)
    chunks = [np.array_split(g.copy(), n) for g in grads]       # every worker cuts its gradient into n chunks
    sent = np.zeros(n)
    for s in range(n - 1):                                        # reduce-scatter
        msgs = [(i, (i - s) % n, chunks[i][(i - s) % n].copy()) for i in range(n)]   # all workers send at once
        for i, c, data in msgs:
            chunks[(i + 1) % n][c] += data                        # the right neighbour adds the chunk to its own
            sent[i] += data.nbytes
    # now worker i holds the complete sum of chunk i + 1
    for s in range(n - 1):                                        # all-gather
        msgs = [(i, (i + 1 - s) % n, chunks[i][(i + 1 - s) % n].copy()) for i in range(n)]
        for i, c, data in msgs:
            chunks[(i + 1) % n][c] = data                         # the right neighbour stores the finished chunk
            sent[i] += data.nbytes
    return [np.concatenate(c) for c in chunks], sent

def naive_allreduce(grads):
    n = len(grads)
    total, sent = grads[0].copy(), np.zeros(n)
    for i in range(1, n):                                         # everyone sends to worker 0, which adds
        total += grads[i]
        sent[i] += grads[i].nbytes
    sent[0] += (n - 1) * total.nbytes                             # worker 0 sends the sum back to everyone
    return [total.copy() for _ in range(n)], sent

n, size = 8, 1_000_000
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
grads = [rng.normal(size=size).astype(np.float32) for _ in range(n)]
exact = np.sum([g.astype(np.float64) for g in grads], axis=0)
ring, ring_sent = ring_allreduce(grads)
naive, naive_sent = naive_allreduce(grads)

S = grads[0].nbytes
print(f'{n} workers, gradient of {size:,} float32 = {S / 1e6:.0f} MB each')
print('ring: all workers bitwise equal:', all(np.array_equal(r, ring[0]) for r in ring))
print(f'ring vs naive: {np.mean(ring[0] != naive[0]):.1%} of the sums differ, by at most {np.abs(ring[0] - naive[0]).max():.1e}')
print(f'max |error| against the float64 sum: ring {np.abs(ring[0] - exact).max():.1e}, naive {np.abs(naive[0] - exact).max():.1e}')
print(f'ring:  every worker sends {ring_sent[0] / 1e6:.1f} MB = {ring_sent[0] / S:.3f} S; formula 2(n-1)/n = {2 * (n - 1) / n:.3f}')
print(f'naive: worker 0 sends {naive_sent[0] / 1e6:.1f} MB = {naive_sent[0] / S:.0f} S and receives {naive_sent[1:].sum() / S:.0f} S')

print('\nTime to all-reduce 14 GB of bf16 gradients (7B parameters) at 50 GB/s per link, bandwidth term only:')
B, G = 50e9, 14e9
for k in [2, 4, 8, 16, 64]:
    print(f'  n = {k:2d}: ring {2 * (k - 1) / k * G / B:5.2f} s   naive {2 * (k - 1) * G / B:6.2f} s')
```

- **`np.array_split`** cuts a gradient into $n$ nearly equal chunks; with 1,000,000 elements and $n = 8$ they are exactly 125,000 elements, 0.5 MB each.
- **The messages of a step are built before any is applied** (`msgs`), because on real hardware all workers send at the same moment: worker $i + 1$ must forward the chunk it held at the start of the step, not the one it just received. Applying the messages one by one would let a chunk travel several hops in one step, a bug that this structure rules out.
- **Reduce-scatter** adds into the neighbour's chunk (`+=`); **all-gather** overwrites it (`=`), since the chunk it receives is already the complete sum.
- **`sent`** counts the bytes leaving each worker. Each sends $2(n - 1) = 14$ chunks of 0.5 MB, 7.0 MB, which is $1.75\, S$ for $S = 4$ MB, exactly $2(n - 1)/n$.
- **The naive scheme** puts everything through worker 0: it receives $7S$ and sends $7S$, 28 MB each way, four times what any ring worker sends, while the other workers' links sit idle.
- **Bitwise equal across workers.** In the ring, every chunk is summed once, by one chain of workers, and then copied, so all workers end with identical bits: the replicas of data parallelism stay exactly in step.
- **Not bitwise equal to the naive sum.** The ring adds the eight contributions to a chunk in a different order (starting at the chunk's own worker and going around), and [floating-point addition is not associative](/notes/acos/floating-point#order-matters-floating-point-addition-is-not-associative), so 51.9% of the 1,000,000 sums differ in their last bits, by at most $2.9 \times 10^{-6}$. Neither is more accurate; against a float64 reference, both are off by about $10^{-6}$, the rounding of float32 near the size of these sums. The same model trained on a different number of GPUs, or with a different all-reduce algorithm, is therefore a different computation, the hardware argument of the [reproducibility note](/notes/mlops/experiments-and-registry#what-a-training-run-depends-on).
- **The time table** applies the two cost formulas to the 14 GB gradient of a 7B model at 50 GB/s per link: the ring approaches $2S/B = 0.56$ s however many workers there are, while the naive time grows linearly, to 35 s for 64 workers, longer than the 1.7 s of computation in a step by a factor of 20.
