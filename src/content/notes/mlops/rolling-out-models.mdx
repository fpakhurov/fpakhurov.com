A new ranking model goes live for 5% of traffic. For the users it reaches it is 10% worse: they buy a little less often. Global conversion drops from 5.000% to 4.975%, a change of half a percent of itself.

With 60,000 users a day, the daily conversion rate already wobbles by about 1.8% of itself from noise alone. The bad model hides inside that wobble. It also returns HTTP 200 with well-formed rankings, so the error dashboards stay flat. The same arithmetic appears in Google's account of canaries, where a release that fails 20% of requests on a 5% canary shows up as about 1% more errors overall ([Google SRE Workbook, ch. 16](#references)).

So the question of this note is practical: how do we find out whether a new model is better, while exposing as few people as possible to a worse one?

## Why a model is harder to roll out than code

The DevOps course already rolls out code safely: one artifact promoted through environments, canaries judged statistically, cheap rollbacks ([continuous delivery note](/notes/devops/continuous-delivery)). All of it applies to models. Models add four difficulties ([Kästner, 2025, ch. 19](#references); [Huyen, 2022, ch. 9](#references)).

A worse model **fails silently**. Broken code throws an exception; a worse model returns plausible answers, and only the distribution of its predictions and the outcomes that follow can reveal it.

**Offline gains may not survive online.** The test set is yesterday's data, the offline metric is a proxy for the product's outcome, and users react to what the model shows them. The [design note](/notes/mlops/ml-system-design) separates the model's metric from the product's metric; only an online comparison measures the second.

**Effects are small and late.** A better ranker moves conversion by a few percent of itself. A better credit model changes default rates months later. Until the true labels arrive, we work with faster proxies: corrections and complaints, an immediate cancellation, a sample of production cases labelled by people. Each proxy is biased in its own way, and the rollout plan names the one it relies on.

**The model changes its own data.** A recommender decides what users see, and so what they click, and the clicks become the next training set. Two models running side by side can also leak effects into each other's groups.

## One ramp from 0% to 100%

The rollout is one ramp of exposure. Each phase answers a different question, and only one phase is meant for deciding whether the model is better ([Xu, Duan and Huang, 2018](#references); [Kohavi, Henne and Sommerfield, 2007](#references)).

<RolloutRamp />

The **shadow** phase shows the candidate to nobody: it answers a copy of real traffic, and we look for what breaks. The **canary** phase shows it to 1% and then 5% of users, long enough to catch a gross failure and short enough that few people suffer from it. The **measurement** phase splits users 50/50 for whole weeks and decides whether the candidate is better. The **full rollout** keeps a small long-term holdout, a few percent of users who stay on the old model so that slow effects can still be measured.

The early phases limit risk; they are not for decisions. A significant gain at 5% is weak evidence, as the section on sample sizes shows. A significant loss there, on errors or on a guardrail, is a reason to stop.

## Shadow: real inputs, no consequences

In a **shadow deployment**, also called a dark launch, every request (or a sample of them) is duplicated. The current model answers it; the candidate receives a copy, and its answer is logged and thrown away. Users see nothing of the candidate. In exchange the candidate meets real traffic: its real distribution of inputs, malformed requests and load peaks.

It finds three kinds of problems.

- Operational failures: crashes on inputs the test set lacked, latency under real load, memory growth.
- Skew: online features that differ from the ones the model was trained on. The [features note](/notes/mlops/features-and-skew) explains the causes; a shadow is often where they show, as predictions that look nothing like the offline ones.
- Different decisions: where and how often the two models disagree, and what that costs downstream.

A fraud model flags 1.2% of a million daily payments for manual review; the candidate, in shadow, flags 2.0% at the same threshold. That is 8,000 more reviews a day. Whether the review team can absorb them, or the threshold must move, is settled before any customer is affected.

The comparison is **paired**: both models score the same request, so their disagreement rate is measured precisely even on a small sample. When the new model replaces a human decision, the shadow can be compared with the human's decision, which gives a labelled estimate of its accuracy before launch.

A shadow cannot measure how users respond, because they only ever see the current model.

It also has to stay out of the way. The copy is sent asynchronously with a timeout, so a slow or crashing candidate never delays the real answer. The candidate must have **no side effects**: no writes to databases, no messages, no charges, no updates to features or caches that the current model reads. This is easy for a model that maps features to a score, and takes care for a model that acts, such as an agent that calls tools. Computing every request twice doubles the inference bill, so a 5–10% sample of traffic is often enough.

## Canary: catching large failures early

A canary gives the candidate's answers to a small share of real requests and compares it with the current model over the same time window. The statistics are those of a code canary: a threshold fixed in advance, a false-alarm rate and a power ([continuous delivery note](/notes/devops/continuous-delivery#judging-a-canary-on-a-small-sample)). The metrics are richer.

- The distribution of predictions: the share of positive decisions, the mean score, the share of each class, with the drift statistics of the [monitoring note](/notes/mlops/monitoring-and-drift).
- Degenerate outputs: constant predictions, default values, the fallback rate of the [serving note](/notes/mlops/serving-models#fallbacks).
- Fast outcome signals where they exist, such as clicks within a session.
- Segments: a model can be fine overall and broken for one country or one language, so the comparison is repeated per segment, with the [multiple-comparison](/notes/probability/hypothesis-tests#multiple-comparisons) correction this requires.

Every metric is broken down by model version. An aggregate over both versions dilutes the canary's failures, as the opening example showed. The aggregation interval must also be shorter than the canary phase itself; an hourly average cannot judge a 30-minute canary.

On Kubernetes, KServe expresses a canary as one field of an `InferenceService`. When the model's location changes, the new revision gets `canaryTrafficPercent` of the requests, and raising the field to 100 promotes it ([KServe documentation](#references)).

```yaml
apiVersion: serving.kserve.io/v1beta1
kind: InferenceService
metadata:
  name: ranker
spec:
  predictor:
    canaryTrafficPercent: 5
    model:
      modelFormat: {name: onnx}
      storageUri: s3://models.example.com/ranker/v2
```

The manifest follows the KServe 0.21 documentation and was not applied to a cluster. This split is per request, so one user may see both models in a session. That is acceptable for catching failures, and wrong for measuring outcomes.

## How many users, and why 50/50

The measurement phase is an **A/B test**, or online controlled experiment. Users, not requests, are assigned at random to the current model A or the candidate B, by a hash of the user's identifier and an experiment salt, `hash(salt + user_id) mod 100 < 50`. The assignment is fixed, so each user always sees one model. One number, the **overall evaluation criterion**, is chosen in advance: conversion per user, revenue per user, sessions per user ([Kohavi, Tang and Xu, 2020](#references)).

The [probability course](/notes/probability/hypothesis-tests#the-ab-test) builds the test and gives its sample size. For a metric with per-user standard deviations $\sigma_A$ and $\sigma_B$, a two-sided test at level $\alpha$ that should detect a true difference $\delta$ with power $1 - \beta$ needs

$$
n = \frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2 \,(\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2)}{\delta^2}
$$

users in each group. For $\alpha = 0.05$ and power 0.8 the first factor is $(1.960 + 0.842)^2 = 7.85$. For a conversion rate, $\sigma^2 = p(1 - p)$.

**Worked example.** A new ranker should raise conversion from 5.0% by 4% of itself, to 5.2%:

$$
n = \frac{7.85 \cdot (0.05 \cdot 0.95 + 0.052 \cdot 0.948)}{0.002^2} \approx 190{,}000 .
$$

That is 190,000 users per group, 380,000 in all. The formula has four levers, and each one changes $n$ a lot.

### The effect

Halving the effect quadruples $n$: a 2% lift needs 753,000 users per group, a 1% lift 3.0 million. The smallest effect worth detecting is a business decision, made before the test.

### The variance

Revenue per user has a standard deviation several times its mean, because most users spend nothing. With mean 2.00 and standard deviation 8.00, a 1% change needs 2.5 million users per group. Two remedies are standard. Capping extreme values (winsorizing) shrinks $\sigma$ at the cost of a small bias. **CUPED** subtracts the part of the metric that a pre-experiment covariate predicts, such as the same user's revenue last month ([Deng et al., 2013](#references)). With correlation $\rho$ between the two,

$$
\sigma^2_{\text{CUPED}} = (1 - \rho^2)\, \sigma^2 ,
$$

so $\rho = 0.6$ cuts the required $n$ by 36%, to 1.6 million.

### The share given to the candidate

With $N$ users in all and a share $q$ in the treatment, the variance of the estimated effect is proportional to

$$
\frac{1}{N q} + \frac{1}{N (1 - q)} = \frac{1}{N q (1 - q)} .
$$

It is smallest at $q = 1/2$. At $q = 5\%$ the same precision needs $0.25 / (0.05 \cdot 0.95) = 5.3$ times as many users. In the example, 380,000 users at 50/50 become 2.0 million at 5%: 6.3 days of a service with 60,000 new users a day become 33 days. This is why the measurement phase is 50/50, and why waiting for significance at the canary's 5% wastes a month.

### Who is analysed

A new ranker changes nothing for a user whose two rankings are identical. If the shadow showed that the models disagree for a fraction $f$ of users, the whole effect sits in that fraction. Averaged over everybody it is diluted to $f\delta_t$, where $\delta_t$ is the effect on the affected users, and $n$ grows by $1/f^2$. Analysing only the **triggered** users, those whose two answers differ, removes the dilution ([Kohavi, Tang and Xu, 2020, ch. 20](#references)). With $f = 20\%$ and a 4% overall lift, the triggered users see a 20% lift, from 5.0% to 6.0%. The test then needs 8,155 triggered users per group, which arrive among 40,800 users per group: about a fifth of the 190,000.

Triggering needs the counterfactual: for every user in A we compute, and log, what B would have answered, and the other way round. A shadow of the other model inside each group provides it.

### How long

Behaviour differs between weekdays and weekends, so the test runs whole weeks: the 6.3 days of the example become 7, and a slower product runs 14. Two weeks is also the minimum to see whether an effect fades.

## Looking before the end

The sample size assumes one look, at the end. Checking every day and stopping at the first $p < 0.05$ gives noise many chances to cross the threshold. The [probability note](/notes/probability/hypothesis-tests#peeking) shows this on A/A tests. For the 14-day experiment of the example, two identical models produce a "significant winner" in 21.8% of runs instead of 5%.

If we must look early, the threshold has to be stricter at every look. A constant threshold, chosen so that the overall false-positive rate stays at 5% over 14 daily looks, is $\lvert z \rvert > 2.61$; the Example finds it by simulation, and it matches Pocock's tables ([Pocock, 1977](#references)). The price is power. With this boundary the true 4% lift is detected in 67% of experiments instead of 80%. In exchange, many experiments stop early: counting those that never cross the boundary as running all 14 days, the average experiment ends on day 9.6.

Other designs move the strictness around: very strict early and close to 1.96 at the end ([O'Brien and Fleming, 1979](#references)), or p-values that stay valid however often they are checked ([Johari et al., 2017](#references)). Whatever the design, daily looks at guardrails are fine; daily decisions on the main metric are not.

## Can the result be trusted?

An experiment can be wrong in ways that no p-value shows. Four checks catch most of them.

### The sample ratio

If users were assigned 50/50, the group sizes must be consistent with 50/50. With 100,700 users in A and 99,300 in B, the chi-square test against an even split gives $\chi^2 = 9.80$ and $p = 0.0017$. Group A received 50.35% of the users instead of 50%, and with 200,000 users that is not chance. A **sample ratio mismatch** means that something between assignment and measurement depends on the group. The new model crashes on some clients, so they never log an outcome. Its slower answers time out some sessions. A bot filter treats the groups differently. The result is not trusted, however good it looks, until the cause is found ([Fabijan et al., 2019](#references)).

### An A/A test

Before the first model experiment, run the platform with the same model in both groups. It should find a difference in about 5% of metrics, and its group sizes should pass the sample-ratio test. An A/A test catches a wrong variance (computed per request when the unit is the user), broken hashing and logging that depends on the group ([Kohavi, Henne and Sommerfield, 2007](#references)).

### Novelty and primacy

Users click on what is new, so a changed ranker can win its first week on novelty alone. Users also resist change, so a better ranker can lose its first days. Plotting the effect by day shows both, and analysing only new users removes them.

### Interference

In a marketplace, a model that sends buyers to some sellers takes them from the sellers of the other group, so the groups are not independent. The remedy is to randomize by market or by time slot, at a cost in sample size.

## Deciding: better, or not worse?

The decision depends on why the new model exists.

A model meant to be *better* must show a gain. The test above is a superiority test: the candidate wins if the effect is significantly positive, and the gain is worth its cost.

A model meant to be *cheaper*, a quantized, distilled or smaller model, does not have to be better. It has to be not worse by more than an agreed margin $m$. "No significant difference" proves nothing here, because an underpowered test finds no difference in almost any case. The right test is one-sided, $H_0: \delta \le -m$ against $\delta > -m$, and it must be sized for the margin:

$$
n = \frac{(z_{1-\alpha} + z_{1-\beta})^2 \cdot 2\sigma^2}{m^2} .
$$

To show that a quantized ranker loses at most 2% of the 5% conversion rate, the test needs 587,000 users per group, three times the superiority test of the example. A margin of 1% needs 2.3 million. Proving that nothing got worse is expensive; the margin should be as wide as the business can accept.

**Guardrail metrics** are what the candidate must not make worse even when it wins: latency percentiles, error and fallback rates, revenue when the main metric is engagement, complaints, important segments. The same caution applies. A test sized for the main metric has little power for a noisy guardrail such as revenue, so it can only rule out large losses on it.

The stop rules are written before the rollout, because rules made during an incident are made under pressure:

| Signal | Example threshold | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Candidate errors and latency | SLO [burn rate](/notes/devops/slos-and-alerting#burn-rate) alert; p99 above the objective for 10 minutes | automatic rollback |
| Fallback rate, degenerate outputs | twice the current model's | automatic rollback |
| Prediction distribution | positive rate outside an agreed band | pause and investigate |
| Sample ratio | $p < 0.001$ | stop; fix the assignment or the logging |
| Guardrail | significantly worse | stop and roll back |
| Main metric, model meant to be better | no significant gain, or a gain below the model's cost | keep the current model |
| Main metric, model meant to be cheaper | non-inferiority within the margin not shown | keep the current model |

Rolling back must also be cheap. The previous version stays loaded (the `version_policy` of the [serving note's Example](/notes/mlops/serving-models#example)), or at least sits in the [registry](/notes/mlops/experiments-and-registry) under an alias the server resolves, so that going back is a change of one pointer. Features the old model needs keep being computed until the rollback window has passed: the expand-and-contract rule of the DevOps course, applied to features. For a model that writes state, such as stored embeddings or cached predictions, the plan also says what happens to what the bad version wrote.

## Rankers: interleaving

A ranking model outputs a list, and lists can be compared on the same user. **Interleaving** merges the lists of two rankers into one, shows it, and credits each click to the ranker that contributed the clicked item. Each user compares both rankers directly, so the differences between users, which dominate the noise of an A/B test, cancel.

**Team-draft interleaving** builds the merged list the way captains pick teams ([Radlinski, Kurup and Joachims, 2008](#references)). In each round a fair coin decides which ranker picks first, and each ranker adds its highest item that is not yet taken.

<TeamDraft />

Over many impressions, the share of impressions won by each ranker is compared with 50% by a sign test, ignoring ties. The coin keeps the comparison fair: neither ranker gets the top position more often.

On commercial web search, interleaving agreed with manual relevance judgements and reached reliable conclusions from far fewer queries than A/B comparisons of click metrics ([Chapelle et al., 2012](#references)). It has limits. It measures a preference between lists from clicks, and says nothing about retention or revenue. It needs both rankers to answer every request, which doubles the compute. It works for two or three rankers at a time. A common practice is therefore two stages: interleaving to pick among many candidates quickly, then an A/B test of the winner on the business metric. Position bias in clicks is the subject of the [learning-to-rank note](/notes/ml/learning-to-rank#clicks-and-position-bias).

## Bandits as an adaptive rollout

*Optional depth: adaptive allocation of traffic. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

An A/B test spends half its traffic on the worse model for its whole duration, by design: that is what keeps its estimate unbiased. A **multi-armed bandit** treats the models as arms and moves traffic towards the better one while it runs. **Thompson sampling** keeps a Beta posterior over each arm's conversion rate and sends each new user to the arm whose sampled rate is highest ([Russo et al., 2018](#references)); UCB rules, built in the [tree-search note](/notes/agents/games-and-tree-search#selection-as-a-bandit-problem-ucb1-and-uct), pick the arm with the highest optimistic estimate. The cost of serving the worse arm, the **regret**, grows only slowly.

Bandits suit short-lived choices with feedback within minutes or hours, such as headlines or thumbnails; contextual bandits are themselves a kind of model ([Li et al., 2010](#references)). For replacing one model with another they are rarer: adaptive allocation biases the estimate of the effect, an arm starved early gives guardrails little data, and an arm that looks good in week one through novelty collects its traffic while it looks good.

## Where it breaks: a winner that fades

A team tests a new ranker for two weeks at 50/50 with 190,000 users per group. The pooled result is a 4% lift, significant at the planned end, and the model ships.

The effect by day tells another story. In a toy version of it, the new ranker shows unfamiliar items, users explore them, and the lift is about 7% in the first week. In the second week it is about 1%. The pooled 4% is an average of a novelty effect that is ending and a real effect that is close to zero. A month after the full rollout, conversion is where it was, and the long-term holdout of the ramp shows it: the holdout group converts at the same rate as everyone else.

Three things in the plan would have caught it. The effect plotted by day shows the decay. New users, who have no habits to break, show the lasting effect without the novelty. And the holdout measures what remains after the experiment has ended. None of them needs more traffic; they need the plan to ask for them before the test starts.

## What you should remember

- A worse model fails silently and its effects are small, so it is rolled out on one ramp: shadow, canary, a 50/50 measurement phase in whole weeks, then 100% with a holdout. Only the 50/50 phase decides.
- A shadow is asynchronous and side-effect free; it finds crashes, latency, skew and the disagreement rate.
- Per group, $n = 7.85\,(\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2)/\delta^2$: 190,000 for a 4% lift on 5%. Halving $\delta$ quadruples $n$, CUPED multiplies it by $1 - \rho^2$, a 5% share costs 5.3 times the users, and analysing only the triggered users cuts it about $1/f$-fold.
- Daily stopping at $p < 0.05$ gives 21.8% false winners over 14 looks; a constant boundary of 2.61 keeps 5% at a cost in power.
- A sample ratio mismatch invalidates a result; an A/A test checks the platform first.
- A cheaper model needs a non-inferiority test sized for its margin; rollback criteria are written before the rollout, and the old version stays one pointer away.

## Example

The program sizes an A/B test for a ranker that should raise conversion from 5.0% by 4%. It then shows what the share given to the candidate and triggering do to that size, sizes a non-inferiority test for a cheaper model, runs one experiment with a sample-ratio check, and simulates 20,000 experiments with daily looks. It runs on Python 3.14.7 with NumPy 2.5 and SciPy 1.18, with a fixed seed.

```python
# Sizing and reading an A/B test of a new ranking model.
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
p_a, lift = 0.050, 0.04                      # control conversion per user, relative effect to detect
p_b = p_a * (1 + lift)
z_a, z_b = stats.norm.ppf(0.975), stats.norm.ppf(0.80)
k = (z_a + z_b) ** 2                         # 7.85 for a two-sided 5% test with power 0.8
var = p_a * (1 - p_a) + p_b * (1 - p_b)
n = int(np.ceil(k * var / (p_b - p_a) ** 2))
print(f'1. {p_a:.3f} -> {p_b:.4f}: n = {n:,} users per group')

# 2. The share q of traffic given to the candidate: the total N needed grows as 1 / (q (1 - q)).
for q in (0.50, 0.05):
    total = k * (var / 2) / (q * (1 - q)) / (p_b - p_a) ** 2
    print(f'2. share {q:.0%}: {total:,.0f} users in total, {total / 60_000:.1f} days at 60,000 a day')

# 3. Triggering: the models rank differently for 20% of users; the whole effect sits in that fifth.
f = 0.20
pt_b = p_a * (1 + lift / f)                  # lift among the triggered users that gives 4% overall
n_t = int(np.ceil(k * (p_a * (1 - p_a) + pt_b * (1 - pt_b)) / (pt_b - p_a) ** 2))
print(f'3. triggered users only: {n_t:,} per group, which needs {n_t / f:,.0f} users per group in all')

# 4. A cheaper model (quantized): show it is not worse by more than 2% of the rate, one-sided at 5%.
margin = 0.02 * p_a
n_ni = int(np.ceil((stats.norm.ppf(0.95) + z_b) ** 2 * 2 * p_a * (1 - p_a) / margin ** 2))
print(f'4. non-inferiority within 2%: {n_ni:,} users per group')

# 5. One experiment at size n with the effect present, and a sample-ratio check.
ca, cb = rng.binomial(n, p_a), rng.binomial(n, p_b)
ra, rb = ca / n, cb / n
se = np.sqrt(ra * (1 - ra) / n + rb * (1 - rb) / n)
z = (rb - ra) / se
print(f'5. A {ra:.4f}, B {rb:.4f}, z = {z:.2f}, 95% CI for the lift [{(rb - ra - z_a * se) / ra:+.1%}, {(rb - ra + z_a * se) / ra:+.1%}]')
chi2, p_srm = stats.chisquare([100_700, 99_300])
print(f'   sample ratio 100,700 vs 99,300: chi2 = {chi2:.2f}, p = {p_srm:.4f}')

# 6. Daily looks for 14 days: A/A runs give the false-positive rate, A/B runs the power.
days, per_day, runs = 14, n // 14, 20_000
m = per_day * np.arange(1, days + 1)
def z_path(p1):
    a = rng.binomial(per_day, p_a, (runs, days)).cumsum(1)
    b = rng.binomial(per_day, p1, (runs, days)).cumsum(1)
    pool = (a + b) / (2 * m)
    return (b - a) / m / np.sqrt(2 * pool * (1 - pool) / m)
aa, ab = z_path(p_a), z_path(p_b)
print(f'6. A/A, stop at the first |z| > 1.96: {np.mean((np.abs(aa) > z_a).any(1)):.3f} false winners')
c = np.quantile(np.abs(aa).max(1), 0.95)     # one constant boundary that keeps 5% over 14 looks
hit = np.abs(ab) > c
stop = np.where(hit.any(1), hit.argmax(1) + 1, days)
print(f'   boundary |z| > {c:.2f}: power {hit.any(1).mean():.3f} (one look at the end: '
      f'{np.mean(np.abs(ab[:, -1]) > z_a):.3f}), mean stop on day {stop.mean():.1f}')
```

```console
$ python3.14 ab_test.py
1. 0.050 -> 0.0520: n = 189,936 users per group
2. share 50%: 379,870 users in total, 6.3 days at 60,000 a day
2. share 5%: 1,999,316 users in total, 33.3 days at 60,000 a day
3. triggered users only: 8,155 per group, which needs 40,775 users per group in all
4. non-inferiority within 2%: 587,343 users per group
5. A 0.0499, B 0.0515, z = 2.17, 95% CI for the lift [+0.3%, +5.9%]
   sample ratio 100,700 vs 99,300: chi2 = 9.80, p = 0.0017
6. A/A, stop at the first |z| > 1.96: 0.218 false winners
   boundary |z| > 2.61: power 0.665 (one look at the end: 0.795), mean stop on day 9.6
```

Line 1 is the sample-size formula with $\sigma^2 = p(1 - p)$ in each group. Part 2 runs the same experiment at 50/50 and at the canary's 5%: a month of traffic instead of a week. Line 3 analyses only the users whose two rankings differ, and needs a fifth of the users. Line 4 is the price of proving that a quantized ranker is at most 2% worse: three times the superiority test.

Line 5 is one experiment in which the effect is real. The test rejects, but the 95% interval for the lift runs from 0.3% to 5.9%, and the interval is what should be reported. Part 6 stops at the first daily $p < 0.05$ and finds a winner between two identical models in 21.8% of runs; the boundary of 2.61 keeps 5% at the cost of power 0.665 instead of 0.795.
