Two courses end where this one begins. The [DevOps course](/notes/devops/what-devops-is) ends with a service that is built from a commit, shipped as an image, rolled out gradually, watched by its SLOs and rolled back when it misbehaves. The [Deep Learning course](/notes/deep-learning/training-in-practice) ends with a network trained by a careful recipe: checked at initialization, tracked run by run, reproducible from a seed. Put the network inside the service and the two stories do not simply add up. The service now depends on data that nobody versions, its behaviour changes when the world changes even if no one commits anything, and its failures rarely raise an exception: a wrong prediction is returned with status 200. This course is about running models as dependable products. This first note asks what the rest of an ML system is made of, which kinds of debt accumulate around a model, how the loop of building and operating a model differs from the loop of shipping code, and how the course is organised.

## Baseline: a model handed over in a file

The arrangement that most teams start with looks like the wall of the [DevOps course](/notes/devops/what-devops-is#baseline-throw-it-over-the-wall), with a model in place of a release. A data scientist pulls a few months of payments into a notebook, cleans them, tries several models and reaches a good validation score. The winner is saved with `pickle`, together with a note on which columns it expects, and handed to the engineers who own the checkout service. They write a scoring endpoint around it, reimplement the cleaning steps in the service's language, and deploy it.

For a while it works. Then three things happen, none of which produces an error.

- **The data change.** An upstream team improves a signal the model uses, or changes its units, or a join starts to miss rows. The model receives inputs unlike the ones it was trained on and keeps answering.
- **The world changes.** Fraudsters adapt to the model that blocks them. Six months later the patterns it learnt are partly obsolete, and nobody knows by how much, because the labels (chargebacks) arrive weeks after the payments.
- **Nobody can redo it.** Retraining means finding the notebook, the exact query, the data as they were that day and the library versions, and recovering which of the dozens of runs was shipped. Usually some of these are lost, so the next model is a new project rather than an update.

The model was the part everyone worked on, and it was not where the trouble came from.

## The model is a small box

[Sculley et al. (2015)](#references), engineers at Google, described what real ML systems consist of, and their picture has become the standard starting point: the code that learns or predicts is a small box surrounded by much larger infrastructure. The drawing below is built on the same idea, arranged along the path of one payment through the fraud system of the running example.

<MlSystem />

1. **Data collection and verification.** Events are gathered from the checkout, the card network and the chargeback process, joined, and checked before anything is trained on them.
2. **Features.** Raw events become the numbers a model consumes, computed once for training from history and again for serving from live data, which must agree.
3. **The model.** Training code and the fitted parameters: the part a machine-learning course is about.
4. **Serving.** An endpoint inside the checkout's latency budget, with a fallback when it fails.
5. **Monitoring.** The inputs, the predictions and, later, the outcomes, compared with what was expected.
6. **Around everything.** Configuration, compute, orchestration of the jobs, analysis tools and the processes that tie them together.

The point is proportion, not exact areas: [Sculley et al.](#references) estimate that a mature system may be at most about 5% machine-learning code. The rest is the subject of this course. The same paper lists the ways in which the rest becomes expensive, which is a useful catalogue of what to design against.

## Hidden technical debt

**Technical debt** is the cost of the changes that a shortcut today will make expensive tomorrow ([Cunningham, 1992](#references)). ML systems carry all of the usual software debt, and [Sculley et al.](#references) argue that they add their own, which is harder to see because it lives at the level of the system, in data and behaviour, rather than in any one file. Six kinds matter throughout the course.

### Entanglement: changing anything changes everything

A model mixes its inputs. If one input's distribution changes, the weights and the use of every other input can change on the next training, and the decision threshold tuned for the old model no longer means what it meant. [Sculley et al.](#references) call this the **CACE principle**: Changing Anything Changes Everything, and it applies to hyperparameters, sampling and data selection as well as to features.

A small experiment shows both effects. The fraud model uses a device-reputation score computed by our team and an IP-reputation score computed by another team; both are noisy measurements of how risky a payment really is. The other team improves its score (less noise, $\sigma$ from 2 to 0.5) and tells no one:

```python
# CACE: another team improves one input signal, and our model changes in two ways.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 50_000
risk = rng.normal(size=n)                                  # how risky a payment really is (never observed)
fraud = rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(4.0 - 2.0 * risk))  # about 7% of payments are fraud
device = risk + rng.normal(scale=1.0, size=n)              # device reputation, computed by our team
ip_old = risk + rng.normal(scale=2.0, size=n)              # IP reputation, computed by another team
ip_new = risk + rng.normal(scale=0.5, size=n)              # the same score after that team improved it
amount = rng.normal(size=n)                                # a feature unrelated to fraud

X_old = np.column_stack([device, ip_old, amount])
X_new = np.column_stack([device, ip_new, amount])
model = LogisticRegression().fit(X_old, fraud)
threshold = np.quantile(model.decision_function(X_old), 0.98)   # flag the riskiest 2% of payments

def report(name, m, X):
    flagged = m.decision_function(X) >= threshold
    w = m.coef_[0]
    print(f'{name}  device {w[0]:+.2f}  ip {w[1]:+.2f}  amount {w[2]:+.2f}'
          f'  flagged {flagged.mean():.1%}  precision {fraud[flagged].mean():.0%}')

report('A. old ip score, old model  ', model, X_old)
report('B. new ip score, old model  ', model, X_new)
report('C. new ip score, retrained  ', LogisticRegression().fit(X_new, fraud), X_new)
```

```console
$ python3 cace.py
A. old ip score, old model    device +0.75  ip +0.20  amount -0.02  flagged 2.0%  precision 42%
B. new ip score, old model    device +0.75  ip +0.20  amount -0.02  flagged 1.6%  precision 47%
C. new ip score, retrained    device +0.30  ip +1.25  amount -0.01  flagged 3.8%  precision 49%
```

Line A is the system as deployed: 2% of payments go to manual review, and 42% of those are fraud. Line B is the morning after the upstream improvement. Nothing in our code changed, yet the share of flagged payments moved from 2.0% to 1.6%: the old model's small weight on the IP score was fitted to a noisy signal, and the cleaner signal shifts its scores. Line C is the next scheduled retraining. The model now leans on the IP score and drops the weight of the device score from 0.75 to 0.30, so our own feature, which no one touched, matters less than half as much as before. The threshold, fixed when the model was first tuned, now flags 3.8% of payments: the review team's queue nearly doubles overnight. Every number moved because of a change in someone else's code.

Mitigations are partial: retune thresholds automatically from held-out data as part of every training run, monitor the share of flagged payments with an alert on a sudden change (an **action limit** in the paper's terms), and treat upstream signals as versioned dependencies that change only on purpose, which is the subject of the [data notes](/notes/mlops/data-quality-and-versioning).

### Undeclared consumers

A model's predictions are usually easy to read: they are logged, written to a table, returned by an API. Other teams start to use them without telling the owner. The fraud score of the running example might feed a marketing model that avoids discounts for risky customers, or a dashboard the finance team uses to forecast losses. These **undeclared consumers** couple the model to systems its owners do not know about, so a change that improves fraud detection can break them, and a model that feeds its own future training data through such a consumer creates a hidden feedback loop. The remedy is the one for any interface: access control on the outputs, and a declared contract with each consumer, which the [data pipelines note](/notes/mlops/data-pipelines#data-contracts) develops.

### Glue code and pipeline jungles

**Glue code** is everything written to move data into and out of a general-purpose ML package: format conversions, wrappers, adapters. It freezes the system around the package's quirks and makes trying another package expensive. A **pipeline jungle** is glue code for data preparation that grew one source at a time: scrapes, joins, samplings and intermediate files whose order and purpose nobody remembers, testable only end to end. Both are prevented by design rather than by care: a common interface around models, and data preparation built as an explicit graph of small, idempotent, tested steps, which is what the [next section of the course](/notes/mlops/data-pipelines#a-pipeline-is-a-graph-of-tasks) builds.

### Configuration debt

An ML system has many settings: which features, which time range of data, which filters, which hyperparameters, which thresholds, which features are unavailable before a certain date or in production. [Sculley et al.](#references) observe that in mature systems the lines of configuration can outnumber the lines of code, and that configuration is rarely reviewed or tested with the same care. A threshold changed in a dashboard, a feature list edited by hand, a training date range typed into a notebook: each is a production change with no history. The rules for good configuration follow the ones for code: small diffs from a previous configuration, mistakes hard to make, every configuration under version control and code review, and checks that assert facts about it (the number of features, that every feature exists in production).

### Other debts in brief

The paper lists more, and later notes take them up where they belong: **unstable data dependencies** (inputs that change under the model, as in the experiment above), **underutilized ones** (features that add little and break often), **feedback loops** (a model that influences its own training data, such as a fraud model whose declined payments never get a label), **dead experimental code paths**, **fixed thresholds in a changing system**, and debts of testing, reproducibility and process. A useful test of a system's debt, from the same paper: how easily can an entirely new approach be tested at full scale, and does improving one model or signal degrade others?

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> A model in production is a system whose behaviour is set by code, data and configuration together, and two of those change without a commit. MLOps applies the discipline of DevOps (versioning, automation, testing, gradual rollout, monitoring) to all three, and adds what code does not need: validation of data, tracking of experiments and lineage, retraining as a routine operation, and monitoring of quality, not only of uptime, because a model fails silently.</aside>

## The lifecycle loop

A model is never finished. It is trained on the past, used on the present, and goes stale as the present drifts away from the past. Its life is therefore a loop rather than a line, and the loop has a fixed set of stages ([Huyen, 2022, chapters 1–2](#references); [Kästner, 2025, chapters 2 and 11](#references); [Kazmierczak, Salama and Huerta, 2024](#references)):

<LifecycleLoop />

1. **Data.** Collect events and labels, validate them, and turn them into a versioned training set.
2. **Train.** Fit a model with recorded code, configuration, data version and environment.
3. **Evaluate.** Compare the candidate with the model in production on fresh held-out data, by the metrics the product cares about, and stop at a gate if it is worse.
4. **Deploy.** Ship the model as a service or a batch job, gradually, with the previous version ready to take over.
5. **Monitor.** Watch inputs, predictions and outcomes; compare them with training data and with expectations.
6. **Retrain.** On a schedule, or when monitoring signals drift or decay, start the loop again on newer data.

The same loop is drawn for software in the DevOps course as plan, code, build, test, release, deploy, operate, monitor. The difference is in what can start a new turn. Software changes when someone commits. A model changes when someone commits, when new data arrive, and when the world drifts, which needs no one at all. That third trigger is what makes MLOps more than DevOps with a model inside.

| | Software (DevOps) | ML system (MLOps) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What defines the behaviour | code and configuration | code, configuration, data and the fitted parameters |
| What is versioned | the commit, the image digest | also the dataset snapshot, the model and its lineage |
| What triggers a change | a commit | a commit, new data, drift in the world |
| How correctness is tested | assertions with exact answers | also statistical thresholds on data and on model quality |
| How a failure shows | errors, crashes, latency | often only as worse predictions, seen later through labels |
| Reproducing a result | rebuild from the commit and lockfile | also the data as of a date, seeds, sometimes the hardware |
| A routine operation | deploy a new version | also retrain the same code on new data |

Google's MLOps guide names the new routine **continuous training** (CT), beside continuous integration and continuous delivery ([Kazmierczak, Salama and Huerta, 2024](#references)): retraining and redeploying the model automatically as part of normal operation. It also widens the old two. CI tests data, schemas and models as well as code, and CD delivers not just a model but a training pipeline that in turn produces and deploys models.

## Maturity levels

Organisations reach this discipline in stages, and two published maturity models describe the stages. They are useful as a vocabulary for "where are we and what is the next step", not as a certification.

### Google's levels 0, 1 and 2

The guide of [Kazmierczak, Salama and Huerta](#references) (Google Cloud Architecture Center, last reviewed 28 August 2024) has three levels.

- **Level 0, manual process.** Every step is done by hand in notebooks and scripts; a data scientist hands a model to engineers; releases are rare; only the prediction service is deployed; nothing watches the model's quality in production. This is the baseline of this note.
- **Level 1, ML pipeline automation.** The steps from data extraction to model validation become an orchestrated pipeline, and that pipeline, rather than a model, is what is deployed. It retrains in production on fresh data (continuous training), triggered on a schedule, on new data, on a drop in quality or on a change in the data distribution. The same pipeline code runs in development and in production. Data and model validation steps act as gates, a metadata store records every run and its artifacts, and a feature store may serve the same features to training and serving.
- **Level 2, CI/CD pipeline automation.** The pipeline itself is built, tested and deployed by a CI/CD system, so that new ideas for features, architectures or training code reach production quickly and safely. A model registry, a feature store, a metadata store and an orchestrator are all in place.

The progression matters: level 1 automates retraining the *same* model on new data, level 2 automates shipping *new* pipelines. A team with a fixed problem and drifting data needs level 1 long before level 2.

### Microsoft's five levels

The Azure Architecture Center's MLOps maturity model (page dated 3 June 2026) splits the same path into five levels and describes each by people, model creation, model release and application integration ([Microsoft, 2026](#references)): **0, no MLOps** (manual everything, a single model file handed off); **1, DevOps but no MLOps** (application builds and tests automated, models still trained and released by hand); **2, automated training** (managed, tracked, reproducible training; releases still manual); **3, automated model deployment** (releases through CI/CD with traceability from deployment back to data, and A/B testing); **4, full MLOps automated operations** (production metrics trigger retraining, promotion by policy). Its level 1 is a state many teams are in: a mature software pipeline with a model wedged into it.

Both models agree on the order: first make training reproducible and automated, then make deployment automated, then close the loop from monitoring back to training. The Example at the end of this note runs one turn of that loop by hand.

## Roles

An ML system needs more kinds of expertise than one person has, and teams usually split the work as follows ([Kästner, 2025, chapter 21](#references); [Huyen, 2022, chapter 11](#references)).

- **Data scientists** frame the problem, explore data, choose and evaluate models, and own the question "is the model good enough, and for whom".
- **Data engineers** build and run the pipelines that collect, clean and store data, and own its freshness, quality and contracts with producers.
- **ML engineers** turn a modelling approach into production software: training pipelines, evaluation gates, serving, monitoring and retraining. They sit between the two roles above and the platform.
- **ML platform engineers** build the shared machinery that every model team uses: the orchestrator, the feature store, the experiment tracker and model registry, the serving platform, the GPU scheduling. They play for models the part that [platform engineering](/notes/devops/what-devops-is#devops-sre-and-platform-engineering) plays for services.
- Around them: **software engineers** of the product that calls the model, **SREs** who keep it within its SLOs, **product managers** who decide what the model is for, and domain experts, here fraud analysts, who label cases and review the model's decisions.

The failure mode of the baseline is the same as in DevOps: a wall between the people who build models and those who run them. The cure is also the same: one team owns the model in production, from data to monitoring, and the platform makes the routine parts self-service. In a small company the five roles may be two people; the responsibilities still exist.

## What changed

| | A model handed over in a file (baseline) | An ML system operated with MLOps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is shipped | a pickled model and a note | a versioned pipeline that produces, evaluates and deploys models |
| Data | a query run once in a notebook | validated, versioned snapshots with lineage to every model |

| Who owns the model in production | nobody in particular | one team, on a shared platform |

## Limitations

- **Sculley's picture is a picture.** The box sizes in the paper and in the figure here are illustrative. The proportion of ML code varies widely, and for a system built around a large language model the model's serving may be the most expensive component, as later notes show.
- **Maturity models are vendor documents.** Both describe the path in terms of their vendor's products, change between revisions and are not validated by measurement. They are useful checklists; the levels are not a score to maximise.
- **Not every model needs the full loop.** A model trained once for a stable problem, or used offline for analysis, may be best served by level 0 done carefully. The machinery pays off when models change often, when there are many of them, or when mistakes are expensive.
- **The roles are a division of labour, not a hierarchy of titles.** Hiring an "MLOps engineer" to sit between the data scientists and production recreates the wall that the role was meant to remove.
- **Tools move quickly.** Product names and versions in this course are those of October 2026; the mechanisms they implement change much more slowly.

## What you should remember

- The model is a small part of an ML system; data collection and verification, features, serving, monitoring, configuration and process make up most of it ([Sculley et al., 2015](#references)).
- Hidden technical debt in ML: entanglement and CACE (changing one input or setting changes the use of all of them and invalidates fixed thresholds), undeclared consumers, glue code, pipeline jungles, configuration debt, unstable data dependencies and feedback loops.
- The lifecycle is a loop: data, train, evaluate, deploy, monitor, retrain. Unlike software, it can be restarted by new data and by drift, without any commit; continuous training is the new routine beside CI and CD.
- ML failures are usually silent: wrong answers with status 200. Check inputs and the distribution of outputs immediately, and quality as soon as labels arrive.
- Google's levels: 0 manual, 1 automated pipeline with continuous training, 2 CI/CD for the pipeline itself. Microsoft's five levels split the same path: no MLOps, DevOps without MLOps, automated training, automated deployment, full automation.
- Data scientists, data engineers, ML engineers and platform engineers share the work; one team should own each model in production.

## Example

The program below runs one turn of the lifecycle loop for the fraud model of the running example, with the two kinds of failure from the baseline: an upstream unit change and a change in the world. Payments arrive in weeks of 20,000. In week 5 the upstream service starts sending amounts in cents, and in week 6 the bug is fixed; from week 6 fraudsters also move to smaller amounts. The model sees three features (the logarithm of the amount, whether the device is new, whether the card is foreign) and sends the riskiest 3% of payments to manual review.

```python
"""One turn of the ML lifecycle loop for a fraud model: train, evaluate, deploy, monitor, retrain."""
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N = 20_000                                          # payments per week

def week(w):
    """Synthetic payments of week w. From week 5 the upstream service sends amounts in cents
    (fixed in week 6); from week 6 fraudsters move to smaller amounts."""
    fraud = rng.random(N) < 0.02
    big = 5.0 if w < 6 else 3.8                     # log-mean amount of a fraudulent payment, in euros
    amount = np.where(fraud, rng.lognormal(big, 0.8, N), rng.lognormal(3.5, 1.0, N)).round(2)
    new_device = rng.random(N) < np.where(fraud, 0.6, 0.1)
    foreign = rng.random(N) < np.where(fraud, 0.5, 0.15)
    sent = amount * 100 if w == 5 else amount       # what the upstream service actually sends
    return sent, new_device, foreign, fraud

def features(amount, new_device, foreign):
    return np.column_stack([np.log1p(amount), new_device, foreign])

data = {w: week(w) for w in range(1, 11)}
X = {w: features(*data[w][:3]) for w in data}
y = {w: data[w][3] for w in data}

# 1. Train on weeks 1-2 and 2. evaluate on week 3, which the model has not seen.
model = LogisticRegression().fit(np.vstack([X[1], X[2]]), np.concatenate([y[1], y[2]]))
threshold = np.quantile(model.predict_proba(np.vstack([X[1], X[2]]))[:, 1], 0.97)  # review 3% of payments
auc = roc_auc_score(y[3], model.predict_proba(X[3])[:, 1])
print(f'train weeks 1-2, evaluate on week 3: AUC {auc:.3f}, gate AUC >= 0.85: {"pass" if auc >= 0.85 else "fail"}')

# 3. Deploy and 4. monitor: inputs and outputs every week, labels (chargebacks) two weeks later.
ref_median = np.median(np.concatenate([data[1][0], data[2][0]]))
print('week  median amount  flagged  input check  output check  recall (known 2 weeks later)')
for w in range(3, 9):
    amount, *_ = data[w]
    flagged = model.predict_proba(X[w])[:, 1] >= threshold
    ratio = np.median(amount) / ref_median
    inputs = 'ALERT' if not 0.5 < ratio < 2 else 'ok'
    outputs = 'ALERT' if flagged.mean() > 2 * 0.03 else 'ok'           # action limit: twice the planned rate
    recall = flagged[y[w]].mean()
    print(f'{w:>4}  {np.median(amount):>10.2f} €  {flagged.mean():>6.1%}  {inputs:>11}  {outputs:>12}  {recall:>8.0%}')

# 5. Retrain on weeks 7-8 (after the fix), compare with the production model on week 9, deploy only if better.
cand = LogisticRegression().fit(np.vstack([X[7], X[8]]), np.concatenate([y[7], y[8]]))
for name, m in [('production', model), ('candidate', cand)]:
    p = m.predict_proba(X[9])[:, 1]
    t = np.quantile(p, 0.97)                        # the same review budget for both
    print(f'{name:>10} on week 9: AUC {roc_auc_score(y[9], p):.3f}, recall at 3% reviewed {(p >= t)[y[9]].mean():.0%}')
```

Reading it stage by stage:

- **`week`** generates the data and plants both failures. About 2% of payments are fraud, far more than in a real shop, so that a synthetic week contains a few hundred frauds to measure; fraudulent amounts are larger until week 6. The variable `sent` is what the model receives, which in week 5 is not what the payment was.
- **Train and evaluate.** The model is fitted on weeks 1–2 and judged on week 3, data it has not seen and that comes after its training data in time, as production data will ([validation that matches deployment](/notes/ml/validation-and-overfitting#splits-that-match-deployment)). The gate is a fixed rule written before the result was known: an AUC of at least 0.85. It passes with 0.945. The threshold is chosen so that 3% of payments go to review, the capacity of the review team.
- **Monitor inputs.** The median amount sent this week is compared with the median of the training weeks, 34 €; a ratio outside 0.5 to 2 raises an alert. In week 5 the median is 3,416 €, a factor of 100, and the alert fires on the first day's data. No exception was raised anywhere: the model scored every payment and the service answered every request.
- **Monitor outputs.** The share of flagged payments has an action limit of twice the planned 3%. In week 5 it is 96.9%: almost every customer would have been sent to review or, with an automatic decline, refused. Either check alone would have caught this failure.
- **Monitor quality.** Recall, the share of fraud that the review catches, needs labels, and chargebacks arrive about two weeks later, so the last column is known only in week $w + 2$. In weeks 6–8 the inputs look normal (median 33 €), the flag rate even falls (2.3–2.7%), and both checks stay quiet, yet recall has collapsed from 55% to about 20%: fraudsters now use amounts the model considers safe. This is the silent failure, and only the delayed labels reveal it.
- **Retrain, evaluate, deploy.** A candidate trained on weeks 7–8, after the unit bug was fixed (training on week 5 would have taught the model that amounts are in cents), is compared with the production model on week 9 at the same review budget. It catches 37% of fraud against 25%, so it replaces the production model, and the loop starts again. The candidate is better but still far from the 55% of the first weeks: the new fraud looks more like ordinary payments, and better features, not only fresher data, are needed. That is a modelling question, which the loop surfaces but does not answer.

The whole loop fits in forty lines because everything is in memory and runs once. In production each stage is a separate job with its own failure modes: data arrive late and twice, checks need thresholds that do not cry wolf, the training data must be exactly reproducible, and the new model must reach users without a risky switch. The rest of the course takes the stages one at a time.
