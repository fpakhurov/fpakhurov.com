[Search](/notes/agents/search-and-planning) returned a sequence of actions, which works when every action has one known outcome. When a step can go wrong, a wheel slips or a test is flaky, the agent cannot commit to a sequence in advance: after each step it may find itself somewhere it did not intend, and it needs to know what to do there too. The object to compute is then a **policy**, an action for every state, and the criterion is the expected sum of rewards rather than the cost of one path. This note sets up the standard model of that problem, the Markov decision process, derives the two Bellman equations that its values satisfy, and solves them with value iteration and policy iteration on a small gridworld. The same equations are the starting point of the whole reinforcement-learning section.

**Notation.** $\mathcal S$ and $\mathcal A$ are finite sets of states and actions. $P(s' \mid s, a)$ is the probability of moving to $s'$ after action $a$ in $s$, $R(s, a, s')$ the reward received on that transition, and $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ the discount. $S_t, A_t, R_{t+1}$ are the random state, action and reward at step $t$. A policy $\pi(a \mid s)$ gives the probability of action $a$ in state $s$; a deterministic policy is written $\pi(s)$. $G_t$ is the return from step $t$; $V^\pi$ and $Q^\pi$ are the state and action values of $\pi$, $V^\star$ and $Q^\star$ the optimal ones. For a vector $V$ indexed by states, $\lVert V \rVert_\infty = \max_s |V(s)|$. $\mathbb E_\pi$ is an expectation over trajectories generated by $\pi$ and $P$.

## Baseline: a fixed plan in a random world

The example of the note is the 4 × 3 gridworld of [Russell and Norvig (2021)](#references), chapter 17. The agent moves on a grid of four columns and three rows, cells written $(\text{column}, \text{row})$ from $(1, 1)$ at the bottom left. Cell $(2, 2)$ is a wall. Entering $(4, 3)$, the goal, gives a reward of $+1$; entering $(4, 2)$, the pit, gives $-1$; both end the episode. Every other step gives $-0.04$, a small cost of time. The actions are N, E, S and W, and they are unreliable: an action moves in its direction with probability $0.8$ and slips to each side, at right angles, with probability $0.1$. A move into the wall or off the grid leaves the agent where it is. We use the discount $\gamma = 0.9$.

Ignore the slips and search finds the shortest path from $(1, 1)$: N, N, E, E, E. Executed blindly, this plan reaches the goal with probability $0.8^5 + 0.1^4 \cdot 0.8 = 0.32776$: either every action succeeds, or the agent slips its way round the other side of the wall (E, E, N, N, then the final E succeeds). It ends in the pit with probability $0.014$, and otherwise stands somewhere on the grid with no plan left. Its expected discounted reward over the five steps is $0.051$. The policy computed below, which reacts to where the agent actually is, is worth $0.374$ from the same start. A fixed plan cannot react; a policy can.

## Markov decision processes

A **Markov decision process** (MDP) is a tuple $(\mathcal S, \mathcal A, P, R, \gamma)$ ([Bellman, 1957](#references); [Puterman, 1994](#references)). Its defining assumption is the **Markov property**: the next state depends only on the current state and action,

$$
\Pr(S_{t+1} = s' \mid S_t, A_t, S_{t-1}, A_{t-1}, \dots, S_0) = P(s' \mid S_t, A_t),
$$

the same property that defines a [Markov chain](/notes/probability/markov-chains#the-markov-property), with the agent's choice added. A policy $\pi$ fixed, the states form an ordinary Markov chain with transition matrix $P_\pi(s, s') = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) P(s' \mid s, a)$.

The agent's objective is the **return**, the discounted sum of future rewards:

$$
G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k+1} .
$$

The discount $\gamma < 1$ does three jobs. It keeps the sum finite: if $|R| \le R_{\max}$, then $|G_t| \le R_{\max}(1 + \gamma + \gamma^2 + \dots) = R_{\max}/(1 - \gamma)$. It expresses a preference for reward sooner rather than later. And it can be read as a probability $1 - \gamma$ that the process stops at each step. Episodic tasks, such as the gridworld, end in **terminal** states; we model them as absorbing states with reward 0, so the same formulas cover both cases.

The **value** of a policy is its expected return from each state, and the **action value** is the expected return after taking a given first action:

$$
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb E_\pi\big[\, G_t \mid S_t = s \,\big], \qquad Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb E_\pi\big[\, G_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a \,\big] .
$$

The optimal values are $V^\star(s) = \max_\pi V^\pi(s)$ and $Q^\star(s, a) = \max_\pi Q^\pi(s, a)$. For a finite MDP with $\gamma < 1$ there is one deterministic, stationary policy $\pi^\star$ that attains $V^\star(s)$ in every state at once ([Puterman, 1994](#references), section 6.2); the rest of the note computes it.

## The Bellman expectation equation

The value of a state is the expected reward of one step plus the discounted value of the state where that step lands. The derivation uses four facts in order: the return is recursive, expectation is linear, the [law of total expectation](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation) splits over the first action and the next state, and the Markov property removes the past:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V^\pi(s) &= \mathbb E_\pi\big[\, R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1} \mid S_t = s \,\big] && G_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1} \\
&= \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, \mathbb E_\pi\big[\, R_{t+1} + \gamma G_{t+1} \mid S_t = s, A_t = a, S_{t+1} = s' \,\big] && \text{total expectation over } A_t, S_{t+1} \\
&= \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \Big( R(s, a, s') + \gamma\, \mathbb E_\pi\big[\, G_{t+1} \mid S_{t+1} = s' \,\big] \Big) && \text{linearity; Markov property} \\
&= \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \big( R(s, a, s') + \gamma V^\pi(s') \big) . && \text{definition of } V^\pi
\end{aligned}
$$

The Markov property is used in the third line: once $S_{t+1} = s'$ is known, the rewards after it do not depend on $s$ or $a$, so the conditional expectation of $G_{t+1}$ is the value of $s'$. The result is the **Bellman expectation equation**:

$$
\boxed{\; V^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, \big( R(s, a, s') + \gamma V^\pi(s') \big) \;}
$$

and in the same way $Q^\pi(s, a) = \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \big( R(s, a, s') + \gamma \sum_{a'} \pi(a' \mid s') Q^\pi(s', a') \big)$.

These are $|\mathcal S|$ linear equations in $|\mathcal S|$ unknowns. In vector form, with $R_\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) R(s, a, s')$ the expected one-step reward,

$$
V^\pi = R_\pi + \gamma P_\pi V^\pi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad V^\pi = (I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1} R_\pi .
$$

The inverse exists: the rows of $P_\pi$ are probability vectors, so $\lVert P_\pi V \rVert_\infty \le \lVert V \rVert_\infty$, and $\gamma P_\pi$ shrinks every vector by at least the factor $\gamma < 1$; the series $I + \gamma P_\pi + \gamma^2 P_\pi^2 + \dots$ converges to $(I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1}$. Evaluating a policy in an MDP with known $P$ and $R$ is a linear solve. **Worked example.** The policy "always go N" never reaches the goal from $(1, 1)$ except by slipping, and pays $-0.04$ for every step on the way; solving the system gives $V^\pi(1, 1) = -0.319$.

## The Bellman optimality equation

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> The best thing to do now is the action whose expected immediate reward plus discounted best value afterwards is largest. Because the future depends only on the state reached, the best value afterwards does not depend on how the agent got there, and the optimal values satisfy one fixed-point equation that can be solved without enumerating policies.</aside>

Two steps lead to it.

**From $V^\star$ to $Q^\star$.** Take action $a$ in $s$, land in $s'$, and from then on act optimally. By the Markov property the problem that starts in $s'$ is the same whatever happened before, so the best expected return from $s'$ is $V^\star(s')$, and

$$
Q^\star(s, a) = \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \big( R(s, a, s') + \gamma V^\star(s') \big) .
$$

**From $Q^\star$ to $V^\star$.** A policy that is free in $s$ can put all its probability on the best first action. Any other choice of $\pi(\cdot \mid s)$ gives a weighted average of the action values, $\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) Q^\star(s, a)$, which is at most their maximum. So $V^\star(s) = \max_a Q^\star(s, a)$. Substituting the first line into the second:

$$
\boxed{\; V^\star(s) = \max_{a} \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, \big( R(s, a, s') + \gamma V^\star(s') \big) \;}
$$

This is the **Bellman optimality equation**. Unlike the expectation equation it is not linear, because of the maximum. Once $V^\star$ is known, the optimal policy is **greedy** with respect to it: $\pi^\star(s) = \arg\max_a Q^\star(s, a)$. The operation on the right-hand side, applied to any $V$, is called a **Bellman backup**:

<BellmanBackup />

1. **State.** In $s = (3, 3)$, next to the goal, the four actions are candidates.
2. **Outcomes.** Action E reaches the goal with probability $0.8$, slips up into the edge and stays with $0.1$, slips down to $(3, 2)$ with $0.1$.
3. **Backed-up values.** Each outcome contributes its reward plus $\gamma$ times the value of the state it lands in: $1 + 0.9 \cdot 0 = 1$ for the goal, whose value is 0 because the episode ends; $-0.04 + 0.9 \cdot 0.928 = 0.795$ for staying; $-0.04 + 0.9 \cdot 0.585 = 0.486$ for $(3, 2)$.
4. **Average.** $Q^\star(s, \text{E}) = 0.8 \cdot 1 + 0.1 \cdot 0.795 + 0.1 \cdot 0.486 = 0.928$.
5. **Maximum.** The other actions give $0.801$ (N), $0.554$ (S) and $0.648$ (W), so $V^\star(3, 3) = 0.928$, and the optimal action is E.

The value $0.928$ appears on both sides: $V^\star$ is a fixed point of the backup. That suggests computing it by applying the backup until nothing changes.

## Value iteration

**Value iteration** starts from any $V_0$, for example $V_0 = 0$, and applies the backup to every state at once, a **sweep**:

$$
V_{k+1}(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \big( R(s, a, s') + \gamma V_k(s') \big), \qquad \text{written } V_{k+1} = T V_k .
$$

In the gridworld, after one sweep only $(3, 3)$, next to the goal, has a positive value, $0.8 \cdot 1 + 0.2 \cdot (-0.04) = 0.792$; every other state has $-0.04$. After two sweeps $(2, 3)$ and $(3, 2)$ turn positive, at $0.523$ and $0.431$. Value spreads backwards from the goal by one cell per sweep, and $V_k$ is the optimal value of acting for $k$ steps.

### Why it converges: the backup is a contraction

**Step 1: a maximum moves by at most the largest change.** For any two functions $f, g$ of $a$, $|\max_a f(a) - \max_a g(a)| \le \max_a |f(a) - g(a)|$. If $\max f \ge \max g$ and $a^\star$ maximizes $f$, then $\max f - \max g \le f(a^\star) - g(a^\star) \le \max_a |f(a) - g(a)|$; the other case is symmetric.

**Step 2: one sweep shrinks every difference by $\gamma$.** For two value functions $U, W$ and any state $s$, write $Q_U(s, a)$ for the bracket of the backup computed with $U$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
|(TU)(s) - (TW)(s)| &= \big|\max_a Q_U(s, a) - \max_a Q_W(s, a)\big| && \text{definition of } T \\
&\le \max_a \big| Q_U(s, a) - Q_W(s, a) \big| && \text{Step 1} \\
&= \max_a \Big| \gamma \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \big( U(s') - W(s') \big) \Big| && \text{the rewards cancel} \\
&\le \gamma \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)\, \lVert U - W \rVert_\infty && |U(s') - W(s')| \le \lVert U - W \rVert_\infty \\
&= \gamma\, \lVert U - W \rVert_\infty . && \textstyle\sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the maximum over $s$:

$$
\boxed{\; \lVert TU - TW \rVert_\infty \le \gamma\, \lVert U - W \rVert_\infty \;}
$$

$T$ is a **contraction** with modulus $\gamma$ in the max norm. By the Banach fixed-point theorem it has exactly one fixed point, which is $V^\star$ by the Bellman optimality equation, and iterating it from any start converges to that point geometrically: with $U = V_k$ and $W = V^\star = TV^\star$,

$$
\lVert V_{k} - V^\star \rVert_\infty \le \gamma^k\, \lVert V_0 - V^\star \rVert_\infty .
$$

**Step 3: when to stop.** $V^\star$ is unknown, but the change between sweeps is observable. By the triangle inequality and the contraction, $\lVert V_{k+1} - V^\star \rVert \le \gamma \lVert V_k - V^\star \rVert \le \gamma \big( \lVert V_k - V_{k+1} \rVert + \lVert V_{k+1} - V^\star \rVert \big)$, so

$$
\lVert V_{k+1} - V^\star \rVert_\infty \le \frac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma}\, \lVert V_{k+1} - V_k \rVert_\infty .
$$

Stopping as soon as a sweep changes no value by more than $\varepsilon (1 - \gamma)/\gamma$ guarantees an error below $\varepsilon$. The greedy policy of a value function within $\varepsilon$ of $V^\star$ loses at most $2\gamma\varepsilon/(1 - \gamma)$ against the optimal one in any state ([Singh and Yee, 1994](#references)), and in a finite MDP it is exactly optimal once $\varepsilon$ is small enough.

**Worked example.** In the gridworld with $\gamma = 0.9$, value iteration from $V_0 = 0$ converges to these values and this greedy policy:

## Policy iteration

Value iteration improves the values and reads off a policy at the end. **Policy iteration** ([Howard, 1960](#references)) works with the policy directly and alternates two steps until the policy stops changing:

1. **Evaluation.** Compute $V^\pi$ exactly, by solving the linear system $(I - \gamma P_\pi) V = R_\pi$.
2. **Improvement.** Make the policy greedy with respect to it: $\pi'(s) = \arg\max_a Q^\pi(s, a)$, with $Q^\pi(s, a) = \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)(R(s, a, s') + \gamma V^\pi(s'))$.

**Why the improvement step improves.** The greedy choice gives $Q^\pi(s, \pi'(s)) \ge Q^\pi(s, \pi(s)) = V^\pi(s)$ in every state. Unrolling this inequality one step at a time, with $\mathbb E_{\pi'}$ meaning that every later action follows $\pi'$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V^\pi(s) &\le Q^\pi(s, \pi'(s)) = \mathbb E\big[\, R_{t+1} + \gamma V^\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid S_t = s, A_t = \pi'(s) \,\big] \\
&\le \mathbb E_{\pi'}\big[\, R_{t+1} + \gamma Q^\pi(S_{t+1}, \pi'(S_{t+1})) \mid S_t = s \,\big] && \text{the same inequality at } S_{t+1} \\
&= \mathbb E_{\pi'}\big[\, R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 V^\pi(S_{t+2}) \mid S_t = s \,\big] \\
&\le \dots \le \mathbb E_{\pi'}\big[\, R_{t+1} + \gamma R_{t+2} + \gamma^2 R_{t+3} + \dots \mid S_t = s \,\big] = V^{\pi'}(s) .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the **policy improvement theorem** ([Sutton and Barto, 2018](#references), section 4.2). If the new policy is no better anywhere, then $V^\pi(s) = \max_a Q^\pi(s, a)$ for every $s$, which is the Bellman optimality equation, so $\pi$ is optimal. Otherwise some value strictly increases. There are finitely many deterministic policies, $|\mathcal A|^{|\mathcal S|}$, and none can repeat, so policy iteration stops after finitely many iterations at an optimal policy.

**Worked example.** From "always go N", whose value at $(1, 1)$ is $-0.319$, the first improvement turns the top row and most of the grid towards the goal, worth $0.263$ at $(1, 1)$; the second fixes the start cell to go N instead of E, worth $0.374$; the third finds nothing to change. Three evaluations, against the 20 sweeps value iteration needed for a precision of $10^{-6}$; each evaluation, however, is a linear solve that costs $O(|\mathcal S|^3)$, while a sweep costs $O(|\mathcal S|^2 |\mathcal A|)$. Between the two lie variants that evaluate a policy approximately with a few sweeps before improving it.

## Partially observable MDPs and belief states

The MDP assumes that the agent sees the state. In a **partially observable** MDP (POMDP) it receives an observation $o$ with probability $O(o \mid s', a)$ after reaching $s'$ by action $a$ ([Kaelbling, Littman and Cassandra, 1998](#references)). The current observation is no longer enough to act on, but a probability distribution over states, the **belief** $b(s)$, is. After action $a$ and observation $o$ it is updated by Bayes' rule, first predicting with the transition model, then weighting by the likelihood of what was seen:

$$
b'(s') = \eta\, O(o \mid s', a) \sum_{s} P(s' \mid s, a)\, b(s), \qquad \eta = \text{the constant that makes } b' \text{ sum to } 1 .
$$

**Worked example.** A cleaning robot's sensor reports dirt correctly with probability $0.9$. Its belief that the square is dirty is $0.3$, and the sensor says "dirty": $b' = 0.9 \cdot 0.3 / (0.9 \cdot 0.3 + 0.1 \cdot 0.7) = 0.27/0.34 = 0.794$. The waiting vacuum agent of the [first note](/notes/agents/what-an-agent-is#performance-measures-and-rationality) did the prediction half of this update without observations: its counter $k$ determined its belief $1 - (1 - p)^{k+1}$ that the unseen square was dirty.

The belief is a sufficient statistic for the history, so a POMDP becomes an MDP whose states are beliefs: the **belief MDP**. Its state space is continuous, a simplex of dimension $|\mathcal S| - 1$, and solving it exactly is intractable in general (finite-horizon POMDPs are PSPACE-complete, [Papadimitriou and Tsitsiklis, 1987](#references)). Practical agents approximate the belief: a filter, a learned recurrent state, or, for a language-model agent, the context window and its notes, which summarize what the agent has observed. Partial observability is the rule rather than the exception for the agents later in the course, and [Context and memory](/notes/agents/context-and-memory) treats what to keep in that summary.

## Limitations

- **The model must be known.** Value and policy iteration need $P$ and $R$ for every state and action. When they are unknown, the agent must learn from experience, the subject of [Q-learning and temporal-difference learning](/notes/agents/temporal-difference-learning).
- **Every state is enumerated.** A sweep touches all of $\mathcal S$, and realistic state spaces, positions of a robot, boards of Go, contexts of a language model, are far too large. This is the curse of dimensionality; function approximation, sampling and search from the current state address it.
- **Rewards are given.** The MDP takes $R$ as part of the problem. Choosing it is the performance-measure problem of the first note, and a badly chosen reward is optimized as faithfully as a good one.
- **The discount is a modelling choice.** Values and policies change with $\gamma$; with $\gamma$ close to 1, both the error bound and the effective horizon $1/(1 - \gamma)$ grow.
- **Partial observability is hard.** Exact POMDP solutions are intractable beyond small problems; practical agents approximate the belief.

## What you should remember

- An MDP is $(\mathcal S, \mathcal A, P, R, \gamma)$ with the Markov property; its solution is a policy, judged by the expected discounted return.
- The Bellman expectation equation, $V^\pi = R_\pi + \gamma P_\pi V^\pi$, follows from the recursive return, total expectation and the Markov property; evaluating a known policy is a linear solve.
- The Bellman optimality equation, $V^\star(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a)(R + \gamma V^\star(s'))$, has the greedy policy as its solution.
- The backup $T$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in the max norm, so value iteration converges from any start, with error at most $\gamma^k$ times the initial one; stop when a sweep changes values by less than $\varepsilon(1 - \gamma)/\gamma$.
- Policy iteration alternates exact evaluation and greedy improvement; the policy improvement theorem makes every step an improvement, and it stops at an optimal policy after finitely many steps.
- When the state is hidden, act on a belief updated by Bayes' rule; the belief MDP is exact but intractable, and agents approximate it.

## Code

The 4 × 3 gridworld as arrays $P[a, s, s']$ and $R[a, s]$, the fixed plan as a baseline, value iteration with the stopping rule, a numerical check of the contraction, policy iteration, and a belief update. NumPy only; seeds are fixed, and exact digits can differ slightly across platforms. The script runs in about a second.

```python
import numpy as np

# 1. the 4 x 3 gridworld: cells (column, row) from (1, 1) at the bottom left; a wall at (2, 2)
cells = [(x, y) for y in (1, 2, 3) for x in (1, 2, 3, 4) if (x, y) != (2, 2)]
ix = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(cells)}
GOAL, PIT = (4, 3), (4, 2)
ACTS = {'N': (0, 1), 'E': (1, 0), 'S': (0, -1), 'W': (-1, 0)}
SIDE = {'N': 'EW', 'S': 'EW', 'E': 'NS', 'W': 'NS'}
def move(c, a):
    n = (c[0] + ACTS[a][0], c[1] + ACTS[a][1])
    return n if n in ix else c                                  # walls and edges: stay put
nS, nA = len(cells), len(ACTS)
P = np.zeros((nA, nS, nS)); R = np.zeros((nA, nS))              # P[a, s, s'] and the expected reward R[a, s]
for a_i, a in enumerate(ACTS):
    for c in cells:
        s = ix[c]
        if c in (GOAL, PIT): P[a_i, s, s] = 1.0; continue      # terminal: absorbing, reward 0
        for b, prob in ((a, 0.8), (SIDE[a][0], 0.1), (SIDE[a][1], 0.1)):
            n = move(c, b)
            P[a_i, s, ix[n]] += prob
            R[a_i, s] += prob * (1.0 if n == GOAL else -1.0 if n == PIT else -0.04)
gamma = 0.9

# 2. the baseline: the plan N, N, E, E, E executed blindly from (1, 1)
d, ret = np.eye(nS)[ix[(1, 1)]], 0.0                            # distribution over cells, discounted reward
for t, a in enumerate('NNEEE'):
    a_i = list(ACTS).index(a)
    ret += gamma ** t * d @ R[a_i]; d = d @ P[a_i]
print(round(d[ix[GOAL]], 5), round(d[ix[PIT]], 3), round(ret, 3))   # 0.32776 0.014 0.051

# 3. value iteration with the stopping rule
def bellman(V, gamma):                                          # the optimality operator T
    return (R + gamma * P @ V).max(axis=0)
def value_iteration(gamma, eps=1e-10):
    V, history = np.zeros(nS), [np.zeros(nS)]
    while True:
        V_new = bellman(V, gamma); history.append(V_new)
        if np.abs(V_new - V).max() < eps * (1 - gamma) / gamma: return V_new, history
        V = V_new
V, hist = value_iteration(gamma)
print(np.round(hist[1], 3)[ix[(3, 3)]], np.round(hist[2], 3)[[ix[(2, 3)], ix[(3, 2)]]])   # 0.792 [0.523 0.431]
Q = R + gamma * P @ V
policy = [list(ACTS)[a] for a in Q.argmax(axis=0)]
for y in (3, 2, 1):
    print('  '.join('  wall ' if (x, y) not in ix else f'{V[ix[(x, y)]]:6.3f}' + (' ' if (x, y) in (GOAL, PIT) else policy[ix[(x, y)]])
                    for x in (1, 2, 3, 4)))
#  0.610E   0.766E   0.928E   0.000
#  0.487N    wall    0.585N   0.000
#  0.374N   0.327E   0.428N   0.189W
print({a: round(float(Q[i, ix[(3, 3)]]), 3) for i, a in enumerate(ACTS)})   # {'N': 0.801, 'E': 0.928, 'S': 0.554, 'W': 0.648}
for g in (0.5, 0.9, 0.99):
    Vg, h = value_iteration(g)
    err = [np.abs(v - Vg).max() for v in h]
    bound = int(np.ceil(np.log(1e-6 / err[0]) / np.log(g)))
    print(g, 'sweeps to 1e-6:', next(k for k, e in enumerate(err) if e < 1e-6), 'by the bound:', bound)
# 0.5 sweeps to 1e-6: 14 by the bound: 20
# 0.9 sweeps to 1e-6: 20 by the bound: 131
# 0.99 sweeps to 1e-6: 26 by the bound: 1371

# 4. the contraction, checked on random pairs of value functions
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
ratios = [np.abs(bellman(U, gamma) - bellman(W, gamma)).max() / np.abs(U - W).max()
          for U, W in (rng.normal(size=(2, nS)) for _ in range(1000))]
print(round(max(ratios), 4))                                    # 0.9: never above gamma

# 5. policy iteration: exact evaluation by a linear solve, then greedy improvement
pi = np.zeros(nS, dtype=int)                                    # start: always go north
for it in range(1, 20):
    P_pi, R_pi = P[pi, np.arange(nS)], R[pi, np.arange(nS)]
    V_pi = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(nS) - gamma * P_pi, R_pi)    # (I - gamma P_pi) V = R_pi
    new = (R + gamma * P @ V_pi).argmax(axis=0)
    print(it, ''.join(list(ACTS)[a] for a in pi), round(V_pi[ix[(1, 1)]], 3))
    if (new == pi).all(): break
    pi = new
print(np.allclose(V_pi, V, atol=1e-8))
# 1 NNNNNNNNNNN -0.319
# 2 EENWNNNEEEN 0.263
# 3 NENWNNNEEEN 0.374
# True

# 6. a belief update: a noisy dirt sensor that is right 90% of the time
prior = np.array([0.3, 0.7])                                    # P(dirty), P(clean)
likelihood = np.array([0.9, 0.1])                               # P(sensor says "dirty" | dirty), (| clean)
posterior = likelihood * prior / (likelihood * prior).sum()
print(posterior.round(3))                                       # [0.794 0.206]
```

## Next

The MDP is solved here because its transitions and rewards are written down. Two settings break that. In a game, part of the "environment" is an opponent who chooses the next state to hurt us, and the state space is so large that only the states reachable from the current position can be examined. The [next note](/notes/agents/games-and-tree-search) handles both: minimax and alpha–beta search for adversaries, and Monte Carlo tree search, which estimates values by sampling instead of sweeping, the method behind AlphaGo, AlphaZero and MuZero.
