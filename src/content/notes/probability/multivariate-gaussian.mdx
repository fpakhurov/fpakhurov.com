A joint distribution of $d$ variables is in general a function of $d$ arguments that no table can hold. One family is different. It is described completely by a mean vector and a covariance matrix, it stays in the family under linear maps, marginalization and conditioning, and all of these are a few lines of linear algebra. That family, the **multivariate Gaussian**, is the noise model of linear regression, the prior of Gaussian processes, the state of a Kalman filter and the latent space of most generative models. This note builds it from the one-dimensional normal and the covariance of the [previous note](/notes/probability/joint-distributions).

**Notation.** $X \in \mathbb R^d$ is a random vector with mean $\mu = E[X]$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$, $\Sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$, symmetric and positive definite. $X \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)$; $Z \sim N(0, I)$ is the standard normal vector. $\lvert \Sigma \rvert$ is the determinant, $A^\top$ the transpose.

## The density

$X \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)$ has the density

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} \lvert \Sigma \rvert^{1/2}} \exp\Big(-\frac12 (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)\Big) .
$$

For $d = 1$ it is the normal density of the [continuous distributions note](/notes/probability/continuous-distributions), with $\Sigma = \sigma^2$. The quantity in the exponent, $\Delta^2(x) = (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)$, is the squared **Mahalanobis distance**: the distance from the mean measured in standard deviations along each direction. The density is constant on the sets where $\Delta$ is constant, which are ellipsoids.

If $\Sigma$ is diagonal, the quadratic form is a sum $\sum_i (x_i - \mu_i)^2 / \sigma_i^2$, the exponential of a sum is a product, and the density factorizes into one normal density per coordinate. The standard normal vector $Z \sim N(0, I)$ is simply $d$ independent $N(0, 1)$ coordinates.

## Covariance as geometry

A symmetric positive definite matrix has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors $q_1, \dots, q_d$ with positive eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d$: $\Sigma = Q \Lambda Q^\top$. In the coordinates of that basis the covariance is diagonal, so the density factorizes there, with variance $\lambda_i$ along $q_i$. The contours are ellipses with axes along the eigenvectors and half-lengths $k\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ at Mahalanobis distance $k$. For

$$
\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda = 2.8,\ 0.2, \qquad \rho = \frac{1.2}{\sqrt{2 \cdot 1}} = 0.85 ,
$$

the long axis points along $(0.83, 0.55)$:

The long axis carries $2.8 / 3 = 93\%$ of the total variance $\operatorname{tr} \Sigma = 3$, and projecting onto it is principal component analysis. The ellipses also correct a common intuition. In one dimension, one standard deviation around the mean holds 68% of the probability; in two dimensions the ellipse of Mahalanobis distance 1 holds only $1 - e^{-1/2} = 39.3\%$, and distance 2 holds $86.5\%$, because $\Delta^2$ is a sum of $d$ squared standard normals and has the chi-squared distribution with $d$ degrees of freedom.

## Affine maps

Linear maps keep Gaussians Gaussian. If $X \sim N(\mu, \Sigma)$, then for any matrix $B$ and vector $c$

$$
BX + c \sim N(B\mu + c,\ B \Sigma B^\top) .
$$

The mean follows from linearity of expectation and the covariance from bilinearity of covariance; that the result is again Gaussian follows from the change-of-variables formula of the [functions of random variables note](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables) in $d$ dimensions. Three consequences are used constantly:

- **Sampling.** $X = \mu + LZ$ with $LL^\top = \Sigma$ (a Cholesky factor) has distribution $N(\mu, \Sigma)$; the [Gaussian distributions note](/notes/generative-models/gaussian-distributions#sampling-from-standard-noise-to-any-gaussian) of the generative models course works through it in PyTorch.
- **Whitening.** Conversely, $L^{-1}(X - \mu) \sim N(0, I)$: any Gaussian is a standard one in suitable coordinates.
- **Linear combinations.** $a^\top X \sim N(a^\top \mu, a^\top \Sigma a)$. For the example above, $X_1 + X_2 \sim N(0, 2 + 1 + 2 \cdot 1.2) = N(0, 5.4)$.

## Marginals and conditionals

Split $X$ into two blocks $X_1$, $X_2$, with the mean and covariance split accordingly:

$$
\mu = \begin{pmatrix} \mu_1 \\ \mu_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \\ \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22} \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The **marginal** of $X_2$ is read off: $X_2 \sim N(\mu_2, \Sigma_{22})$, a special case of an affine map that selects coordinates. The **conditional** of $X_2$ given $X_1 = x_1$ is Gaussian as well, with

$$
E[X_2 \mid X_1 = x_1] = \mu_2 + \Sigma_{21} \Sigma_{11}^{-1} (x_1 - \mu_1), \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(X_2 \mid X_1 = x_1) = \Sigma_{22} - \Sigma_{21} \Sigma_{11}^{-1} \Sigma_{12} .
$$

For the example, given $X_1 = x_1$ the second coordinate is $N(0.6\, x_1,\ 1 - 1.2^2/2) = N(0.6\, x_1, 0.28)$:

<GaussianConditioning />

Two features of the formulas matter far beyond this example. The conditional mean is a **linear** function of the observed value, so for jointly Gaussian data linear regression is not an approximation but the exact best predictor, the subject of the [conditional expectation note](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation). And the conditional covariance, the **Schur complement** $\Sigma_{22} - \Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\Sigma_{12}$, does not depend on the observed value: observing $X_1$ shrinks the uncertainty about $X_2$ by the same amount wherever $X_1$ lands. Gaussian process regression and the Kalman filter are these two formulas applied to large covariance matrices.

## Uncorrelated means independent, for Gaussian vectors

For a jointly Gaussian vector, zero covariance between two blocks makes $\Sigma$ block-diagonal, and the density factorizes: **uncorrelated implies independent**. This is a property of the joint distribution, not of the marginals. Let $X \sim N(0, 1)$ and let $Y = SX$, where $S = \pm 1$ is an independent fair sign. Then $Y \sim N(0, 1)$ as well, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[S]\, E[X^2] = 0$, but $\lvert Y \rvert = \lvert X \rvert$: the two are as dependent as can be. The pair is not jointly Gaussian: every linear combination of a Gaussian vector is normal, and $X + Y$ equals 0 with probability $1/2$, which no normal variable does.

## High dimensions: the shell

The density of $N(0, I)$ is largest at the origin, and yet samples in high dimension are never near it. The squared length $\lVert Z \rVert^2 = Z_1^2 + \dots + Z_d^2$ has mean $d$ and variance $2d$, so the length concentrates around $\sqrt d$ with a spread that stays near $0.7$:

<GaussianShell />

In 100 dimensions the length is $10 \pm 0.7$, and a length below 5 has probability about $10^{-15}$. The density is high near the centre, but the centre has almost no volume; the volume, and with it the probability, is in a thin shell. Two practical consequences: interpolating linearly between two latent samples of a generative model passes through a region the model never saw during training, which is why spherical interpolation is often used instead; and in high dimensions, distances between independent Gaussian points are all about $\sqrt{2d}$, so nearest neighbours become less informative.

## What changed

| | A general joint distribution | The multivariate Gaussian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parameters | a function of $d$ arguments | $\mu$ and $\Sigma$: $d + d(d+1)/2$ numbers |
| Contours | anything | ellipsoids, axes from the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ |
| Linear maps, marginals | new distributions to compute | $N(B\mu + c, B\Sigma B^\top)$; read off blocks |
| Conditionals | renormalized slices | Gaussian, linear mean, Schur complement covariance |
| Uncorrelated | not enough for independence | equivalent to independence |

## Limitations

- **Thin tails and one mode.** Real data in high dimension are rarely Gaussian: images, text embeddings and returns have heavy tails, clusters and curved structure; mixtures, flows and diffusion models exist for that reason.
- **The covariance matrix is expensive.** It has $d(d+1)/2$ entries, 500,500 for $d = 1{,}000$; estimating it needs many more samples than dimensions, and inverting it costs $O(d^3)$. Diagonal, low-rank or sparse-precision structures are used in practice.
- **Marginal normality proves nothing about the joint.** Each coordinate can be normal while the vector is not, as the sign example shows; formulas for conditionals and independence apply only when the vector is jointly Gaussian.

## What you should remember

- $N(\mu, \Sigma)$ has density $\propto \exp(-\frac12 (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu))$; contours are ellipsoids of constant Mahalanobis distance.
- The eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ give the axes, the square roots of its eigenvalues the half-lengths; in 2D one Mahalanobis unit holds 39%, not 68%.
- $BX + c \sim N(B\mu + c, B\Sigma B^\top)$; $X = \mu + LZ$ with $LL^\top = \Sigma$ samples any Gaussian.
- Marginals are blocks of $\mu$ and $\Sigma$; conditionals are Gaussian with a mean linear in the observation and covariance $\Sigma_{22} - \Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\Sigma_{12}$.
- For jointly Gaussian vectors, uncorrelated means independent.
- A standard normal vector in $d$ dimensions has length about $\sqrt d \pm 0.7$.

## Code

The example of the figures checked by sampling, with NumPy:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

S = np.array([[2.0, 1.2], [1.2, 1.0]])
w, V = np.linalg.eigh(S)
print(w.round(3), np.abs(V[:, 1]).round(3))                         # [0.2 2.8] [0.832 0.555]: the long axis
print(round(1.2 / np.sqrt(2), 2), [round(float(stats.chi2(2).cdf(k * k)), 3) for k in (1, 2)])   # 0.85 [0.393, 0.865]

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
L = np.linalg.cholesky(S)                                            # sampling: X = L Z
X = rng.normal(size=(1_000_000, 2)) @ L.T
print(np.cov(X.T).round(2).tolist())                                 # [[2.0, 1.2], [1.2, 1.0]]
d2 = np.einsum('ij,jk,ik->i', X, np.linalg.inv(S), X)                # squared Mahalanobis distances
print(round(float((d2 <= 1).mean()), 3), round(float((d2 <= 4).mean()), 3))   # 0.393 0.865
print(round(float(X.sum(axis=1).var()), 2))                          # 5.39, against 2 + 1 + 2 * 1.2 = 5.4

# conditioning on X1 = 2: the samples with X1 close to 2
near = X[np.abs(X[:, 0] - 2) < 0.02, 1]
print(round(1.2 / 2 * 2, 2), round(1 - 1.2**2 / 2, 2), round(float(near.mean()), 2), round(float(near.var()), 2))   # 1.2 0.28 1.2 0.28

# normal marginals, zero correlation, and still dependent: Y = S X with a random sign S
x = rng.normal(size=1_000_000)
y = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=x.size) * x
print(round(float(np.corrcoef(x, y)[0, 1]), 3), round(float((x + y == 0).mean()), 3))   # -0.001 0.501

# the shell: the length of a standard normal vector in 100 dimensions
r = np.linalg.norm(rng.normal(size=(100_000, 100)), axis=1)
print(round(float(r.mean()), 2), round(float(r.std()), 2), f'{stats.chi(100).cdf(5):.1e}')   # 9.97 0.71 1.1e-15
```
