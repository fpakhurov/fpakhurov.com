The posterior of a real model, a distribution over molecular configurations or an energy-based model of images has a density that can be evaluated only up to a constant, $p(x) = \tilde p(x) / Z$ with $Z$ an intractable integral, in many dimensions. The direct methods of the [Monte Carlo note](/notes/probability/monte-carlo) need a proposal close to $p$, and there is none. **Markov chain Monte Carlo** turns the [ergodic theorem](/notes/probability/markov-chains#stationary-distributions) around: build a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is $p$, run it, and use its states as samples. The construction needs only ratios $\tilde p(y)/\tilde p(x)$, in which $Z$ cancels. This last note of the course derives the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm, shows how it fails, and ends with Langevin dynamics, the sampler of diffusion models.

**Notation.** $p(x) = \tilde p(x)/Z$ is the target; $q(y \mid x)$ a proposal distribution; $X_0, X_1, \dots$ the chain. $\nabla \log p(x)$ is the **score**, which does not depend on $Z$.

## Detailed balance

A chain with transition probabilities $P(x \to y)$ satisfies **detailed balance** with respect to $p$ if

$$
p(x)\, P(x \to y) = p(y)\, P(y \to x) \quad \text{for all } x, y .
$$

In equilibrium, the flow of probability from $x$ to $y$ equals the flow back. Summing over $x$ gives $\sum_x p(x) P(x \to y) = p(y)$: $p$ is stationary. Detailed balance is a local condition that is easy to arrange, and if the chain is also irreducible and aperiodic, the [Markov chains note](/notes/probability/markov-chains) guarantees convergence to $p$ from any start.

## Metropolis–Hastings \{#metropolis-hastings\}

From the current state $x$, propose $y \sim q(\cdot \mid x)$ and accept it with probability

$$
\alpha(x, y) = \min\Big(1, \frac{\tilde p(y)\, q(x \mid y)}{\tilde p(x)\, q(y \mid x)}\Big) ;
$$

otherwise stay at $x$. For $y \ne x$ the transition probability is $q(y \mid x)\,\alpha(x, y)$, and $p(x) q(y \mid x) \alpha(x, y) = \min\big(p(x) q(y \mid x),\ p(y) q(x \mid y)\big)$ is symmetric in $x$ and $y$: detailed balance holds. The constant $Z$ appears in both numerator and denominator and cancels. With a symmetric proposal, such as $y = x + \sigma \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim N(0, 1)$, the ratio is simply $\tilde p(y)/\tilde p(x)$: moves uphill are always accepted, moves downhill sometimes. This is **random-walk Metropolis**, the algorithm of Metropolis and colleagues from 1953.

The step size $\sigma$ decides whether it works:

Over 20,000 steps on a standard normal target, a step of $0.1$ accepts 97% of proposals and is worth only 57 independent samples; a step of $25$ accepts 5% and is worth 708; a step of $2.4$ accepts 44% and is worth 4,815. For random-walk Metropolis in many dimensions, theory suggests tuning the step to an acceptance rate of about 0.23; in one dimension, about 0.44.

## Mixing and diagnostics

Successive states of a chain are correlated, so $n$ states carry less information than $n$ independent samples. The **effective sample size** $n / (1 + 2\sum_{k \ge 1} \rho_k)$, with $\rho_k$ the autocorrelation at lag $k$, measures how many independent samples they are worth; the error of an average over the chain is $\sigma / \sqrt{\text{ESS}}$, not $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Practical use adds three habits: discard an initial **burn-in** before the chain has reached its stationary regime, run several chains from dispersed starting points, and compare the variance between chains with the variance within them (the $\hat R$ statistic).

No diagnostic can detect what a chain has never visited. For a target with two separated modes, a chain with a small step stays in the mode it starts in:

<BimodalMixing />

In 20,000 steps the small-step chain spends 0% of its time in the right mode, the large-step chain 50%. The first chain's samples look perfectly healthy; only a second chain started in the other mode, or knowledge of the target, reveals the problem. Multimodality is the main practical failure of MCMC, and tempering methods, which run chains on flattened versions of the target, exist to cross the barriers.

## Gibbs sampling

When the conditional distribution of each coordinate given the others is easy to sample, **Gibbs sampling** updates one coordinate at a time from its conditional. It is Metropolis–Hastings with the conditional as the proposal, and the acceptance probability is always 1. For the bivariate normal of the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#marginals-and-conditionals) with unit variances and correlation $\rho$, the conditionals are $X_1 \mid X_2 = x_2 \sim N(\rho x_2, 1 - \rho^2)$ and symmetrically.

<GibbsZigzag />

Strong correlation makes Gibbs slow: each move is along an axis, and the conditional variance $1 - \rho^2$ is small. Successive draws of $X_1$ have correlation $\rho^2$, which is $0.81$ for $\rho = 0.9$ and $0.98$ for $\rho = 0.99$; 20,000 sweeps are then worth about 2,300 and 190 independent samples. Reparametrizing to remove the correlation, or updating correlated coordinates in blocks, fixes it. Gibbs sampling is the workhorse for models with conjugate conditionals, such as topic models and many hierarchical Bayesian models.

## Langevin dynamics

Random-walk proposals ignore where the probability is. **Langevin dynamics** uses the score to push each step uphill and adds noise to keep exploring:

$$
x_{t+1} = x_t + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \nabla \log p(x_t) + \sqrt{\varepsilon}\, z_t, \qquad z_t \sim N(0, I) .
$$

As $\varepsilon \to 0$ its stationary distribution is $p$; for a finite step it is slightly off. On a standard normal target, whose score is $-x$, the stationary variance is $1/(1 - \varepsilon/4)$: $1.026$ for $\varepsilon = 0.1$, $1.143$ for $\varepsilon = 0.5$. Adding a Metropolis–Hastings acceptance step removes the bias (MALA); simulating Hamiltonian dynamics with a momentum variable gives **Hamiltonian Monte Carlo**, the default sampler of modern probabilistic programming systems such as Stan. With minibatch gradients, the same update is **stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics**, which turns noisy gradient descent into approximate posterior sampling for neural networks.

The score needs neither $Z$ nor the density itself, only its gradient, and this is the bridge to generative models. Score-based models learn $\nabla \log p$ of images from data and generate by running Langevin dynamics at decreasing noise levels, as the [score matching note](/notes/generative-models/score-matching#sampling-with-langevin-dynamics) of the generative models course shows; diffusion models are the same idea seen as reversing a noising process.

## What changed

| | Direct Monte Carlo | Markov chain Monte Carlo |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Needs | samples from $p$ or a close proposal | $\tilde p$ up to a constant (or its score) |
| Samples | independent | correlated; count the effective sample size |
| High dimensions | proposals degrade exponentially | local moves still work, at the cost of mixing time |
| Typical algorithms | inversion, rejection, importance sampling | Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs, Langevin, HMC |
| Main failure | infinite-variance weights | slow mixing, chains stuck in one mode |

## Limitations

- **Convergence is asymptotic and hard to verify.** A chain can look stationary while it has explored only part of the target; diagnostics detect some failures, never all.
- **Tuning matters.** Step sizes, proposal shapes, parametrizations and the number of chains change efficiency by orders of magnitude.
- **It is expensive for big data and big models.** Every step evaluates the target, often on the whole dataset; for neural networks variational inference, ensembles or stochastic-gradient MCMC are cheaper approximations.

## What you should remember

- Detailed balance $p(x) P(x \to y) = p(y) P(y \to x)$ makes $p$ stationary.
- Metropolis–Hastings: propose $y \sim q(\cdot \mid x)$, accept with $\min\big(1, \tilde p(y) q(x \mid y) / (\tilde p(x) q(y \mid x))\big)$; only ratios of $\tilde p$ are needed.
- Too small a step crawls, too large a step is rejected; aim for acceptance of roughly 0.23 in many dimensions.
- Correlated samples are worth an effective sample size $n/(1 + 2\sum \rho_k)$; run several chains; beware of modes a chain never visits.
- Gibbs sampling updates coordinates from their conditionals; strong correlation makes it slow.
- Langevin dynamics follows the score plus noise; it underlies HMC, stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics and score-based diffusion models.

## Code

Random-walk Metropolis with three step sizes, a two-mode target, Gibbs sampling and unadjusted Langevin dynamics:

```python
import numpy as np, math

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def metropolis(logp, x0, step, n):
    """Random-walk Metropolis: propose x + step * N(0, 1), accept with probability min(1, p(y) / p(x))."""
    x, lp, chain, accepted = x0, logp(x0), np.empty(n), 0
    for t in range(n):
        y = x + step * rng.normal()
        ly = logp(y)
        if math.log(rng.random()) < ly - lp:
            x, lp, accepted = y, ly, accepted + 1
        chain[t] = x
    return chain, accepted / n

def ess(chain):
    """Effective sample size from the autocorrelations, summed until they drop below 0.05."""
    c = chain - chain.mean()
    f = np.fft.rfft(c, 2 * len(c))
    ac = np.fft.irfft(f * np.conj(f))[: len(c)]
    ac /= ac[0]
    cut = np.argmax(ac < 0.05)
    return len(c) / (1 + 2 * ac[1:cut].sum())

# a standard normal target with three step sizes, 20,000 steps each: acceptance rate and effective sample size
for step in (0.1, 2.4, 25):
    chain, rate = metropolis(lambda x: -x * x / 2, 0.0, step, 20_000)
    print(step, round(rate, 3), round(ess(chain)))
# 0.1 0.971 57 | 2.4 0.443 4815 | 25 0.053 708

# two modes at -3 and 3: the share of time in the right mode with a small and a large step
mix = lambda x: math.log(0.5 * math.exp(-(x + 3) ** 2 / 0.72) + 0.5 * math.exp(-(x - 3) ** 2 / 0.72))
for step in (0.5, 4.0):
    chain, rate = metropolis(mix, -3.0, step, 20_000)
    print(step, round(float((chain > 0).mean()), 3))
# 0.5 0.0 | 4.0 0.502

# Gibbs for a bivariate normal: successive draws of x1 have correlation rho^2
for rho in (0.5, 0.9, 0.99):
    x1 = x2 = 0.0
    s, xs = math.sqrt(1 - rho ** 2), np.empty(20_000)
    for t in range(20_000):
        x1 = rho * x2 + s * rng.normal()
        x2 = rho * x1 + s * rng.normal()
        xs[t] = x1
    print(rho, round(float(np.corrcoef(xs[:-1], xs[1:])[0, 1]), 3), round(ess(xs)))
# 0.5 0.264 12075 | 0.9 0.807 2292 | 0.99 0.978 188

# unadjusted Langevin on N(0, 1), whose score is -x: the step size biases the stationary variance to 1 / (1 - eps / 4)
for eps in (0.1, 0.5):
    x, xs = 0.0, np.empty(200_000)
    for t in range(200_000):
        x = x + eps / 2 * (-x) + math.sqrt(eps) * rng.normal()
        xs[t] = x
    print(eps, round(float(xs[1000:].var()), 3), round(1 / (1 - eps / 4), 3))
# 0.1 1.026 1.026 | 0.5 1.13 1.143
```
