A temperature in Celsius becomes one in Fahrenheit, a measurement is standardized, a log-price is exponentiated back to a price, a uniform random number is turned into an exponential waiting time. Each time a random variable $X$ with a known distribution is passed through a function $g$, and the question is the distribution of $Y = g(X)$. For counts the answer is a sum; for densities it is the **change-of-variables formula**, which says that probability is conserved while density is squeezed or stretched. The same formula, read backwards, is how computers sample from any distribution and how normalizing flows compute likelihoods.

**Notation.** $Y = g(X)$. For an invertible $g$, $x = g^{-1}(y)$ is the preimage of $y$, and $g'$ the derivative. $f_X, F_X$ and $f_Y, F_Y$ are the densities and CDFs of $X$ and $Y$.

## The discrete case

If $X$ takes countably many values, so does $Y$, and $Y = y$ happens exactly when $X$ lands on one of the values that $g$ sends to $y$:

$$
P(Y = y) = \sum_{x \,:\, g(x) = y} P(X = x) .
$$

For a fair die $X$ and $Y = (X - 3)^2$, the value $Y = 1$ comes from $X = 2$ and $X = 4$, so $P(Y = 1) = 2/6$. Nothing more is needed: probabilities of values are carried along and added up where values merge.

## The distribution function method

For a continuous $X$ the safe general method goes through the CDF, because $\{Y \le y\}$ is an event about $X$:

$$
F_Y(y) = P(g(X) \le y) ,
$$

and the density of $Y$ is the derivative of the result.

**A square.** Let $X \sim U(-1, 1)$ and $Y = X^2$. For $0 \le y \le 1$, $Y \le y$ means $-\sqrt y \le X \le \sqrt y$, an interval of length $2\sqrt y$ out of 2:

$$
F_Y(y) = \sqrt y, \qquad f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{2 \sqrt y} .
$$

Half the time $X^2$ is below $1/4$, although $1/4$ is only a quarter of its range; the density of $Y$ is infinite at 0, where the parabola is flat and many values of $X$ crowd into a few values of $Y$.

**A linear map.** For $Y = aX + b$ with $a > 0$, $F_Y(y) = P(X \le (y - b)/a) = F_X((y - b)/a)$, so

$$
f_Y(y) = \frac{1}{\lvert a \rvert} f_X\Big(\frac{y - b}{a}\Big) ,
$$

and the same holds for $a < 0$. Applied to $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $a = 1/\sigma$, $b = -\mu/\sigma$, it gives the density of $N(0, 1)$: the standardization $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$ promised in the [previous note](/notes/probability/continuous-distributions). The other direction also holds: $aX + b$ of a normal $X$ is normal, so a temperature of $N(20, 3^2)$ °C is one of $N(68, 5.4^2)$ °F.

## The change-of-variables formula

For a strictly increasing differentiable $g$, $F_Y(y) = F_X(g^{-1}(y))$, and differentiating gives the density of $Y$ directly; for a decreasing $g$ the derivative changes sign, which the absolute value absorbs:

$$
f_Y(y) = \frac{f_X(x)}{\lvert g'(x) \rvert}, \qquad x = g^{-1}(y) .
$$

The formula says that probability is conserved band by band. A short interval of length $dx$ around $x$ carries probability $f_X(x)\, dx$, and $g$ maps it to an interval of length $dy = \lvert g'(x) \rvert\, dx$ around $y$ that must carry the same probability, $f_Y(y)\, dy = f_X(x)\, dx$. Where $g$ squeezes ($\lvert g' \rvert < 1$), the density rises; where it stretches, the density falls:

The example is the **log-normal** distribution: $Y = e^X$ with $X \sim N(0, 1)$ has $g'(x) = e^x = y$ and

$$
f_Y(y) = \frac{\varphi(\ln y)}{y}, \qquad y > 0 .
$$

Monotone maps carry quantiles along, so the median of $Y$ is $e^0 = 1$ and $P(Y > e) = P(X > 1) = 0.159$. Quantities that are products of many positive factors, such as incomes, file sizes or response times, are often close to log-normal: their logarithm is a sum, close to normal for the reasons of the [central limit theorem](/notes/probability/central-limit-theorem).

If $g$ is not monotone, every preimage contributes: $f_Y(y) = \sum_{x : g(x) = y} f_X(x) / \lvert g'(x) \rvert$. For $Y = X^2$ above, the two preimages $\pm\sqrt y$ each contribute $\frac{1/2}{2\sqrt y}$, which gives the same $1/(2\sqrt y)$.

## Inverse transform sampling

Read in reverse, the CDF method turns uniform random numbers into any distribution. If $U \sim U(0, 1)$ and $F$ is a continuous, strictly increasing CDF, then $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$, because

$$
P(F^{-1}(U) \le x) = P(U \le F(x)) = F(x) .
$$

For any CDF, with flat stretches or jumps, the same holds with the quantile function $Q(u) = \inf\{x : F(x) \ge u\}$ of the [previous note](/notes/probability/continuous-distributions#the-distribution-function-and-quantiles) in place of $F^{-1}$: $Q(u) \le x$ exactly when $u \le F(x)$, because $F$ is non-decreasing and continuous from the right. The discrete sampler below is this $Q$.

The same idea samples a discrete distribution. For a categorical distribution with probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_K$, cut $[0, 1]$ into consecutive pieces of lengths $p_1, \dots, p_K$ and return the piece in which $U$ lands; that is one lookup in the cumulative sums. A language model [samples its next token](/notes/nlp/gpt#decoding-turning-probabilities-into-text) exactly this way, after temperature or top-$p$ have reshaped the probabilities.

The converse is the **probability integral transform**: if $X$ has a continuous CDF $F$, then $F(X) \sim U(0, 1)$. It is how probabilistic forecasts are checked, since the values $F(x)$ of a well-calibrated forecaster at the observed outcomes must look uniform, and it is why the p-value of a test whose statistic has a continuous distribution under the null hypothesis is uniformly distributed when that hypothesis is true. For a discrete statistic the p-value is only **super-uniform**, $P(p \le \alpha) \le \alpha$: in the two-sided test of 100 coin flips of the [hypothesis tests note](/notes/probability/hypothesis-tests#the-logic-of-a-test), a fair coin gives $p \le 0.05$ with probability $0.035$. The test stays valid, but it is conservative.

## Normalizing flows in one dimension

A normalizing flow models a complicated density as a simple one pushed through a chain of invertible maps, $Y = g_K(\cdots g_1(Z))$ with $Z \sim N(0, 1)$. Applying the change-of-variables formula once per map gives the exact log-density of a data point:

$$
\log f_Y(y) = \log \varphi(z) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log \lvert g_k'(z_{k-1}) \rvert, \qquad z_0 = z = g^{-1}(y), \; z_k = g_k(z_{k-1}) .
$$

Training maximizes this log-likelihood over the parameters of the maps. In more than one dimension the derivative becomes the determinant of the Jacobian (the [multiple integrals note](/notes/math/multiple-integrals#densities-under-a-transform) derives that formula), and making that determinant cheap is the design problem of the [normalizing flows note](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows#change-of-variables) in the generative models course.

## What changed

| | Before | With the change-of-variables formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution of $g(X)$ | simulate and look at a histogram | exact: $f_X(x) / \lvert g'(x) \rvert$, or $F_Y$ through $F_X$ |
| Standardizing a normal | assumed | proved: $(X - \mu)/\sigma \sim N(0, 1)$ |
| Sampling a distribution | needs a special trick per distribution | $F^{-1}(U)$ for any CDF; cumulative sums for a categorical |
| Density of a flow | — | exact log-likelihood, one $\log \lvert g' \rvert$ per map |

## Limitations

- **$F^{-1}$ is not always available in closed form.** The normal CDF has no elementary inverse; samplers use other transformations (Box–Muller, which needs two variables) or numerical inversion.
- **The formula needs a differentiable, locally invertible $g$.** A function with flat pieces, such as rounding or clipping, puts positive probability on single values, and $Y$ is then not continuous: rounding makes it discrete, clipping makes it a mixture of masses and a density.
- **Functions of several variables need their joint distribution.** The distribution of the sum or the ratio of two random variables depends on how they depend on each other, the subject of the [joint distributions note](/notes/probability/joint-distributions).

## What you should remember

- Discrete: $P(g(X) = y)$ sums $P(X = x)$ over the preimages of $y$.
- Continuous, general: compute $F_Y(y) = P(g(X) \le y)$, then differentiate.
- Continuous, monotone $g$: $f_Y(y) = f_X(x) / \lvert g'(x) \rvert$ at $x = g^{-1}(y)$; probability is conserved, density is squeezed or stretched.
- Linear maps keep normals normal; $(X - \mu)/\sigma \sim N(0, 1)$; $e^X$ of a normal is log-normal.
- $F^{-1}(U)$, in general $Q(U)$, has CDF $F$ (inverse transform sampling); $F(X)$ is uniform when $F$ is continuous (probability integral transform).

## Code

The two bands of the figure, the square of a uniform, and inverse transform sampling, checked numerically:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Z = stats.norm()

# Y = e^X for X ~ N(0, 1): each band keeps its probability over an interval of a different length
for a, b in [(-1.2, -0.7), (0.8, 1.3)]:
    p = Z.cdf(b) - Z.cdf(a)
    print(round(p, 3), round(np.exp(b) - np.exp(a), 3), round(p / (np.exp(b) - np.exp(a)), 3))
# 0.127 0.195 0.649   (squeezed: high density)
# 0.115 1.444 0.08    (stretched: low density)
print(round(Z.sf(1), 4), stats.lognorm(1).median())              # 0.1587 1.0

# Y = X^2 for X ~ U(-1, 1): P(Y <= 1/4) = sqrt(1/4)
y = rng.uniform(-1, 1, 1_000_000) ** 2
print(round((y <= 0.25).mean(), 3))                              # 0.5

# inverse transform: -ln(1 - U) is Exp(1)
u = np.array([0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9])
print((-np.log(1 - u)).round(3))                                 # [0.105 0.357 0.693 1.204 2.303]
x = -np.log(1 - rng.random(1_000_000))
print(round(x.mean(), 3), round((x > 2).mean(), 4))              # 1.0 0.1354; exactly 1 and e^-2 = 0.1353

# a categorical distribution sampled through cumulative sums, as for a next token
p = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])
k = np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(p), rng.random(1_000_000), side='right')
print((np.bincount(k) / 1_000_000).round(3))                     # [0.499 0.301 0.199]

# the probability integral transform: F(X) is uniform
print(np.quantile(Z.cdf(rng.normal(size=1_000_000)), [0.1, 0.5, 0.9]).round(3))   # [0.1   0.499 0.9  ]
```
