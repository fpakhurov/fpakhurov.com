## Three axioms \{#three-axioms\}

A **probability space** consists of $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$. The set $\Omega$ contains outcomes. The family $\mathcal F$ contains the subsets admitted as events: it includes $\Omega$ and is closed under complements and countable unions. Such a family is called a **σ-algebra**. Probability $P$ is defined on that family and satisfies

$$
\begin{gathered}
P(A)\ge0\quad(A\in\mathcal F),\qquad P(\Omega)=1,\\
P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(A_i)
\quad\text{when }A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing\text{ for }i\ne j.
\end{gathered}
$$

The last requirement is **countable additivity**. It includes finite disjoint unions by using empty events after the last nonempty one. Disjointness matters: probabilities of overlapping events cannot simply be added.

On a finite or countable space with every subset admitted as an event, one may choose nonnegative singleton weights that sum to one and define

$$
P(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}p_\omega,\qquad \sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p_\omega=1.
$$

This constructs a probability measure. Conversely, under this discrete event convention, countable additivity determines each event from its singleton probabilities. A coarser event family may not contain singleton sets, so that description must not be silently assumed for every σ-algebra.

Our finite case records three bits, A, B and C, where 1 means the corresponding flag is present. These eight disjoint records have the following stipulated integer weights, divided by 36:

| Record ABC | 000 | 100 | 010 | 001 | 110 | 101 | 011 | 111 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weight | 13 | 5 | 7 | 3 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 1 |

For example, the event $A$ contains 100, 110, 101 and 111, hence $P(A)=(5+4+2+1)/36=1/3$. No independence between the flags is assumed. The table itself defines their joint behaviour.

The axioms provide a mathematical convention used throughout the course. They do not establish that a proposed model fits observations, or that every account of subjective belief must use countable additivity. Interpretation and empirical adequacy remain separate questions from consistency of this calculus.

## The rules that follow \{#the-rules-that-follow\}

Decompose events into disjoint pieces. Since $A$ and $A^c$ partition $\Omega$, $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$, and $P(\varnothing)=0$. If $A\subseteq B$, then $B=A\cup(B\setminus A)$ is disjoint, so $P(B)=P(A)+P(B\setminus A)\ge P(A)$. This gives monotonicity and $P(A)\le1$.

For two arbitrary events, split $B$ into its overlap with $A$ and its part outside $A$. The result is

$$
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).
$$

In our table, $P(B)=13/36$ and $P(A\cap B)=5/36$. Therefore $P(A\cup B)=20/36=5/9$. Directly summing the six records with A or B present gives the same answer. This is an algebra check on the model, not an assumption about independence.

Countable additivity also controls limits. If $E_n\subseteq E_{n+1}$, partition their union into $E_1,E_2\setminus E_1,E_3\setminus E_2,\ldots$. Summing these disjoint increments gives

$$
P\!\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_n\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}P(E_n).
$$

For a decreasing sequence, taking complements similarly gives the probability of the intersection as the limit. These are continuity properties of a probability measure, not claims that every arbitrary sequence of events has a probability limit.

## Inclusion–exclusion \{#inclusion-exclusion\}

Adding three event probabilities counts an outcome once for every flag it has. Subtracting pairwise intersections removes one copy for each pair of present flags. A record with all three flags then has coefficient zero, so one copy must be restored:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(A\cup B\cup C)={}&P(A)+P(B)+P(C)\\
&-P(A\cap B)-P(A\cap C)-P(B\cap C)+P(A\cap B\cap C).
\end{aligned}
$$

The single-event numerators are 12, 13 and 7; the pairwise numerators are 5, 3 and 2; the triple numerator is 1. Hence

$$
P(A\cup B\cup C)=\frac{12+13+7-5-3-2+1}{36}=\frac{23}{36}.
$$

This agrees with the complement of the unflagged record, whose weight is 13. The figure shows which correction affects which kind of record.

For $n$ events, inclusion–exclusion alternates over all nonempty intersections. An outcome belonging to exactly $m\ge1$ events receives the coefficient

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{m}(-1)^{j+1}\binom mj=1,
$$

which follows by subtracting the $j=0$ term from $(1-1)^m=0$. An outcome in none receives zero. This pointwise counting identity proves the formula after weighting outcomes, and it also holds for general probability measures by finite additivity.

## The union bound in practice \{#the-union-bound-in-practice\}

If intersections are unknown, we can retain a bound. Replace each event after the first by the part not already covered; these disjoint parts are no larger than the original events. Countable additivity and monotonicity imply

$$
P\!\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)\le\sum_iP(A_i).
$$

In the flag case the bound is $32/36=8/9$, compared with the exact $23/36$. Subtracting the three pair terms gives $22/36=11/18$, a lower bound here; restoring the triple term produces the exact answer. Unknown overlap is the information lost in the simpler upper bound.

For a separately specified design calculation, suppose three failure events each have verified probability caps $1/100$, $1/80$ and $1/200$. Then the probability of any failure is at most $11/400=0.0275$, whatever their dependence. These are assumed caps, not estimates supplied by the axioms. An observed frequency without an uncertainty argument is not automatically such a cap. The same logic gives Bonferroni control of the probability of any false rejection when the individual tests have valid error bounds; no independence assumption is required. [Concentration inequalities](/notes/probability/inequalities-and-concentration) develop bounds used to justify such inputs.

## Infinitely many outcomes \{#infinitely-many-outcomes\}

Consider a positive-integer outcome $N$ with assigned probabilities

$$
p_n=\frac1{n(n+1)}=\frac1n-\frac1{n+1},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$

The partial sum through $m$ telescopes to $m/(m+1)$, tending to one. Thus these nonnegative weights define a probability on all subsets of the positive integers. In particular, $P(N>m)=1/(m+1)$. The event $N\le m$ increases to the whole space, and its probabilities tend to one as required. The program checks finite partial sums; the identity and its limit supply the infinite argument.

A continuous model behaves differently. Uniform probability on $[0,1]$ assigns an interval its length. A singleton can be enclosed by intervals of arbitrarily small length, so its probability is zero. Yet an outcome still belongs to the interval: zero probability is not the same as an empty event. A countable set of points has probability zero by countable additivity. The interval is uncountable, so one cannot apply that axiom to add its point probabilities.

The existence of a measure agreeing with interval lengths on Borel sets is a measure-construction theorem. It is not established merely by saying “give each point probability zero.”

## Why not every set is an event \{#why-not-every-set-is-an-event\}

A σ-algebra specifies which questions have probabilities. On the real line, the **Borel σ-algebra** is generated by open intervals. It contains countable unions, intersections and complements built from them, including the sets described by the continuous inequalities used here. This describes a particular family generated by intervals; it is not a declaration that every subset of the real line is Borel.

For example, a coarse observation that only distinguishes the presence or absence of flag A has event family $\{\varnothing,A,A^c,\Omega\}$. This is already a valid σ-algebra, even though it does not distinguish all eight records. Thus the event family can describe available information, not just a technical restriction for infinite sets.

### A set with no probability \{#a-set-with-no-probability\}

Optional mathematical boundary: with the axiom of choice, select one point from each class of numbers in $[0,1)$ whose differences are rational. Shifts of the selected set by distinct rational $r\in[0,1)$, taken modulo one, are pairwise disjoint and cover the interval: each point has a representative, and an overlap would place two representatives in one class. If all these sets were measurable and all rotations preserved their probability, each shifted copy would have the same mass $v$. Countable additivity would require $1=v+v+\cdots$, impossible for either $v=0$ or $v>0$. This is the Vitali obstruction, not a computable finite fixture. The contradiction uses rotation invariance as well as the probability axioms; it does not forbid every probability measure on all subsets of a continuum. Weaver's Definition A.1 and Theorem A.2 give the corresponding mathematical argument.

## What changed \{#what-changed\}

| From the finite counting model | To the probability-space model |
| --- | --- |
| All detailed outcomes equally likely | A specified measure assigns event weights. |
| Count a complement or overlap | The same algebra follows from disjoint additivity. |
| A finite list of records | Countable spaces and continuous models require consistent limits. |
| Every subset admitted | The event family must be stated or understood. |

## Limitations \{#limitations\}

The axioms constrain a model; they do not identify a real mechanism, validate estimated error rates or supply independence. Countable additivity is the convention used here, not a claim that all philosophical accounts of probability agree. The optional nonmeasurable-set argument is an existence result using choice and invariance, not something tested by enumeration. No numerical check of finitely many terms proves an infinite identity without the accompanying argument.

## What you should remember \{#what-you-should-remember\}

State the outcome space, event family and measure. Add probabilities directly only for disjoint events; account for overlap otherwise. Countable additivity makes increasing and decreasing event limits usable. A union bound trades missing intersection information for an upper bound. Different generators can assign different probabilities to identical output records while both obeying every axiom.

## Code \{#code\}

This exact standard-library calculation checks the weighted flag union, the two-stage branch weights and five finite partial sums. No data or packages are downloaded.

```python
from fractions import Fraction

weights = {'000': 13, '100': 5, '010': 7, '001': 3,
           '110': 4, '101': 2, '011': 1, '111': 1}
union = Fraction(sum(w for s, w in weights.items() if '1' in s), 36)
print('any flag:', union)
modes = {'A': ('u', 'v'), 'B': ('v',), 'C': ('u', 'v', 'w')}
branch = {a+b: Fraction(1, 3*len(formats))
          for a, formats in modes.items() for b in formats}
assert sum(branch.values()) == 1
print('uniform pair, mode then format:', Fraction(1, 6), branch['Cw'])
for m in (1, 2, 5, 10, 25):
    partial = sum(Fraction(1, n*(n+1)) for n in range(1, m+1))
    assert partial == Fraction(m, m+1)
    print(m, partial, 1-partial)
```

```text
uniform pair, mode then format: 1/6 1/9
1 1/2 1/2
2 2/3 1/3
5 5/6 1/6
10 10/11 1/11
25 25/26 1/26
```
