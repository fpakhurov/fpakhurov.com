The [opening note](/notes/probability/what-probability-is) showed the share of heads in coin flips drifting toward one half and called a probability a long-run frequency. The **law of large numbers** turns that observation into a theorem: the average of independent copies of a random variable converges to its expectation. It is what makes a probability measurable, a validation score meaningful and a simulation a method of computation. This note states the law in its two forms, explains the two kinds of convergence they use, and turns the law into Monte Carlo estimation.

**Notation.** $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ (and, where needed, variance $\sigma^2$); $\bar X_n = (X_1 + \dots + X_n)/n$. An event happens **almost surely** if it has probability 1.

## The weak law

For every $\varepsilon > 0$,

$$
P\big(\lvert \bar X_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon\big) \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty .
$$

The average **converges in probability** to $\mu$: for large $n$, a deviation of any fixed size is unlikely. With a finite variance the proof is one line of the [previous note](/notes/probability/inequalities-and-concentration): Chebyshev's inequality bounds the probability by $\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)$. Khinchin showed that a finite mean is enough, with no assumption on the variance.

## The strong law

The weak law speaks about one large $n$ at a time. It leaves open whether a particular infinite sequence of flips could keep making excursions away from the mean forever, rarer and rarer but never stopping. The strong law rules this out:

$$
P\big(\bar X_n \to \mu\big) = 1 .
$$

The average **converges almost surely**: with probability 1 the sequence of averages, as a sequence of numbers, has the limit $\mu$. Kolmogorov proved it under the same assumption as Khinchin, a finite mean. Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, not the other way round.

The difference is easiest to see on indicators. Let $A_1, A_2, \dots$ be independent events and $X_n = \mathbb 1_{A_n}$. If $P(A_n) \to 0$, then $X_n \to 0$ in probability. Whether $X_n \to 0$ almost surely, that is, whether the events eventually stop happening, is decided by the **Borel–Cantelli lemmas**: if $\sum_n P(A_n) < \infty$, only finitely many $A_n$ happen, almost surely; if the events are independent and $\sum_n P(A_n) = \infty$, infinitely many happen, almost surely.

<TwoConvergences />

With $P(A_n) = 1/n$ the expected number of events up to $10^5$ is $1 + \frac12 + \dots + \frac{1}{10^5} \approx 12.1$, and they never stop: the chance of at least one between $10^4$ and $10^5$ is $1 - \frac{10^4}{10^5} = 0.9$. With $P(A_n) = 1/n^2$ the expected total is $\pi^2/6 \approx 1.64$, almost all of it in the first steps. The proof of the strong law applies the first lemma to the events $\{\lvert \bar X_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon\}$, whose probabilities fall fast enough when the tails are controlled.

For practice the distinction rarely matters: both laws say that one long simulation, or one large validation set, gives an average close to the expectation. It matters for algorithms that run a single trajectory forever, such as stochastic gradient descent or a Markov chain, where the question is whether that one trajectory converges.

## Monte Carlo estimation

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Any expectation is a limit of averages. To compute $E[g(X)]$, draw independent samples $X_1, \dots, X_n$ and average $g(X_i)$; the law of large numbers guarantees convergence, and the error falls like $\sigma/\sqrt n$ whatever the dimension of $X$.</aside>

Integrals become expectations by choosing a distribution. The area of the quarter disk $x^2 + y^2 \le 1$ in the unit square is $P(U_1^2 + U_2^2 \le 1) = \pi/4$ for independent uniform $U_1, U_2$, so $\pi$ is four times the share of random points that land inside:

<MonteCarloPi />

With a million points the estimate is $3.1413$, with standard error $0.0016$: Monte Carlo is slow, three correct digits for a million samples, and each extra digit costs a hundred times more. Its strength is that the rate $1/\sqrt n$ does not depend on the dimension. A grid with 10 points per axis needs $10^{10}$ points in 10 dimensions; a Monte Carlo estimate of the volume of the 10-dimensional unit ball, $\pi^5/120 = 2.55$, from a million random points of the cube $[-1, 1]^{10}$ is $2.53$. The relative error, 2%, is large because only one point in 400 lands inside the ball: estimating the probability of a rare event by plain sampling is expensive, which is the problem [importance sampling](/notes/probability/monte-carlo) solves.

The same principle runs through machine learning. A training loss is a Monte Carlo estimate of the risk, a minibatch gradient a Monte Carlo estimate of the full gradient, a validation accuracy a Monte Carlo estimate of the probability of a correct answer, and the reparametrized loss of a [variational autoencoder](/notes/generative-models/vae) a Monte Carlo estimate of an expectation over latent codes.

## When averages do not converge

The law needs a finite mean, and its speed depends on the tails.

- **No mean.** The Cauchy distribution of the [expectation note](/notes/probability/expectation-and-variance) has none, and the average of $n$ Cauchy samples is again standard Cauchy, exactly as spread out as a single sample. Its averages over $10^2$, $10^4$ and $10^6$ samples in the code below are $-0.01$, $-0.33$ and $2.86$: more data does not help.
- **A mean but no variance.** A Pareto distribution with tail exponent $1.5$ has mean 3 and infinite variance. Its averages do converge, but slowly and in jumps, whenever a single huge value arrives: $3.22$, $3.74$, $3.04$ for the same sample sizes. Incomes, file sizes and the number of followers of an account behave like this, and so do averages computed from them.
- **Dependence.** Averages of dependent values, such as the successive states of a Markov chain, still converge under conditions studied in the [Markov chains note](/notes/probability/markov-chains), but the effective number of samples is smaller than $n$.

## What changed

| | Before | With the law of large numbers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Probability as frequency | an observation | a theorem: $\bar X_n \to \mu$ almost surely |
| Convergence | intuitive | in probability (weak law) and almost surely (strong law), separated by Borel–Cantelli |
| Expectations and integrals | computed analytically | estimated by averaging samples, error $\sigma/\sqrt n$ in any dimension |
| Failure modes | | no mean (Cauchy), infinite variance (slow), dependence (fewer effective samples) |

## Limitations

- **The law says nothing about the rate.** How close $\bar X_n$ is to $\mu$ for a given $n$ needs the inequalities of the previous note or the central limit theorem of the next.
- **Identically distributed is an assumption.** If the distribution drifts, as user behaviour does between training and deployment, the average converges to the average of a changing target, or to nothing.
- **Monte Carlo is slow for rare events and high precision.** Relative error grows as the target probability shrinks; variance reduction, importance sampling and quasi-random sequences exist for that reason.

## What you should remember

- Weak law: $P(\lvert \bar X_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon) \to 0$ (convergence in probability); strong law: $P(\bar X_n \to \mu) = 1$ (almost sure convergence). Both need only a finite mean.
- Borel–Cantelli: $\sum P(A_n) < \infty$ means finitely many $A_n$ happen; for independent events, $\sum P(A_n) = \infty$ means infinitely many.
- Monte Carlo: $E[g(X)] \approx \frac1n \sum g(X_i)$ with error $\sigma/\sqrt n$, independent of the dimension.
- Without a mean (Cauchy) averages do not settle; with infinite variance they settle slowly and in jumps.

## Code

The two kinds of convergence, two Monte Carlo integrals, and averages that misbehave:

```python
import numpy as np, math

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# independent events with P(A_n) = 1/n and 1/n^2, n up to 100,000: how many happen, 1,000 runs each
N = 100_000
n = np.arange(1, N + 1)
c1 = [int((rng.random(N) < 1 / n).sum()) for _ in range(1000)]
c2 = [int((rng.random(N) < 1 / n**2).sum()) for _ in range(1000)]
print(round(float(np.mean(c1)), 2), round(float(np.sum(1 / n)), 2), round(float(np.mean(c2)), 2), round(math.pi**2 / 6, 2))   # 12.05 12.09 1.68 1.64
print(1 - 10_000 / 100_000, round(1 - float(np.prod(1 - 1 / n[100:] ** 2)), 4))   # 0.9 0.0099: an event after 10^4 (1/n); after 100 (1/n^2)

# Monte Carlo: pi from the unit square, and the volume of the 10-dimensional unit ball
u = rng.random((2, 1_000_000))
print(round(4 * float(((u**2).sum(axis=0) <= 1).mean()), 4), round(4 * math.sqrt(math.pi / 4 * (1 - math.pi / 4) / 1e6), 4))   # 3.1413 0.0016
x = rng.uniform(-1, 1, (1_000_000, 10))
share = float(((x**2).sum(axis=1) <= 1).mean())
print(round(share * 2**10, 3), round(math.pi**5 / 120, 3), round(math.sqrt(share * (1 - share) / 1e6) / share, 3))   # 2.527 2.55 0.02

# no mean: Cauchy averages wander; a finite mean with infinite variance (Pareto, mean 3) converges slowly
c = rng.standard_cauchy(10**6)
p = rng.pareto(1.5, 10**6) + 1
print([round(float(c[:k].mean()), 2) for k in (10**2, 10**4, 10**6)], [round(float(p[:k].mean()), 2) for k in (10**2, 10**4, 10**6)])
# [-0.01, -0.33, 2.86] [3.22, 3.74, 3.04]
```

## Next

The law of large numbers says where the average goes; the $1/\sqrt n$ rule says how far it typically is. Neither says what shape its fluctuations have. The [next note](/notes/probability/central-limit-theorem) shows that the shape is almost always the same, the normal distribution, whatever the distribution of the individual terms, and turns that fact into error bars.
