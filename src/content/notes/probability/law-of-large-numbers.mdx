The [concentration bounds](/notes/probability/inequalities-and-concentration) control an average at a specified sample size. Now let that size grow. Does a small failure probability at each size also mean that one continuing experiment eventually stays close to its target? These are different statements, and the distinction matters when interpreting a long run of measurements.

We will use one deliberately constructed distribution throughout this note and the next. Independent observations take the following values:

| $x$ | $-1$ | $2$ | $4$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $P(X=x)$ | $1/2$ | $1/3$ | $1/6$ |

Its moments are $\mu=5/6$ and $\sigma^2=137/36$. Write $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$ and $\bar X_n=S_n/n$. The observations are not normal; nothing here assumes they are.

## The weak law

Convergence in probability means that, for every fixed $\varepsilon>0$,

$$
P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\geq\varepsilon)\longrightarrow0.
$$

The threshold stays fixed while the sample size grows. It does not demand that the observed error decrease at every step.

For independent observations with common mean $\mu$ and common finite variance $\sigma^2$, covariance terms vanish. Thus $E\bar X_n=\mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n$. Chebyshev gives the entire finite-variance proof:

$$
P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\geq\varepsilon)\leq\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$

Identical distributions are sufficient, but the variance argument also works for uncorrelated observations with a common mean and uniformly bounded variances. For iid integrable observations the weak law still holds without finite variance; the argument above no longer proves it. The strong law below supplies that conclusion instead.

For our finite law, set $\varepsilon=1/2$. Exact probabilities come from the coefficients of $(3+2t^3+t^5)^n/6^n$: exponent $k$ represents $S_n=k-n$. We sum coefficients whose averages cross the threshold; no sampled trajectory is used.

| $n$ | Exact failure probability | Chebyshev bound | Hoeffding bound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $6$ | $0.626736$ | $2.537037$ | $1.773841$ |
| $24$ | $0.229574$ | $0.634259$ | $1.237567$ |
| $96$ | $0.012747$ | $0.158565$ | $0.293214$ |
| $384$ | $0.000000525$ | $0.039641$ | $0.000924$ |

Hoeffding uses the range width $5$, giving $2\exp(-2n\varepsilon^2/25)$. A bound above $1$ gives no information beyond the elementary bound $P(A)\leq1$. Neither bound equals the actual probability. At moderate sizes Chebyshev can even be smaller than Hoeffding; the exponential rate wins eventually.

## The strong law

Almost-sure convergence concerns an entire infinite sequence:

$$
P\!\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}\bar X_n=\mu\right)=1.
$$

Outside a probability-zero set of sequences, for every positive tolerance there is a finite, sequence-dependent index after which the average stays within it. The theorem provides no common deterministic index that works for every sequence, and the averages need not move monotonically.

For iid observations, $E|X_1|<\infty$ is sufficient for this conclusion. This is the integrable strong law; Durrett's Theorem 2.4.1 proves the more general pairwise-independent version through truncation and subsequences. We will prove the bounded iid case directly, using the exponential bound already available.

### The Borel–Cantelli lemmas \{#the-borel-cantelli-lemmas\}

Let $A_n$ be events. The event $A_n\text{ infinitely often}$ means that infinitely many of them occur.

* If $\sum_nP(A_n)<\infty$, then $P(A_n\text{ infinitely often})=0$. Independence is unnecessary: the probability of any event after index $N$ is at most the tail sum, which tends to zero.
* If the events are independent and $\sum_nP(A_n)=\infty$, then $P(A_n\text{ infinitely often})=1$. Indeed, the probability of no event from $N$ through $M$ is at most $\exp(-\sum_{n=N}^MP(A_n))$, which tends to zero for each $N$.

Apply the first statement to $A_n=\{|\bar X_n-\mu|\geq\varepsilon\}$. For iid observations in a fixed bounded interval the Hoeffding probabilities form a summable geometric bound at every fixed positive tolerance. Therefore only finitely many deviations occur. Apply this to all $\varepsilon=1/k$, $k=1,2,\ldots$: the countable intersection of these probability-one events still has probability one, and every positive tolerance contains one of these smaller tolerances. That proves almost-sure convergence in the bounded iid case. The events $A_n$ overlap in their observations; they do not need to be independent for this argument.

The summability condition also explains why convergence in probability alone is weaker. Stipulate independent events with $P(A_n)=1/(n+2)$ and let $Y_n=\mathbf1_{A_n}$. Then $Y_n\to0$ in probability, but the harmonic sum diverges, so the value $1$ appears infinitely often almost surely. The events are not deviations of our sample mean; this is a separate construction distinguishing two definitions.

A finite window makes the difference visible. Independence and a telescoping product give

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\!\left(\bigcup_{n=N}^{4N}A_n\right)&=1-\prod_{n=N}^{4N}\frac{n+1}{n+2}\\
&=1-\frac{N+1}{4N+2}\\
&\longrightarrow\frac34.
\end{aligned}
$$

A single late time is unlikely to contain an event, while a growing window can still contain one with substantial probability. These window values illustrate the construction; the Borel–Cantelli argument establishes the infinite-sequence conclusion.

## Monte Carlo estimation

Suppose an integral is represented as an expectation. Choose $U_i$ iid uniform on $[0,1]$ and $g(u)=1+u^2$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
I=Eg(U)&=\int_0^1(1+u^2)\,du\\
&=\frac43,\\
E[g(U)^2]&=\int_0^1(1+2u^2+u^4)\,du\\
&=\frac{28}{15},\\
\operatorname{Var}(g(U))&=\frac4{45}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The estimator $\widehat I_n=n^{-1}\sum_i g(U_i)$ is unbiased. Its mean squared error is $4/(45n)$, so its root mean squared error is $2/\sqrt{45n}$. This is an average over repeated runs, not the realized error of every run. Halving this RMS error requires four times as many independent evaluations.

The sampling design can improve the constant. For each independent $U_j$, evaluate both $g(U_j)$ and $g(1-U_j)$ and average them:

$$
\begin{aligned}
h(u)&=\frac{g(u)+g(1-u)}2\\
&=\frac32-u+u^2,\\
Eh(U)&=\frac43,\\
\operatorname{Var}(h(U))&=\frac1{180}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The two values inside a pair are dependent; different pairs are independent. With the same total budget $2m$ of integrand evaluations, the plain estimator has variance $2/(45m)$ and the paired estimator has variance $1/(180m)$. The variance reduction factor is $8$, not $16$: a pair costs two evaluations. RMS improves by $\sqrt8$.

This gain follows from this particular integrand. Pairing alone does not guarantee improvement for arbitrary functions. Unbiasedness and convergence survive here because the paired average has the correct expectation and finite variance.

## When averages do not converge

First check dependence. Let $Z$ be a nondegenerate integrable random variable. If every observation equals $Z$, then $\bar X_n=Z$ for all $n$. More observations have added no independent information. A nondegenerate $Z$ does not converge in probability to its expectation merely because it has been repeated.

Next check integrability. For iid standard Cauchy variables, the characteristic function is $\varphi_X(t)=e^{-|t|}$. The average has characteristic function $[\varphi_X(t/n)]^n=e^{-|t|}$, hence exactly the same Cauchy distribution at every size. Its mean is undefined and its spread does not shrink. Symmetry gives a center, not a finite expectation. Other dependence structures can satisfy laws of large numbers, but they require additional assumptions beyond this iid theorem.

## What changed

A finite-sample inequality became a limit statement. Chebyshev proved convergence in probability for finite variance. A summable exponential bound plus Borel–Cantelli proved eventual pathwise control for bounded observations. The Monte Carlo example then used the variance formula to compare two sampling designs at equal cost.

## Limitations

The law does not specify a practical stopping time, a finite-sample confidence level or a universal convergence speed. A long stable-looking trace cannot verify independence or finite expectation. The general integrable theorem permits infinite variance; the RMS calculations in the Monte Carlo example do not extend to that case.

## What you should remember

* A weak law controls the probability of a fixed-size error at a late time.
* A strong law controls the eventual behavior of almost every entire sequence.
* Summable event probabilities need no independence for the first Borel–Cantelli lemma; its divergent-sum counterpart needs independence.
* Monte Carlo precision depends on variance and effective independent samples, so compare sampling designs at the same computational budget.
