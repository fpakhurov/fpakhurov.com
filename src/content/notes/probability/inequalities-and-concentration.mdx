An average error can be small while a large error remains possible. To control that possibility, we need a statement about a tail probability. When the full distribution is unknown, an inequality trades a limited set of assumptions for a bound that holds across many distributions.

After [conditional expectation](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation), we know how to calculate average prediction losses. Here the question is how far a random observation or sample average can move from its expectation.

## Markov's inequality \{#markovs-inequality\}

Let $W$ be nonnegative with finite expectation, and choose a threshold $t>0$. Every outcome where $W\ge t$ contributes at least $t$ to $W$. Using the indicator of that event,

$$
t\,\mathbf1_{\{W\ge t\}}\le W.
$$

Taking expectations and dividing by the positive threshold gives **Markov's inequality**:

$$
\Pr(W\ge t)\le\frac{\mathbb E[W]}{t}.
$$

The mean limits how much probability can be placed far above zero. The proof needs no independence or density; nonnegativity is essential. The same statement and indicator proof appear in [Siegrist, Additional Properties, Markov's Inequality](https://www.randomservices.org/random/expect/Properties2.html).

For a running example, let $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ be independent Bernoulli variables with success probability $p=2/5$, and set

$$
\bar X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i.
$$

The average is nonnegative and has expectation $2/5$. Markov therefore gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(\bar X_n\ge1/2)&\le\frac{2/5}{1/2},\\
&=\frac45.
\end{aligned}
$$

This direct bound does not improve with the sample size: it has used only the unchanged mean. It concerns a one-sided event. The later table will compare bounds for a different, explicitly two-sided event.

## Chebyshev's inequality \{#chebyshevs-inequality\}

If a real variable $W$ has mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, its squared centered value is nonnegative. Apply Markov to that value:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(|W-\mu|\ge t)
&=\Pr((W-\mu)^2\ge t^2),\\
&\le\frac{\mathbb E[(W-\mu)^2]}{t^2},\\
&=\frac{\sigma^2}{t^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is **Chebyshev's inequality**, valid for $t>0$. It bounds a two-sided deviation without a distributional shape assumption. See [Siegrist, Variance, Chebyshev's Inequality](https://www.randomservices.org/random/expect/Variance.html).

For our independent Bernoulli observations, the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances. Dividing by the square of the sample size gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac{p(1-p)}n.
$$

Choose $n=150$ and a deviation $\varepsilon=1/10$. The variance of the average is $0.0016$, so

$$
\Pr(|\bar X_{150}-0.4|\ge0.1)\le0.16.
$$

The bound now improves with the sample size because it uses the variance of an average. Pairwise uncorrelated observations would suffice for this variance calculation; full independence will matter for the exponential bound below.

The model also permits an exact check. Write $S=150\bar X_{150}$. The event includes counts $S\le45$ or $S\ge75$, with the boundary counts included. Summing the binomial masses gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{150}
&=\sum_{k=0}^{45}\binom{150}{k}(0.4)^k(0.6)^{150-k}\\
&\quad+\sum_{k=75}^{150}\binom{150}{k}(0.4)^k(0.6)^{150-k},\\
&\approx0.015376202.
\end{aligned}
$$

Chebyshev is conservative for this particular law. That does not make it incorrect: the inequality must also cover distributions with much heavier tails than a bounded Bernoulli average.

## Hoeffding's inequality \{#hoeffdings-inequality\}

Suppose independent observations satisfy $a_i\le X_i\le b_i$ almost surely. Write $\mu_n=\mathbb E[\bar X_n]$ and choose $\varepsilon>0$. Assume the sum of squared interval widths is positive. A one-sided bound is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(\bar X_n-\mu_n\ge\varepsilon)
&\le\exp\left[-\frac{2n^2\varepsilon^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(b_i-a_i)^2}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The same bound applies to the lower tail. Their union therefore has at most twice this probability. This is [Hoeffding's Theorem 2 (1963), equation (2.6)](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830). If every interval has width zero, the observations are constants and the positive-deviation probability is zero instead.

For observations in $[0,1]$, the two-sided statement simplifies to

$$
\Pr(|\bar X_n-\mu_n|\ge\varepsilon)
\le2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}.
$$

At our chosen $n=150$ and $\varepsilon=0.1$, the upper bound is $0.099574137$. It is smaller than the variance-only bound here, while still exceeding the exact probability. Hoeffding uses boundedness and independence, but does not require identical distributions or Bernoulli observations. A bound above $1$ is valid but uninformative; plotting it clipped at $1$ does not turn it into an estimated probability.

## Where the exponent comes from \{#where-the-exponent-comes-from\}

Exponentiation turns an upper-tail event into an event about a positive variable. For $\lambda>0$, Markov gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(\bar X_n-\mu_n\ge\varepsilon)
&\le e^{-\lambda n\varepsilon}\,
\mathbb E\!\left[e^{\lambda\sum_i(X_i-\mathbb E[X_i])}\right],\\
&=e^{-\lambda n\varepsilon}\prod_{i=1}^n
\mathbb E[e^{\lambda(X_i-\mathbb E[X_i])}].
\end{aligned}
$$

The equality uses independence. Choosing $\lambda$ to minimize the resulting bound is the **Chernoff method**; it is an optimization of an upper bound, not a normal approximation.

For a variable supported on an interval of width $c$, the centered exponential moment satisfies

$$
\log\mathbb E[e^{\lambda(X-\mathbb E[X])}]
\le\frac{\lambda^2c^2}{8}.
$$

Here is why the quadratic exponent is possible. Let $\psi(\lambda)$ be the logarithm on the left. Reweight the original probabilities proportionally to $e^{\lambda X}$ and normalize. Differentiating the logarithm of the exponential moment shows that $\psi''(\lambda)$ is the variance under this tilted distribution. Tilting changes weights, but not the interval containing $X$. For any law in an interval of width $c$, its variance is at most $c^2/4$: the expected squared distance from the interval midpoint is at most that value, and centering at its own mean only reduces it. Thus $\psi''(\lambda)\le c^2/4$. Since $\psi(0)=\psi'(0)=0$, integrating this bound twice proves the displayed inequality. Bounded support justifies differentiating the exponential moments. Hoeffding's [proof of Theorem 2, equations (4.11)–(4.16)](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830) establishes the same exponential-moment bound.

For width-one intervals, substitution gives

$$
\Pr(\bar X_n-\mu_n\ge\varepsilon)
\le\exp\left(-\lambda n\varepsilon+\frac{n\lambda^2}{8}\right).
$$

The exponent is minimized at $\lambda=4\varepsilon$. Substituting gives the one-sided bound; applying the same argument to the negatives of the observations and adding the two tail bounds gives the factor $2$.

For Bernoulli observations we know the exponential moment exactly:

$$
\mathbb E[e^{\lambda X}]=1-p+pe^\lambda.
$$

At an upper threshold $q>p$, minimizing the log bound gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(\bar X_n\ge q)
&\le\inf_{\lambda>0}
\exp\bigl(n\log(1-p+pe^\lambda)-\lambda nq\bigr),\\
e^{\lambda_*}&=\frac{q(1-p)}{p(1-q)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $0<p<q<1$. Substituting the optimizer yields

$$
\Pr(\bar X_n\ge q)\le e^{-nD(q\Vert p)},
$$

where the binary divergence is

$$
D(q\Vert p)=q\log\frac qp+(1-q)\log\frac{1-q}{1-p}.
$$

For a lower threshold $0<q<p$, the same calculation uses a negative $\lambda$ and bounds $\Pr(\bar X_n\le q)$. Combining $q=0.3$ and $q=0.5$ for our stipulated law gives $0.085969291$. This agrees with the optimized bounded-variable expression in [Hoeffding's Theorem 1, equation (2.1)](https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1963.10500830); here it was derived from the Bernoulli moment directly.

## A first generalization bound \{#a-first-generalization-bound\}

A single validation result is another sample average. Let $h$ be a predictor fixed independently of a validation sample of size $n$, and let its loss lie in $[0,1]$. The validation observations are independent and identically distributed from the target distribution. Denote population risk by $L(h)$ and empirical validation risk by $\widehat L_n(h)$. Hoeffding gives

$$
\Pr(|\widehat L_n(h)-L(h)|\ge\varepsilon)
\le2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}.
$$

Now suppose we compare a fixed finite family $\mathcal H$ of $M$ predictors using that same validation sample. The models' validation errors can be dependent; the **union bound** still gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr\!\left(\bigcup_{h\in\mathcal H}
\{|\widehat L_n(h)-L(h)|\ge\varepsilon\}\right)
&\le\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2},\\
&=2M e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Set the right side to a desired failure probability $0<\delta<1$ and solve:

$$
\varepsilon_{n,M,\delta}
=\sqrt{\frac{\log(2M/\delta)}{2n}}.
$$

With probability at least $1-\delta$, all the empirical risks are within this tolerance of their population risks simultaneously. Therefore the same event covers a model selected after comparing these $M$ validation results. Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David derive this finite-class guarantee in [Understanding Machine Learning, Section 4.2, Corollary 4.6](https://www.cs.huji.ac.il/~shais/UnderstandingMachineLearning/understanding-machine-learning-theory-algorithms.pdf).

Choose $n=1200$, $M=12$ and $\delta=0.05$. The tolerance is $0.050718940$. If a selected model had $108$ errors on those $1200$ validation observations, its empirical error would be $0.09$. On the simultaneous guarantee event its population error would then be at most $0.140718940$. This count is a stipulated illustration, not a measured performance result.

The family must be specified independently of this validation sample. Training on a separate sample is compatible with the argument: condition on that training data, then apply the bound to the independent validation data. Constructing an unrestricted new family in response to validation outcomes is not covered merely by counting the final candidates. The logarithmic factor accounts for the fixed family actually considered, not only the single model eventually reported.

## What changed \{#what-changed\}

A tail probability can be controlled without computing its full distribution. Stronger usable assumptions led to smaller bounds in the constructed Bernoulli example: a variance calculation gave $0.16$, bounded independent observations gave about $0.0996$, and the exact exponential moment gave about $0.0860$. Accounting for model selection adds a separate requirement: a simultaneous guarantee across the candidate family.

## Limitations \{#limitations\}

Nonnegativity, finite variance, boundedness and independence are distinct assumptions. None can be inferred just because an observed sample looks well behaved. If all $150$ observations are repeated copies of one Bernoulli variable, their average is that variable itself. Its variance is $0.24$, and a deviation of at least $0.1$ from $0.4$ occurs with probability $1$. Treating the repeated observations as independent would produce a false concentration claim.

The bounds describe probability over repeated random samples under a specified distribution. A confidence level is not a posterior probability that a fixed model's risk has a particular value after observing data. Distribution shift, adaptive validation use and unbounded losses require different assumptions or analysis. Exact binomial sums here verify one finite example; they do not prove a distribution-free theorem or a real system's reliability.

## What you should remember \{#what-you-should-remember\}

Start by writing the exact event and checking assumptions. Markov controls nonnegative variables through their mean; Chebyshev applies it to a squared deviation. Independent bounded sums admit exponential bounds via exponential moments. Keep the factor for two tails, and use a union bound when a guarantee must cover several candidates simultaneously. A numerical upper bound and an estimated probability are different objects.

## Code \{#code\}

This exact integer sum checks the specified binomial event; no Monte Carlo sampling is involved:

```python
from fractions import Fraction
import math

n = 150
numerators = [math.comb(n, k) * 2 ** k * 3 ** (n - k)
              for k in range(n + 1)]
tail = Fraction(sum(numerators[:46]) + sum(numerators[75:]), 5 ** n)
print(f"exact_tail={float(tail):.9f}")
print(f"chebyshev={24 / n:.9f} hoeffding={2 * math.exp(-n / 50):.9f}")
epsilon = math.sqrt(math.log(2 * 12 / 0.05) / (2 * 1200))
print(f"validation_epsilon={epsilon:.9f} risk_upper={0.09 + epsilon:.9f}")
```

```text
validation_epsilon=0.050718940 risk_upper=0.140718940
```
