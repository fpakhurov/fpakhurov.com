A waiting time, a height, the error of a measurement or the position of a random point can take any value in an interval. Each single value then has probability zero, as the [axioms note](/notes/probability/kolmogorovs-axioms) showed for a point in $[0, 1]$, so a list of probabilities of values, the PMF of the [previous note](/notes/probability/discrete-distributions), describes nothing. The probability sits on intervals instead, and its concentration at each point is described by a **density**. This note introduces densities, the distribution function and quantiles, and three distributions: the uniform, from which random numbers are made, the exponential, the waiting time with no memory, and the normal, which the [central limit theorem](/notes/probability/central-limit-theorem) will put behind every average.

**Notation.** $f_X$ is the density of $X$, $F_X(x) = P(X \le x)$ its distribution function (CDF), $F_X^{-1}(q)$ its $q$-quantile. $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ reads "$X$ is normal with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$"; $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ are the density and CDF of the standard normal $N(0, 1)$.

## Densities

A random variable $X$ is **continuous** if there is a function $f_X \ge 0$ with $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\, dx = 1$ such that for every interval

$$
P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X(x)\, dx .
$$

Probabilities are areas under the density. Whether the endpoints are included does not matter, since single points have probability 0. Sums over values become integrals; otherwise everything said about PMFs carries over.

A density is not a probability. For a short interval, $P(x \le X \le x + h) \approx f_X(x)\, h$: the density is probability *per unit length*, and it can exceed 1. A point uniform on $[0, 0.5]$ has density 2 on that interval. The units also change with the scale: a height measured in metres instead of centimetres has a density 100 times larger, while all probabilities stay the same.

## The distribution function and quantiles

The CDF $F_X(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f_X(t)\, dt$ rises from 0 to 1. Where it is differentiable, its derivative is the density, $F_X' = f_X$, so every probability is both an area under $f$ and a rise of $F$:

<DensityAndCdf />

The CDF answers "how likely is $X$ to be at most $x$"; its inverse answers "below which value does $X$ fall with probability $q$". The **$q$-quantile** is the $x$ with $F_X(x) = q$: the median is the $0.5$-quantile, the quartiles the $0.25$- and $0.75$-quantiles. Service-level targets are quantiles: a "p99 latency of 300 ms" says that the $0.99$-quantile of the response time is 300 ms. Inverting $F$ is also how random numbers with any distribution are made from uniform ones, the subject of the [next note](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables).

## Uniform

$X \sim U(a, b)$ has the constant density $1/(b - a)$ on $[a, b]$ and 0 outside: the probability of an interval inside $[a, b]$ is its length divided by $b - a$. A computer's random number generator produces (approximations to) independent $U(0, 1)$ numbers, and every other distribution in a simulation is made from them.

## Exponential

A machine fails, a request arrives, an atom decays. If such events happen at a constant rate $\lambda$ per unit time, the waiting time $T$ until the first one has the **exponential** distribution, $T \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$:

$$
f_T(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad t \ge 0 .
$$

It is the continuous version of the geometric distribution. Divide time into steps of length $\Delta$ and run a trial at every step with success probability $\lambda \Delta$. The number of steps until the first success is geometric, so $P(T > t) = (1 - \lambda \Delta)^{t/\Delta}$, which tends to $e^{-\lambda t}$ as $\Delta \to 0$:

<GeometricToExponential />

It is also the other face of the Poisson distribution. If the number of events in an interval of length $t$ is $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda t)$, the first event comes after $t$ exactly when the interval contains none: $P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^0 / 0! = e^{-\lambda t}$. For requests arriving at 2 per second, a whole second without a request has probability $e^{-2} = 0.135$.

Like the geometric, the exponential is **memoryless**: $P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = e^{-\lambda (s + t)} / e^{-\lambda s} = P(T > t)$. A component whose lifetime is exponential is as good as new at every moment; it does not age. The converse also holds: a survival function with $P(T > s + t) = P(T > s) P(T > t)$ for all $s, t$ must be exponential, so the exponential is the *only* memoryless continuous distribution. Real components do wear out, and their lifetimes are then modelled by distributions whose failure rate grows with age.

The exponential is skewed. With $\lambda = 2$ per second the mean wait is $1/\lambda = 0.5$ s, the median $\ln 2 / \lambda = 0.35$ s, and the $0.99$-quantile $\ln 100 / \lambda = 2.3$ s, more than four times the mean. A system sized for the average wait misses the tail.

## Normal

The **normal** (Gaussian) distribution $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has the density

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\Big(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big) ,
$$

a bell centred at $\mu$ with width set by $\sigma$. Every normal is a shifted and rescaled standard one: if $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma \sim N(0, 1)$, which the [next note](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables) proves. So one table, of $\Phi$, serves all normals; $\Phi$ has no formula in elementary functions and is computed numerically.

The **68–95–99.7 rule** gives the probability of falling within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean: $0.6827$, $0.9545$ and $0.9973$. A machine cuts rods of length $N(100, 0.2^2)$ mm, and rods outside $[99.5, 100.5]$ are rejected. Both limits are $2.5$ standard deviations from the mean, so the reject rate is $P(\lvert Z \rvert > 2.5) = 0.0124$.

The normal's tails are thin: the density falls like $e^{-x^2/2}$. $P(Z > 3) = 0.00135$, and $P(Z > 5) = 2.9 \cdot 10^{-7}$, about one in 3.5 million. Measurement errors and averages of many small effects are close to normal, for reasons the [central limit theorem](/notes/probability/central-limit-theorem) explains. Returns on financial assets, file sizes or word frequencies are not, and modelling them as normal underestimates extreme events by orders of magnitude.

## What changed

| | Discrete | Continuous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Described by | PMF $p_X(k) = P(X = k)$ | density $f_X(x)$, probability per unit length |
| $P(X = x)$ | can be positive | always 0 |
| $P(a \le X \le b)$ | sum of $p_X(k)$ | integral of $f_X$ |
| CDF | a staircase | continuous, with $F_X' = f_X$ |
| Waiting time | geometric, memoryless | exponential, the only memoryless one |

## Limitations

- **Not every variable is discrete or continuous.** Daily rainfall is exactly 0 with positive probability and continuous otherwise; such mixed distributions need a mass at 0 and a density elsewhere. (There are also continuous CDFs with no density at all, which do not appear in practice.)
- **A named distribution is a model.** The exponential assumes a constant rate, the normal assumes thin tails; checking the fit, as with the horse kicks of the previous note, comes before using either.
- **Densities depend on the units and the parametrization.** Probabilities do not; comparing density values across scales, or reading a density value as a probability, is a common error.

## What you should remember

- A continuous variable has $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X$; single values have probability 0; a density is probability per unit length and can exceed 1.
- $F_X(x) = P(X \le x)$, $F_X' = f_X$; the $q$-quantile solves $F_X(x) = q$ (median, quartiles, p99).
- Uniform $U(a, b)$: constant density $1/(b-a)$; the raw material of simulation.
- Exponential $\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$: $P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}$, mean $1/\lambda$, memoryless; the waiting time of a Poisson stream of events.
- Normal $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$: $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$ is standard normal; within 1, 2, 3 standard deviations with probability $0.683$, $0.954$, $0.997$; thin tails.

## Code

The numbers of the note with `scipy.stats`, and memorylessness by simulation:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

Z = stats.norm()                                                   # the standard normal
print([round(float(Z.cdf(k) - Z.cdf(-k)), 4) for k in (1, 2, 3)])   # [0.6827, 0.9545, 0.9973]
print(round(2 * Z.sf(2.5), 4), round(Z.sf(3), 5), f'{Z.sf(5):.1e}')   # 0.0124 0.00135 2.9e-07

print(stats.uniform(0, 0.5).pdf(0.2))                              # 2.0: a density, not a probability

T = stats.expon(scale=1 / 2)                                       # waiting time at a rate of 2 per second
print(round(T.sf(1), 4), T.mean(), round(T.median(), 3), round(T.ppf(0.99), 3))   # 0.1353 0.5 0.347 2.303

# memorylessness by simulation: P(T > 1.5 | T > 0.5) = P(T > 1)
t = np.random.default_rng(0).exponential(1 / 2, 1_000_000)
print(round((t > 1).mean(), 4), round((t[t > 0.5] > 1.5).mean(), 4))   # 0.1353 0.136

# a trial every d seconds with success probability d: P(wait > 1) tends to e^-1
print([round((1 - d) ** round(1 / d), 4) for d in (0.5, 0.1, 0.01)], round(np.exp(-1), 4))   # [0.25, 0.3487, 0.366] 0.3679
```
