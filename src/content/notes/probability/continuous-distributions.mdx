A waiting time, a height, the error of a measurement or the position of a random point can take any value in an interval. Each single value then has probability zero, as the [axioms note](/notes/probability/kolmogorovs-axioms) showed for a point in $[0, 1]$, so a list of probabilities of values, the PMF of the [previous note](/notes/probability/discrete-distributions), describes nothing. The probability sits on intervals instead, and its concentration at each point is described by a **density**. This note introduces densities, the distribution function and quantiles, and three distributions: the uniform, from which random numbers are made, the exponential, the waiting time with no memory, and the normal, which the [central limit theorem](/notes/probability/central-limit-theorem) will put behind every average. Two families close it: the gamma on positive numbers and the beta on $[0, 1]$.

**Notation.** $f_X$ is the density of $X$, $F_X(x) = P(X \le x)$ its distribution function (CDF), $F_X^{-1}(q)$ its $q$-quantile. $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ reads "$X$ is normal with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$"; $\varphi$ and $\Phi$ are the density and CDF of the standard normal $N(0, 1)$.

## Densities

A random variable $X$ is **continuous** (strictly, *absolutely* continuous) if there is a function $f_X \ge 0$ with $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\, dx = 1$ such that for every interval

$$
P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X(x)\, dx .
$$

Probabilities are areas under the density. Whether the endpoints are included does not matter, since single points have probability 0. Sums over values become integrals; otherwise everything said about PMFs carries over.

A density is not a probability. For a short interval, $P(x \le X \le x + h) \approx f_X(x)\, h$: the density is probability *per unit length*, and it can exceed 1. A point uniform on $[0, 0.5]$ has density 2 on that interval. A density also depends on the units: a height measured in metres instead of centimetres has a density 100 times larger, while all probabilities stay the same.

## The distribution function and quantiles

The CDF $F_X(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f_X(t)\, dt$ rises from 0 to 1. At every point where $f_X$ is continuous, the derivative of the CDF is the density, $F_X' = f_X$, so every probability is both an area under $f$ and a rise of $F$:

<DensityAndCdf />

The CDF answers "how likely is $X$ to be at most $x$"; its inverse answers "below which value does $X$ fall with probability $q$". The **$q$-quantile** is the smallest $x$ with $F_X(x) \ge q$,

$$
Q(q) = \inf\{x : F_X(x) \ge q\} .
$$

Where $F_X$ is continuous and strictly increasing, this is the unique $x$ with $F_X(x) = q$, written $F_X^{-1}(q)$. The general definition handles the two exceptions: a flat stretch of $F_X$, a range of values that $X$ never takes, would give many solutions of $F_X(x) = q$, and a jump, a value with positive probability, would give none. The median is the $0.5$-quantile, the quartiles the $0.25$- and $0.75$-quantiles. Service-level targets are quantiles: a "p99 latency of 300 ms" says that the $0.99$-quantile of the response time is 300 ms. Inverting $F$ is also how random numbers with any distribution are made from uniform ones, the subject of the [next note](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables).

## Uniform

$X \sim U(a, b)$ has the constant density $1/(b - a)$ on $[a, b]$ and 0 outside: the probability of an interval inside $[a, b]$ is its length divided by $b - a$. A computer's random number generator produces (approximations to) independent $U(0, 1)$ numbers, and every other distribution in a simulation is made from them.

## Exponential

A machine fails, a request arrives, an atom decays. Suppose such events happen at a constant rate $\lambda$ per unit time *and without memory*: in every short interval of length $\Delta$ an event happens with probability about $\lambda \Delta$, whatever happened before (a **homogeneous Poisson process**). Then the waiting time $T$ until the first one has the **exponential** distribution, $T \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_T(t) &= \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \\
P(T > t) &= e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad t \ge 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

It is the continuous version of the geometric distribution. Divide time into steps of length $\Delta$ and run a trial at every step with success probability $\lambda \Delta$. The number of steps until the first success is geometric, so $P(T > t) = (1 - \lambda \Delta)^{t/\Delta}$, which tends to $e^{-\lambda t}$ as $\Delta \to 0$:

<GeometricToExponential />

It is also the other face of the Poisson distribution. If the number of events in an interval of length $t$ is $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda t)$, the first event comes after $t$ exactly when the interval contains none: $P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t} (\lambda t)^0 / 0! = e^{-\lambda t}$. For requests arriving at 2 per second, a whole second without a request has probability $e^{-2} = 0.135$. A constant average rate alone is not enough. Buses that come exactly every 10 minutes also arrive at a rate of $1/10$ per minute, but a passenger who turns up at a random moment waits a uniform time on $[0, 10]$, with mean 5 minutes, not an exponential one with mean 10.

Like the geometric, the exponential is **memoryless**: $P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = e^{-\lambda (s + t)} / e^{-\lambda s} = P(T > t)$. A component whose lifetime is exponential is as good as new at every moment; it does not age. The converse also holds: a survival function with $P(T > s + t) = P(T > s) P(T > t)$ for all $s, t$ must be exponential, so the exponential is the *only* memoryless continuous distribution. Real components do wear out, and their lifetimes are then modelled by distributions whose failure rate grows with age.

The exponential is skewed. With $\lambda = 2$ per second the mean wait is $1/\lambda = 0.5$ s, the median $\ln 2 / \lambda = 0.35$ s, and the $0.99$-quantile $\ln 100 / \lambda = 2.3$ s, more than four times the mean. A system sized for the average wait misses the tail.

## Normal

The **normal** (Gaussian) distribution $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has the density

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\Big(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big) ,
$$

a bell centred at $\mu$ with width set by $\sigma$. Every normal is a shifted and rescaled standard one: if $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma \sim N(0, 1)$, which the [next note](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables) proves. So one table, of $\Phi$, serves all normals; $\Phi$ has no formula in elementary functions and is computed numerically.

The **68–95–99.7 rule** gives the probability of falling within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean: $0.6827$, $0.9545$ and $0.9973$. A machine cuts rods of length $N(100, 0.2^2)$ mm, and rods outside $[99.5, 100.5]$ are rejected. Both limits are $2.5$ standard deviations from the mean, so the reject rate is $P(\lvert Z \rvert > 2.5) = 0.0124$.

The normal's tails are thin: the density falls like $e^{-x^2/2}$. $P(Z > 3) = 0.00135$, and $P(Z > 5) = 2.9 \cdot 10^{-7}$, about one in 3.5 million. Measurement errors and averages of many small effects are close to normal, for reasons the [central limit theorem](/notes/probability/central-limit-theorem) explains. Returns on financial assets, file sizes or word frequencies are not, and modelling them as normal underestimates extreme events by orders of magnitude.

## Gamma

Requests arrive at $b = 2$ per second, and the [exponential](#exponential) gave the wait for the first one. How long until the third? The wait $T$ exceeds $t$ exactly when $[0, t]$ holds fewer than three requests, so with $\mathrm{Poisson}(bt)$ counts

$$
P(T > t) = \sum_{k=0}^{2} e^{-bt}\, \frac{(bt)^k}{k!} .
$$

At $t = 1$ this is $e^{-2}(1 + 2 + 2) = 0.677$. For the wait until the $a$-th event the sum runs to $k = a - 1$, and its derivative telescopes to the density, for $t > 0$,

$$
f_T(t) = \frac{b^a}{(a-1)!}\, t^{a-1} e^{-bt} .
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation: the density of the waiting time</summary>

Write $p_k(t) = e^{-bt}(bt)^k/k!$ for the Poisson probabilities, and $p_{-1} = 0$. The product rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_k'(t) &= -b\, p_k(t) + e^{-bt}\, \frac{k\, b\, (bt)^{k-1}}{k!} \\
&= b\, \big(p_{k-1}(t) - p_k(t)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The density is the derivative of the CDF $1 - P(T > t)$. In the sum, every $p_k$ with $k < a - 1$ appears once with each sign and cancels:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_T(t) &= -\frac{d}{dt} \sum_{k=0}^{a-1} p_k(t) \\
&= b \sum_{k=0}^{a-1} \big(p_k(t) - p_{k-1}(t)\big) \\
&= b\, p_{a-1}(t) = \frac{b^a\, t^{a-1} e^{-bt}}{(a-1)!} .
\end{aligned}
$$

</details>

Only the factorial requires an integer $a$. The **gamma function** extends it to every $a > 0$:

$$
\Gamma(a) = \int_0^\infty t^{a-1} e^{-t}\, dt .
$$

$\Gamma(1) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t}\, dt = 1$, and integration by parts gives the step from $a$ to $a + 1$; the boundary term vanishes at 0 because $a > 0$ and at infinity because $e^{-t}$ decays faster than any power grows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma(a + 1) &= \int_0^\infty t^{a} e^{-t}\, dt \\
&= \big[-t^a e^{-t}\big]_0^\infty + a \int_0^\infty t^{a-1} e^{-t}\, dt \\
&= a\, \Gamma(a) .
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\Gamma(n) = (n - 1)!$ for integers $n$, and the **gamma distribution** with **shape** $a > 0$ and **rate** $b > 0$, $X \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a, b)$, has the density, for $x > 0$,

$$
f(x) = \frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}\, x^{a-1} e^{-bx} .
$$

It integrates to 1, because the substitution $t = bx$ turns the definition of $\Gamma(c)$ into

$$
\int_0^\infty x^{c-1} e^{-bx}\, dx = \frac{\Gamma(c)}{b^c} ,
$$

here with $c = a$. With $a = 1$, $\Gamma(1) = 1$ and $x^0 = 1$, the density is $b e^{-bx}$: $\mathrm{Gamma}(1, b) = \mathrm{Exp}(b)$. The shape sets the form. For $a < 1$ the density is infinite at 0, for $a = 1$ it falls from $b$, and for $a > 1$ it rises from 0 to a peak where the derivative of $\ln f$, $(a - 1)/x - b$, vanishes, at $x = (a - 1)/b$.

The moments come from the same integral, with $c = a + 1$ and $c = a + 2$, the [definition of the expectation](/notes/probability/expectation-and-variance#expectation) $E[X] = \int x f(x)\, dx$, and $\Gamma(a + 2) = (a + 1)\, a\, \Gamma(a)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= \frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(a + 1)}{b^{a+1}} = \frac ab, \\
E[X^2] &= \frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(a + 2)}{b^{a+2}} = \frac{a(a + 1)}{b^2}, \\
\operatorname{Var}(X) &= E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = \frac{a}{b^2} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

the last line by the [variance formula](/notes/probability/expectation-and-variance#variance). For $a = 1$ they are the exponential's $1/b$ and $1/b^2$. The wait for the third request at 2 per second has mean $1.5$ s and standard deviation $\sqrt3/2 = 0.87$ s.

A gamma variable is positive, and its density is a power of $x$ times an exponential in $x$. A normal likelihood, read as a function of the precision $\lambda = 1/\sigma^2$, has the same form, so the Bayesian methods course uses the gamma as the [prior for a precision](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#normal-with-known-mean-a-gamma-prior-on-the-precision). Libraries often parametrize by the scale $1/b$ instead of the rate: `scipy.stats.gamma(a, scale=1/b)` is $\mathrm{Gamma}(a, b)$ here.

## Beta

A click-through rate, a share of votes or the probability of heads lies in $[0, 1]$. The uniform spreads belief evenly over that interval; the **beta distribution** with shape parameters $a, b > 0$, $X \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$, tilts it with powers of $x$ and $1 - x$. For $0 < x < 1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= \frac{x^{a-1} (1 - x)^{b-1}}{B(a, b)}, \\
B(a, b) &= \int_0^1 x^{a-1} (1 - x)^{b-1}\, dx = \frac{\Gamma(a)\, \Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a + b)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The **beta function** $B(a, b)$ is the normalizing constant, and its value through $\Gamma$ follows from a change of variables in a double integral.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: the beta function through the gamma function</summary>

Write $\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)$ as a double integral over $s, r > 0$ ([Fubini](/notes/math/multiple-integrals#fubini-one-variable-at-a-time)) and [change variables](/notes/math/multiple-integrals#the-change-of-variables-formula) to $s = tx$, $r = t(1 - x)$ with $t > 0$ and $0 < x < 1$. The Jacobian determinant of $(t, x) \mapsto (s, r)$ is $x \cdot (-t) - t \cdot (1 - x) = -t$, so $ds\, dr = t\, dt\, dx$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)
&= \int_0^\infty \!\! \int_0^\infty s^{a-1} r^{b-1} e^{-s-r}\, ds\, dr \\
&= \int_0^\infty \!\! \int_0^1 (tx)^{a-1} \big(t(1 - x)\big)^{b-1} e^{-t}\, t\, dx\, dt \\
&= \int_0^\infty t^{a+b-1} e^{-t}\, dt \int_0^1 x^{a-1} (1 - x)^{b-1}\, dx \\
&= \Gamma(a + b)\, B(a, b) .
\end{aligned}
$$

</details>

With $a = b = 1$ the density is $1/B(1, 1) = \Gamma(2)/(\Gamma(1)\Gamma(1)) = 1$ on $[0, 1]$: $\mathrm{Beta}(1, 1) = U(0, 1)$. A larger $a$ moves the mass toward 1, a larger $b$ toward 0, and $a = b$ gives a density symmetric about $1/2$. For $a, b > 1$ the peak is where the derivative of $\ln f$, $(a - 1)/x - (b - 1)/(1 - x)$, vanishes, at $x = (a - 1)/(a + b - 2)$.

Multiplying the density by $x$ raises the power $a - 1$ by one, so the moments are ratios of beta functions. In the second line $\Gamma(b)$ cancels, $\Gamma(a + 1) = a\,\Gamma(a)$ and $\Gamma(a + b + 1) = (a + b)\,\Gamma(a + b)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E[X] &= \frac{B(a + 1, b)}{B(a, b)} \\
&= \frac{\Gamma(a + 1)\, \Gamma(a + b)}{\Gamma(a)\, \Gamma(a + b + 1)} = \frac{a}{a + b}, \\
E[X^2] &= \frac{B(a + 2, b)}{B(a, b)} = \frac{a(a + 1)}{(a + b)(a + b + 1)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The variance is $E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$ over the common denominator, where the bracket is $a^2 + ab + a + b - a^2 - ab - a = b$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X)
&= \frac{a\big[(a + 1)(a + b) - a(a + b + 1)\big]}{(a + b)^2 (a + b + 1)} \\
&= \frac{ab}{(a + b)^2 (a + b + 1)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $\mathrm{Beta}(1, 1)$ this gives the uniform's mean $1/2$ and variance $1/12$. For $\mathrm{Beta}(2, 5)$, $B(2, 5) = 1! \cdot 4!/6! = 1/30$, the mean is $2/7 = 0.286$, the variance $10/392 = 0.0255$, and the peak at $x = 1/5$ has height $30 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 0.8^4 = 2.46$, the envelope constant of the [rejection sampling example](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#rejection-sampling). The beta is the standard prior for a probability: after $k$ heads in $n$ flips a $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ prior becomes the posterior $\mathrm{Beta}(a + k, b + n - k)$, as the [Bayesian inference note](/notes/probability/bayesian-inference#conjugate-priors-the-beta-binomial) derives.

## What changed

| | Discrete | Continuous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Described by | PMF $p_X(k) = P(X = k)$ | density $f_X(x)$, probability per unit length |
| $P(X = x)$ | can be positive | always 0 |
| $P(a \le X \le b)$ | sum of $p_X(k)$ | integral of $f_X$ |
| CDF | a staircase | continuous, with $F_X' = f_X$ |
| Waiting time | geometric, memoryless | exponential, the only memoryless one |

## Limitations

- **Not every variable is discrete or continuous.** Daily rainfall is exactly 0 with positive probability and continuous otherwise; such mixed distributions need a mass at 0 and a density elsewhere. (There are also continuous CDFs with no density at all, which do not appear in practice.)
- **A named distribution is a model.** The exponential assumes a constant rate, the normal assumes thin tails; checking the fit, as with the horse kicks of the previous note, comes before using either.
- **Densities depend on the units and the parametrization.** Probabilities do not; comparing density values across scales, or reading a density value as a probability, is a common error.

## What you should remember

- A continuous variable has $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f_X$; single values have probability 0; a density is probability per unit length and can exceed 1.
- $F_X(x) = P(X \le x)$, $F_X' = f_X$; the $q$-quantile solves $F_X(x) = q$ (median, quartiles, p99).
- Uniform $U(a, b)$: constant density $1/(b-a)$; the raw material of simulation.
- Exponential $\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$: $P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}$, mean $1/\lambda$, memoryless; the waiting time of a Poisson stream of events.
- Normal $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$: $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$ is standard normal; within 1, 2, 3 standard deviations with probability $0.683$, $0.954$, $0.997$; thin tails.
- $\mathrm{Gamma}(a, b)$: mean $a/b$, variance $a/b^2$, $\mathrm{Gamma}(1, b) = \mathrm{Exp}(b)$. $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$: mean $a/(a+b)$, variance $ab/((a+b)^2(a+b+1))$, $\mathrm{Beta}(1, 1) = U(0, 1)$.

## Code

The numbers of the note with `scipy.stats`, memorylessness by simulation, and the gamma and beta examples:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

Z = stats.norm()                                                   # the standard normal
print([round(float(Z.cdf(k) - Z.cdf(-k)), 4) for k in (1, 2, 3)])   # [0.6827, 0.9545, 0.9973]
print(round(2 * Z.sf(2.5), 4), round(Z.sf(3), 5), f'{Z.sf(5):.1e}')   # 0.0124 0.00135 2.9e-07

print(stats.uniform(0, 0.5).pdf(0.2))                              # 2.0: a density, not a probability

T = stats.expon(scale=1 / 2)                                       # waiting time at a rate of 2 per second
print(round(T.sf(1), 4), T.mean(), round(T.median(), 3), round(T.ppf(0.99), 3))   # 0.1353 0.5 0.347 2.303

# memorylessness by simulation: P(T > 1.5 | T > 0.5) = P(T > 1)
t = np.random.default_rng(0).exponential(1 / 2, 1_000_000)
print(round((t > 1).mean(), 4), round((t[t > 0.5] > 1.5).mean(), 4))   # 0.1353 0.136

# a trial every d seconds with success probability d: P(wait > 1) tends to e^-1
print([round((1 - d) ** round(1 / d), 4) for d in (0.5, 0.1, 0.01)], round(np.exp(-1), 4))   # [0.25, 0.3487, 0.366] 0.3679

# gamma and beta; scipy takes the scale 1/b, not the rate b
G = stats.gamma(3, scale=1 / 2)                                    # the wait for the third request at 2 per second
print(round(G.sf(1), 3), G.mean(), round(G.std(), 3))              # 0.677 1.5 0.866
print(np.isclose(stats.gamma(1, scale=1 / 2).pdf(0.7), stats.expon(scale=1 / 2).pdf(0.7)), round(float(stats.beta(1, 1).pdf(0.3)), 6))   # True 1.0
B = stats.beta(2, 5)
print(round(B.mean(), 3), round(B.var(), 4), round(B.pdf(0.2), 4))   # 0.286 0.0255 2.4576
```
