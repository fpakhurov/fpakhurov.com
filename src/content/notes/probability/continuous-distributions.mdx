A rounded measurement can have a probability table even when its underlying value varies continuously. Suppose we construct a mathematical model on the interval from −1 to 3, with more probability near the upper end. Asking for the probability of the exact value 2 will give zero. Asking for the probability of a reading between 0 and 2 will give one half. The interval, including its width, carries the probability.

## Densities \{#densities\}

In [Discrete distributions](/notes/probability/discrete-distributions), we added probabilities attached to individual values. For an **absolutely continuous distribution**, we instead define a nonnegative probability density $f$ whose total integral is one. For any interval with $a<b$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx&=1,\\
P(a<X\le b)&=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.
\end{aligned}
$$

The integral over a single point is zero, so changing either interval endpoint from open to closed leaves the probability unchanged. A continuous cumulative distribution need not have a density: singular continuous distributions are possible. Here we work with the absolutely continuous case, where these integral formulas apply ([Siegrist, Continuous Distributions](#references)).

Our independently stipulated example has a linearly increasing density:

$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}
(x+1)/8,&-1\le x\le3,\\
0,&x\notin[-1,3].
\end{cases}
$$

Its normalization follows by integrating the line. The same antiderivative gives the interval probability:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-1}^{3}\frac{x+1}{8}\,dx
&=\left[\frac{(x+1)^2}{16}\right]_{-1}^{3}=1,\\
P(0\le X\le2)
&=\frac{9}{16}-\frac{1}{16}=\frac12.
\end{aligned}
$$

A density has units reciprocal to those of the measurement. If we express the same value as $Y=X/5$, an interval of width $dy$ corresponds to width $5\,dy$ on the original scale. Preserving its probability therefore gives, for $-1/5<y<3/5$,

$$
f_Y(y)=5f_X(5y)=\frac{5(5y+1)}8.
$$

The density approaches $5/2$ at the upper edge. Values above one are valid; the integral over the whole support is still one. At a continuity point of $f$, the probability in a short interval is approximately $f(x)\,h$ for width $h>0$. The approximation can fail near a boundary or across a rapidly changing density.

## The distribution function and quantiles \{#the-distribution-function-and-quantiles\}

The **cumulative distribution function**, or CDF, records the probability accumulated up to a threshold. It is defined for every real-valued random variable. For a density model,

$$
F(x)=P(X\le x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt.
$$

A CDF is nondecreasing and right-continuous, with limits zero and one at the two ends of the real line. Subtracting its values gives $P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)$. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives $F'(x)=f(x)$ wherever the density is continuous; more generally the equality holds almost everywhere for an absolutely continuous CDF.

For our rising density, integration gives

$$
F(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&x<-1,\\
(x+1)^2/16,&-1\le x\le3,\\
1,&x>3.
\end{cases}
$$

We can also start with a required probability and seek its threshold. For $0<p<1$, define the **lower quantile** by

$$
Q(p)=\inf\{x:F(x)\ge p\}.
$$

The infimum selects the first threshold reaching the requested probability. When the CDF is continuous and strictly increasing across its support, solving $F(x)=p$ gives the ordinary inverse. In this example,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(p)&=4\sqrt p-1,\\
Q(9/16)&=2,\\
Q(1/2)&=2\sqrt2-1\approx1.828427.
\end{aligned}
$$

The generalized definition also handles flat sections. Consider a separate model which chooses uniformly from $[-1,0]$ with probability $1/2$ and uniformly from $[2,4]$ otherwise. Its CDF is $1/2$ throughout $[0,2]$. Every point in that interval is a median, while the lower-quantile convention selects $Q(1/2)=0$. An inverse routine needs this convention when a solution is not unique ([Siegrist, Distribution and Quantile Functions](#references)).

## Uniform \{#uniform\}

If equal-length subintervals inside a bounded range should have equal probabilities, a constant density implements that assumption. For finite endpoints $a<b$, write $U\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(a,b)$. Requiring total mass one determines the height. Integrating and then solving for the threshold gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_U(x)&=\frac1{b-a},\quad a<x<b,\\
F_U(x)&=\frac{x-a}{b-a},\quad a\le x\le b,\\
Q_U(p)&=a+(b-a)p,\quad 0<p<1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The density is zero outside the interval; the CDF is zero below it and one above it. For our choice $a=-2$ and $b=5$, the interval from 1 to 3 occupies two of the seven available length units, so its probability is $2/7$. The upper quartile is $Q_U(3/4)=13/4$.

Uniformity belongs to a chosen scale. If $U$ is uniform on $[0,1]$ and $V=U^2$, then $P(V\le v)=P(U\le\sqrt v)=\sqrt v$ for $0\le v\le1$. The transformed distribution has a curved CDF. Calling a model uniform therefore requires specifying the variable as well as its allowed range.

## Exponential \{#exponential\}

The geometric distribution counts independent trials until the first success. To obtain a waiting time, place trials at intervals of length $\Delta>0$. Set their success probability to $p_\Delta=\lambda\Delta$, where the rate $\lambda>0$ is fixed and $\Delta<1/\lambda$. If $K$ counts trials starting at one, define $T_\Delta=\Delta K$.

For fixed $t\ge0$, waiting longer than $t$ requires failure in all trials scheduled by that time. There are $\lfloor t/\Delta\rfloor$ such trials, hence

$$
P(T_\Delta>t)=(1-\lambda\Delta)^{\lfloor t/\Delta\rfloor}.
$$

Taking logarithms makes the limit explicit. We use $\log(1-u)=-u+O(u^2)$ as $u\to0$ and $\Delta\lfloor t/\Delta\rfloor\to t$. The logarithm tends to $-\lambda t$, giving the **exponential distribution**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S(t)&=P(T>t)=e^{-\lambda t},\\
F(t)&=1-e^{-\lambda t},\\
f(t)&=\lambda e^{-\lambda t},\quad t>0,\\
Q(p)&=-\frac{\log(1-p)}{\lambda},\quad 0<p<1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $S$ is the survival function. The density comes from differentiating $F$; the quantile comes from solving $F(t)=p$. For $t<0$, both the density and CDF are zero. With our chosen rate $\lambda=3/5$, the discretization at $t=2$ approaches the limit as follows:

| Trial spacing $\Delta$ | $P(T_\Delta>2)$ |
| --- | --- |
| 0.50 | 0.240100 |
| 0.20 | 0.278501 |
| 0.05 | 0.295712 |

The limiting probability is $e^{-6/5}\approx0.301194$. These are computed probabilities of stipulated models, not observations of a real arrival process.

The exponential law is **memoryless**. For $s,t\ge0$, conditioning on survival to $s$ gives

$$
P(T>s+t\mid T>s)
=\frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}}
=e^{-\lambda t}.
$$

Elapsed waiting time leaves the conditional distribution of the remaining wait unchanged. This is a substantive modeling assumption. For a uniform wait on $[0,4]$, the probability of surviving past 3 given survival past 1 is $1/3$, whereas surviving past 2 unconditionally has probability $1/2$.

Conversely, for a proper nonnegative waiting time with positive survival at every finite time, memorylessness implies $S(s+t)=S(s)S(t)$. Iterating this identity determines $S$ on rational times; right-continuity determines it on all times. The result is $S(t)=e^{-\lambda t}$ with $\lambda>0$ ([Siegrist, The Exponential Distribution, Memoryless Property](#references)). No empirical waiting-time dataset is guaranteed to satisfy this assumption.

## Normal \{#normal\}

A symmetric model with an unbounded range is often useful for fluctuations around a center. Define the normal distribution $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ for $\mu\in\mathbb R$ and $\sigma>0$ by

$$
f_X(x)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$

The second parameter is the variance; $\sigma$ is the standard deviation. The normalizing constant follows from the Gaussian integral. One proof squares the integral of $e^{-x^2/2}$ and uses polar coordinates to evaluate the resulting two-dimensional integral as $2\pi$ ([Siegrist, The Normal Distribution, Standard Normal Distribution](#references)).

Subtracting the center and measuring distance in units of $\sigma$ gives $Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. Define its CDF as $\Phi$. Transforming the two endpoints then gives

$$
P(a<X\le b)
=\Phi\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)
-\Phi\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
$$

For the independently chosen parameters $\mu=7/4$ and $\sigma=3/5$, the intervals within one, two and three standard deviations have the following probabilities. Their values depend only on the standardized endpoints, so the same table applies to every normal distribution.

| Distance from $\mu$ | Probability inside |
| --- | --- |
| At most $\sigma$ | 0.682689 |
| At most $2\sigma$ | 0.954500 |
| At most $3\sigma$ | 0.997300 |

Symmetry gives $P(|Z|>2.5)=2[1-\Phi(2.5)]\approx0.012419$. The familiar percentages are properties of a normal model. Knowing a variable's mean and variance alone does not establish these tail probabilities. A normal model also assigns positive probability to every open interval, which matters when the quantity has strict physical bounds.

## Gamma \{#gamma\}

An exponential variable describes one memoryless waiting stage. A sum of several independent stages with the same rate motivates the **gamma family**. We use a shape $\alpha>0$ and a rate $\beta>0$. Define the gamma function and the density by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma(\alpha)&=\int_0^\infty u^{\alpha-1}e^{-u}\,du,\\
f_X(x)&=\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x},\quad x>0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The density is zero for negative $x$; its value at zero can be chosen arbitrarily without changing probabilities. Near zero, the defining integral is finite because $\alpha>0$; at infinity, exponential decay dominates the power. Substituting $u=\beta x$ in the density integral gives exactly $\Gamma(\alpha)/\Gamma(\alpha)=1$. Integration by parts gives $\Gamma(\alpha+1)=\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)$, and $\Gamma(1)=1$ gives $\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!$ for positive integers.

For integer shape $n$, the density is that of a sum of $n$ independent exponential variables of rate $\beta$. This follows by induction using convolution; see the proof in [Siegrist, The Gamma Distribution, Poisson Process](#references). At shape one, the formula itself reduces to the exponential density. Positive noninteger shapes remain valid density models, without an interpretation as a literal noninteger count of stages.

For our choice $\alpha=3$ and $\beta=2$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_X(x)&=4x^2e^{-2x},\quad x>0,\\
F_X(x)&=1-e^{-2x}(1+2x+2x^2),\quad x\ge0,\\
P(X\le1)&=1-5e^{-2}\approx0.323324.
\end{aligned}
$$

The CDF is checked directly: its value at zero is zero, its limit is one, and differentiating it gives $4x^2e^{-2x}$. Parameters need units too. The rate $\beta$ has inverse units of $X$; a scale parameter would be $\theta=1/\beta$. In this example the scale is $1/2$, so supplying a scale of 2 to an implementation would specify a different distribution.

## Beta \{#beta\}

For a continuously varying proportion, the interval $[0,1]$ is a natural support. The **beta family** controls how density accumulates near its two ends. For $\alpha,\beta>0$, define

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(\alpha,\beta)&=\int_0^1u^{\alpha-1}(1-u)^{\beta-1}\,du,\\
f_X(x)&=\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)},
\quad 0<x<1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The density is zero outside the interval. Both endpoint integrals are finite because their power exponents exceed −1. Dividing by $B$ normalizes the density. The two beta parameters are shapes; the symbol $\beta$ no longer denotes the gamma rate. We choose the distribution family before interpreting its parameters.

Our example uses $\alpha=2$ and $\beta=3$. Expanding and integrating $x(1-x)^2$ over $[0,1]$ gives $B(2,3)=1/12$. Consequently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_X(x)&=12x(1-x)^2,\\
F_X(x)&=6x^2-8x^3+3x^4,\quad 0\le x\le1,\\
P(1/4\le X\le3/4)&=F_X(3/4)-F_X(1/4)=11/16.
\end{aligned}
$$

Differentiating the density gives $f'_X(x)=12(1-x)(1-3x)$. It rises up to $x=1/3$ and falls afterwards, so its mode is $1/3$. When both shape parameters equal one, the density is uniform. A shape below one permits the density to diverge at its corresponding endpoint while remaining integrable. Even then, an exact endpoint has zero probability under the beta model ([Siegrist, The Beta Distribution](#references)).

## What changed \{#what-changed\}

Sums of point masses have become integrals over intervals. The CDF still answers the same threshold question for discrete and continuous variables. A density plot needs its horizontal scale: its height alone does not specify a probability.

## Limitations \{#limitations\}

Recording precision changes the observed distribution. Round our rising-density variable to the nearest integer and call the result $R$. Integrating over the rounding bins gives:

| Recorded $R$ | −1 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Probability | 1/64 | 1/8 | 1/4 | 3/8 | 15/64 |

For instance, $R=2$ corresponds to $1.5\le X<2.5$ and has probability $3/8$. Ties have probability zero. A continuous model for the latent value is compatible with repeated identical recorded readings, and their probabilities require integrating across bins.

A continuous density also cannot represent a genuine atom, such as a positive probability of exactly zero. Such a model needs an explicit discrete component. Finally, a fitted family and its estimated quantiles carry uncertainty: calculating its p99 exactly does not establish the population p99 or a guaranteed upper bound on future observations.

## What you should remember \{#what-you-should-remember\}

- Interval probabilities are areas under a density; point probabilities are zero.
- A CDF accumulates mass; a lower quantile picks the first threshold reaching it.
- Uniformity depends on the measurement scale, and exponential waiting assumes memorylessness.
- Normal tail percentages require a normal model.
- Gamma rate and scale are reciprocals; beta shapes describe a bounded density.

## Code \{#code\}

This standard-library calculation reproduces selected values above. It evaluates formulas directly; no fitted model, external dataset or random sample is involved.

```python
from math import exp, erf, sqrt

print(f"rising median: {2 * sqrt(2) - 1:.6f}")
for delta, n in [(0.5, 4), (0.2, 10), (0.05, 40)]:
    print(f"survival at 2, delta={delta:.2f}: {(1 - 0.6 * delta) ** n:.6f}")
print(f"continuous survival at 2: {exp(-1.2):.6f}")
print(f"normal within 2 sigma: {erf(2 / sqrt(2)):.6f}")
print(f"gamma CDF at 1: {1 - 5 * exp(-2):.6f}")
```

```text
rising median: 1.828427
survival at 2, delta=0.50: 0.240100
survival at 2, delta=0.20: 0.278501
survival at 2, delta=0.05: 0.295712
continuous survival at 2: 0.301194
normal within 2 sigma: 0.954500
gamma CDF at 1: 0.323324
```
