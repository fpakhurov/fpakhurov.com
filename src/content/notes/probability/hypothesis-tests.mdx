Ten successes in twelve trials look unusual if each trial succeeds with probability one half. Nine successes look unusual too. A decision needs a boundary chosen before seeing the count, together with a calculation of how often that boundary is crossed when the null model is correct.

The sampling design is part of that calculation. Inspecting another metric, waiting for another batch or reallocating treatment labels can change the rule even when the final screen still displays the same p-value formula.

## The logic of a test

A **null hypothesis** $H_0$ specifies parameter values or distributions to be assessed. An alternative $H_1$ describes the departures of interest. Let $R$ be the rejection region: the set of possible datasets for which the rule rejects. For $0<\alpha<1$, a level-$\alpha$ test requires

$$
\sup_{\theta\in H_0}\Pr_\theta\{X\in R\}\leq\alpha.
$$

Rejecting a true null is a type I error. At a particular alternative $\theta$, the **power** is the probability of rejection; one minus that probability is the type II error rate. Reducing type I error by making $R$ smaller can reduce power too. These are properties of a rule across possible samples ([Neyman and Pearson, 1933](#references), §II, pp. 296–297).

For an exact example, let $K$ count successes among twelve independent Bernoulli trials. Test $H_0:p=0.5$ against $H_1:p>0.5$. Large counts support this directional alternative. If we reject at $K\geq10$, the null rejection probability is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr_{0.5}\{K\geq10\}
&=2^{-12}\sum_{k=10}^{12}\binom{12}{k}\\
&=\frac{79}{4096}\\
&\approx0.019287.
\end{aligned}
$$

Allowing $K=9$ would increase it to 0.072998, above 0.05. Thus the nonrandomized upper-tail rule has size 0.019287 even when designed for level 0.05. Discrete outcomes prevent arbitrary exact error rates. The same region also has level at most 0.05 for the composite null $p\leq0.5$: couple each trial to a shared uniform variable and observe that success counts increase with $p$.

At $p=0.75$, its power is

$$
\sum_{k=10}^{12}\binom{12}{k}(0.75)^k(0.25)^{12-k}
\approx0.390675.
$$

The rule therefore misses this fairly large departure more often than it detects it. Failure to reject cannot establish that $p=0.5$. Power belongs to a specified alternative and sample size; a test has no single power value independent of effect size.

<TestLogic />

For an observed count $k$, define the one-sided **p-value** by its null upper tail:

$$
p_{\mathrm{obs}}=\Pr_{0.5}\{K\geq k\}.
$$

At $k=10$ it is 0.019287; at $k=9$ it is 0.072998. Rejecting when this value is at most 0.05 reproduces the chosen region. More generally, a valid p-value $P(X)$ obeys

$$
\Pr_\theta\{P(X)\leq u\}\leq u
$$

for every true-null parameter and every $u\in[0,1]$. In a discrete model, this inequality need not be equality. The tail includes outcomes at least as extreme as the observation, including ties; assigning probability only to the observed count would give a different, generally invalid rule.

## The A/B test

Consider two independent randomized groups of equal size eighty. Let successes be 24 in A and 38 in B. These invented counts illustrate a fixed-horizon comparison of Bernoulli probabilities, with one observation per independent unit. The estimated absolute effect is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat p_A&=0.3,\\
\hat p_B&=0.475,\\
\hat\Delta&=\hat p_B-\hat p_A\\
&=0.175.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the null $p_A=p_B=p$, independence gives the variance of the difference:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\hat p_B-\hat p_A)
=p(1-p)\left(\frac1{n_A}+\frac1{n_B}\right).
$$

Estimate the common null probability using all successes and observations. This pooled estimate and the test statistic are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat p_{\mathrm{pool}}&=\frac{24+38}{80+80}\\
&=0.3875,\\
\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}_0&\approx0.077030,\\
Z&=\frac{\hat\Delta}{\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}_0}\\
&\approx2.271847.
\end{aligned}
$$

With sufficiently large group counts, the null standardized difference is approximately normal by the central limit theorem. A two-sided normal tail gives $p_{\mathrm{obs}}\approx0.02310$. The two-sided choice must precede inspection of the direction; choosing the more favorable one-sided tail afterwards changes the test.

To estimate the effect away from the null, allow the groups to have different probabilities. The unpooled variance estimate is

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\hat\Delta)^2
=\frac{\hat p_A(1-\hat p_A)}{n_A}
+\frac{\hat p_B(1-\hat p_B)}{n_B}.
$$

Its square root is 0.075777. The approximate 95% effect interval is $[0.02648,0.32352]$: about 2.65 to 32.35 percentage points. This wide range matters even though the test rejects at 0.05. An exact numerical duality between a test and an interval requires inversion of that same test; a pooled test and an unpooled Wald interval can differ near a boundary.

Randomization identifies the effect of assignment under the experimental assumptions. Counting many sessions from the same user as independent can understate uncertainty. Interference between users, selective outcome collection and changes to treatment during the test also alter the model. Decide the unit, outcome, horizon and analysis before looking at the result; set a practically relevant effect size for planning power.

## What a p-value is not

A tail probability under $H_0$ does not assign probability to $H_0$. A posterior probability requires a model for alternatives and prior weights. Likewise, a p-value is not the chance that a reported discovery is false, the size of an effect or the chance of replication. Each question conditions on different information.

Large samples can detect effects too small to matter operationally; small samples can leave important effects unresolved. Report an effect estimate and a compatible uncertainty interval alongside the test. To argue that an effect is sufficiently small, specify an equivalence margin and use a procedure designed for that claim. A nonsignificant difference alone does not supply it.

## Peeking

Suppose we observe independent Bernoulli outcomes under $p=0.5$ and check cumulative counts after 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 observations. At every look choose the smallest count whose exact upper-tail probability is at most 0.05. Every one of these six fixed-horizon tests is valid on its own. The rule that stops at the first rejection uses the union of their rejection events ([Johari, Pekelis and Walsh, 2019](#references), §§3–4).

The cumulative counts are dependent, so multiplying six nonrejection probabilities would be wrong. We can count paths exactly. Let $A_n(k)$ be the number of length-$n$ binary sequences with $k$ successes that have survived all earlier looks. Starting from $A_0(0)=1$, extend each surviving sequence by zero or one:

$$
\widetilde A_n(k)=A_{n-1}(k)+A_{n-1}(k-1).
$$

At a scheduled look with boundary $c_n$, set $A_n(k)=0$ for $k\geq c_n$; otherwise keep $\widetilde A_n(k)$. At unscheduled times keep every extension. Let $\tau$ be the first rejection time, or infinity when no rejection occurs. Each complete null sequence has probability $2^{-n}$, so

$$
\Pr_{0.5}\{\tau\leq n\}
=1-2^{-n}\sum_kA_n(k).
$$

| Observations | Reject at count | This look alone | Any look so far |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 9 | 0.01074 | 0.01074 |
| 20 | 15 | 0.02069 | 0.02715 |
| 30 | 20 | 0.04937 | 0.06084 |
| 40 | 26 | 0.04035 | 0.07428 |
| 50 | 32 | 0.03245 | 0.08203 |
| 60 | 37 | 0.04623 | 0.09649 |

By the sixth look, the false-positive probability is nearly 9.65%. We have changed the sampling rule, despite retaining a valid formula at each individual look.

<Peeking />

A planned fixed horizon is one remedy. For finitely many planned looks, allocate error budgets $\alpha_j$ whose sum is at most $\alpha$; the union bound then controls the chance of any false rejection without requiring independent looks. This can be conservative. Sequential methods instead calibrate a complete stopping rule or an always-valid p-value process. Repeatedly applying an ordinary fixed-horizon cutoff is not such a calibration.

## Multiple comparisons

The same issue appears when we inspect many endpoints or variants. With eight independent tests that each reject a true null with probability exactly 0.05, the chance of at least one false rejection is

$$
1-0.95^8\approx0.336580.
$$

Independence is needed for that product, but not for the **Bonferroni** guarantee. Let $V$ count false rejections. If each true-null p-value is valid, testing each of $m$ hypotheses at level $\alpha/m$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr\{V\geq1\}
&\leq\sum_{i\in I_0}\Pr\{P_i\leq\alpha/m\}\\
&\leq |I_0|\frac\alpha m\\
&\leq\alpha,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $I_0$ indexes the unknown set of true nulls. For eight tests and a familywise level of 0.05, the individual cutoff is 0.00625.

**Holm's step-down procedure** uses the ordered p-values. Compare the smallest to $\alpha/m$, the next to $\alpha/(m-1)$, and continue only while every comparison passes. Stop at the first failure and retain that hypothesis and all remaining ones ([Holm, 1979](#references), §2, Theorem 1).

For five invented ordered p-values $0.003,0.014,0.021,0.18,0.4$, the first comparison passes against 0.01. The second fails against 0.0125. Only the first hypothesis is rejected. We do not restart the procedure at a later, larger threshold.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: why Holm controls familywise error</summary>

If $m_0=0$, there are no true nulls and the familywise error is zero. Now suppose there are $m_0\geq1$ true nulls. At the rank $r$ of the first true null, at least $m_0$ hypotheses remain. If any true null is rejected, this first one must pass a cutoff no larger than $\alpha/m_0$. Its p-value is the smallest among the true nulls. The union bound and individual validity give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr\{V\geq1\}
&\leq\sum_{i\in I_0}\Pr\{P_i\leq\alpha/m_0\}\\
&\leq\alpha.
\end{aligned}
$$

This proof permits dependence between tests. It controls the probability of at least one false rejection, a different target from the expected fraction of false discoveries among rejected hypotheses.

</details>

## Permutation tests

A reference distribution can come from the assignment mechanism. Consider six units with fixed outcomes $0,1,2,4,7,9$. Assign exactly three units to B, choosing uniformly among the twenty possible groups. Under the **sharp null** that treatment changes no unit's outcome, these values stay fixed under every assignment. Use the absolute difference of group means as the test statistic.

For the observed assignment A=$[0,1,2]$ and B=$[4,7,9]$, the mean difference is $17/3$. Exactly two of the twenty assignments are at least as extreme in absolute value: this split and its reversal. The two-sided randomization p-value is therefore 0.1. A prespecified upper-tail statistic would give 0.05; choosing it after seeing which group is larger would discard half of the relevant tail.

The validity follows from uniform assignment, conditional on fixed outcomes. Rank the twenty statistic values from most to least extreme, counting all ties as extreme. At most an $\alpha$ fraction of assignments can have a tail fraction at most $\alpha$. This is a special case of the general randomization argument in [Hemerik and Goeman (2020)](#references), §3.2, Theorem 3.

Without randomized treatment, an ordinary permutation test needs a null distribution invariant under the allowed label permutations, such as iid observations from the same distribution. Equal means alone do not imply that invariance when spreads differ. In a paired design, unrestricted shuffling across people destroys the design; any within-pair swaps must be justified by its randomization or symmetry assumptions. Preserve the actual assignment restrictions when constructing the reference set.

## What changed

The uncertainty calculation now belongs to a complete decision procedure: statistic, tail, stopping rule, family of comparisons and reference distribution. Altering any of these after inspecting the data can change the error rate. A small number on the final screen cannot recover the calibration of a different procedure.

## Limitations

The exact binomial and randomization calculations are exact only for their stated models and designs. The A/B z-test uses a normal approximation. None of these procedures corrects outcome leakage or unrepresentative samples. A statistically detectable effect also needs a decision criterion that accounts for its size, costs and consequences.

## What you should remember

- Choose the null, direction and analysis rule before inspecting the outcome.
- A valid p-value controls rejection probabilities under the null.
- Power depends on the alternative and sample size.
- Repeated looks and multiple comparisons require joint error control.
- Permutations must respect the reason labels are exchangeable.

## Code

The standard library can enumerate both small reference distributions exactly. The binomial calculation prints 0.019287 and 0.390675; the complete randomization calculation prints 0.100000.

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import combinations
from math import comb


def upper_tail(n, cutoff, p):
    return sum(Fraction(comb(n, k)) * p**k * (1-p)**(n-k)
               for k in range(cutoff, n+1))

print(f"Null rejection: {float(upper_tail(12, 10, Fraction(1, 2))):.6f}")
print(f"Power at .75: {float(upper_tail(12, 10, Fraction(3, 4))):.6f}")
y = [0, 1, 2, 4, 7, 9]
observed = Fraction(17, 3)
contrasts = [Fraction(2 * sum(y[i] for i in group) - sum(y), 3)
             for group in combinations(range(6), 3)]
p_value = Fraction(sum(abs(value) >= observed for value in contrasts), len(contrasts))
print(f"Randomization p: {float(p_value):.6f}")
```

```text
Null rejection: 0.019287
Power at .75: 0.390675
Randomization p: 0.100000
```
