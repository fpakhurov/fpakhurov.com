The [law of large numbers](/notes/probability/law-of-large-numbers) says that an average approaches its expectation. It leaves a practical gap: how should we describe the remaining error at a large but finite sample size? The central limit theorem changes the scale of that error and gives a limiting shape. Using that shape at a particular sample size remains an approximation.

## The theorem

Let $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ be iid, with mean $\mu$ and variance $0<\sigma^2<\infty$. For $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$ and $\bar X_n=S_n/n$, define

$$
\begin{aligned}
Z_n&=\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\\
&=\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Then $Z_n\Rightarrow N(0,1)$. The arrow means convergence in distribution: $P(Z_n\leq z)\to\Phi(z)$ for every real $z$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. Every real point is a continuity point of that CDF. The observation distribution itself does not become normal, nor does an unscaled sum have a fixed limiting distribution.

The scaling has two jobs. Subtracting $n\mu$ removes the growing center of the sum. Dividing by $\sigma\sqrt n$ makes its variance equal to $1$. Dividing by $n$ instead would make its variance vanish and recover the law of large numbers. Unit variance alone does not prove a normal limit; independence and the finite-variance argument below do the remaining work.

Reuse the original law from the previous note: values $(-1,2,4)$ have probabilities $(1/2,1/3,1/6)$, giving $\mu=5/6$ and $\sigma^2=137/36$. Its sum remains discrete at every finite size. The exact CDFs below come from integer convolution of this law.

It improves this example but is not a universal error guarantee. A density curve does not reproduce the sum's point masses.

## Why the normal

A characteristic function, $\varphi_Y(t)=E[e^{itY}]$, is defined even when a moment-generating function is not. Let $Y_i=(X_i-\mu)/\sigma$, so $EY_i=0$ and $EY_i^2=1$. A finite second moment gives the expansion

$$
\varphi_Y(u)=1-\frac{u^2}{2}+o(u^2),\qquad u\to0.
$$

This does not silently assume a third moment. For the remainder $r(v)=e^{iv}-1-iv+v^2/2$, we have $r(v)/v^2\to0$ and $|r(v)|\leq v^2$ for real $v$. Thus $r(uY)/u^2$ tends to zero pointwise and is bounded in absolute value by $Y^2$. Dominated convergence justifies taking expectations; this is the finite-second-moment expansion in Durrett's Theorem 3.3.20.

Independence turns the characteristic function of a sum into a product. For fixed $t$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi_{Z_n}(t)&=\left[\varphi_Y\!\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt n}\right)\right]^n\\
&=\left[1-\frac{t^2}{2n}+o(n^{-1})\right]^n\\
&\longrightarrow e^{-t^2/2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last function is the characteristic function of $N(0,1)$. The continuity theorem converts its pointwise convergence, with continuity at zero, into convergence in distribution. This is the step that connects the algebra to probabilities; matching just a mean and variance would not suffice. The argument establishes a limit, not a numerical rate at a chosen sample size.

## Error bars

For this mathematical example the population variance is known. Define the interval with random center $\bar X_n$ and deterministic half-width $1.96\sigma/\sqrt n$:

$$
C_n=\left[\bar X_n-1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\ \bar X_n+1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\right].
$$

The event $\mu\in C_n$ is exactly $|Z_n|\leq1.96$. Therefore its probability approaches $\Phi(1.96)-\Phi(-1.96)\approx0.950004$. Coverage describes repeated samples from the stipulated distribution: the fixed mean lies in a random interval. It is not a posterior probability assigned to the mean after observing one sample.

We can check finite-sample coverage exactly for the same original law:

| $n$ | Standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$ | Half-width | Exact coverage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $4$ | $0.975392$ | $1.911768$ | $0.965278$ |
| $16$ | $0.487696$ | $0.955884$ | $0.945030$ |
| $64$ | $0.243848$ | $0.477942$ | $0.949531$ |

Coverage can fall on either side of its limiting value and need not approach it monotonically. At size $16$, the coverage calculation includes integer sums from $-1$ through $28$; the endpoints follow from the interval inequality, not from rounding the observed mean. Quadrupling the sample size halves the standard error, while changing the discrete law's coverage in a separate way.

When the population variance is unknown, replacing it by a consistent variance estimate needs an additional argument. Exact Student intervals require normal observations; the iid finite-variance CLT alone does not make a finite-sample Student interval exact. Estimating variance and interpreting confidence intervals have their own place later in the course.

## How fast

A convergence statement does not provide a universal sample size at which the approximation becomes accurate. One useful extra assumption is $\rho=E|X_1-\mu|^3<\infty$. The Berry–Esseen inequality bounds the largest absolute CDF error:

$$
\sup_z|P(Z_n\leq z)-\Phi(z)|\leq\frac{C\rho}{\sigma^3\sqrt n}.
$$

Here $C$ is a universal constant. Durrett's Theorem 3.4.17 supplies the valid but loose choice $C=3$; it is not a claim about the best known constant. A larger standardized third absolute moment means this bound requires more observations for the same error tolerance.

For our law, $\rho=641/72$ and $\rho/\sigma^3\approx1.199219$. The exact largest CDF discrepancy is obtained by checking both sides of every jump. Checking only right-continuous CDF values can miss the worst discrepancy just before a mass.

| $n$ | Exact largest CDF discrepancy | Bound with $C=3$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $0.326338$ | $3.597656$ |
| $4$ | $0.112895$ | $1.798828$ |
| $16$ | $0.035719$ | $0.899414$ |
| $64$ | $0.015869$ | $0.449707$ |

Bounds above $1$ are uninformative and can be capped at $1$. These bounds are much larger than the actual errors here. The theorem controls absolute error in a CDF; it does not promise a small relative error for a very rare tail event. Infinite third moment invalidates this particular bound without invalidating a finite-variance CLT.

## Modes of convergence

Keep three meanings of an arrow separate. Almost-sure convergence follows individual sequences outside a null set. Convergence in probability makes fixed-size deviations unlikely. Convergence in distribution compares marginal CDFs at the limit CDF's continuity points.

$$
X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}X\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_n\xrightarrow{P}X\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X_n\Rightarrow X.
$$

For the first implication, the indicator of $|X_n-X|>\varepsilon$ tends to zero almost surely and is bounded by $1$; dominated convergence makes its expectation vanish. For the second, compare $P(X_n\leq x)$ to $P(X\leq x-\varepsilon)$ and $P(X\leq x+\varepsilon)$, with an error of at most $P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)$. Take limits at a continuity point and then shrink $\varepsilon$.

The reverse implications fail in general. The [independent-event construction](/notes/probability/law-of-large-numbers#the-borel-cantelli-lemmas) already separated probability from almost-sure convergence. To separate distribution from probability, let $Z$ be uniform on $\{-3,3\}$ and set $X_n=(-1)^nZ$. Every $X_n$ has exactly the distribution of $Z$, but for odd $n$ we have $|X_n-Z|=6$. Thus $P(|X_n-Z|>1)$ alternates between $1$ and $0$, so it cannot tend to zero.

There is an important exception: if $X_n\Rightarrow c$ for a deterministic constant $c$, then $X_n\xrightarrow{P}c$. The CDF converges at points just below and above that constant, forcing the probability outside any fixed neighborhood to vanish. This is why a limiting point mass can express the same consistency as a weak law, while a nondegenerate normal limit describes a different scale of fluctuation.

## When the theorem fails

If all observations equal one random variable with finite positive variance, their covariance terms do not vanish and the independent-sum proof breaks. If variance is infinite, the normalization and the second-order expansion used here are unavailable. Cauchy averages, discussed in the previous note, retain the same distribution instead of concentrating. Other limits and other normalizations can exist; this theorem does not cover them.

For nonidentically distributed independent terms, one must also control whether a few terms dominate the total variance. Merely having many summands does not supply that control. Conversely, some dependent sequences do have central limit theorems under suitable dependence assumptions; an observed normal-looking histogram does not verify those assumptions.

## What changed

The law of large numbers collapsed an average toward a constant. The CLT magnified its centered error by a square-root scale and identified a distributional limit. Exact convolution then separated the limit approximation, a continuity correction, finite-sample coverage and a rigorous but loose error bound.

## Limitations

A good central approximation need not estimate rare-event probabilities well. Known-variance error bars here are not a recipe for correlated data or an estimated variance without further justification. The exact numerical discrepancies belong to our specified finite law, not to every distribution with the same moments.

## What you should remember

* Standardization centers the sum and fixes its variance; the iid finite-variance theorem supplies the normal limit.
* Distributional convergence does not make individual trajectories approach a normal random variable on the same probability space.
* Continuity corrections and asymptotic intervals are approximations whose finite-sample behavior can be checked separately.
* Berry–Esseen adds a finite third absolute moment and gives an absolute CDF bound, not a universal sample-size rule or relative-tail guarantee.
