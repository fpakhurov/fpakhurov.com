Two successes among sixteen observations give an estimated probability of 0.125. A symmetric error bar built from that estimate extends below zero. Clipping the endpoint makes the range look plausible, but leaves a more important question: how often does the construction contain the true probability when we collect a new sample?

An interval needs a sampling rule and a target. We will distinguish uncertainty about a population mean from variation among individual observations, and condition every guarantee on the model used to calculate it.

## What 95% means

Let $X$ denote the complete random sample, $\theta$ a fixed population parameter, and $C(X)$ an interval calculated by a predetermined procedure. For $0<\alpha<1$, a confidence level of $1-\alpha$ requires the coverage inequality

$$
\Pr_\theta\{\theta\in C(X)\}\geq1-\alpha
$$

for every parameter value allowed by the model. The interval changes with the sample. A realized interval either contains the fixed parameter or misses it. Its coverage statement concerns repeated sampling under the stated assumptions ([Neyman, 1937](#references), §II(a)).

A 95% procedure does not promise exactly nineteen successes in every twenty repetitions. If independent repetitions all have coverage 0.95, their coverage indicators have mean 0.95; their observed proportion approaches this value by the law of large numbers. A Bayesian credible interval instead assigns posterior probability to a parameter under a specified likelihood and prior. The endpoints can sometimes coincide, but the probability statements use different random objects.

Coverage alone is insufficient for usefulness. The whole real line always covers a real parameter. We also need an interval narrow enough to resolve the effect of interest. Increasing the sample can reduce sampling uncertainty; it cannot remove selection bias or repair a wrong definition of the target population.

## Intervals from the normal approximation

Suppose independent observations follow $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, with known positive $\sigma$. The mean is exactly normal. Standardizing produces a **pivot** whose distribution does not depend on the unknown mean:

$$
Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim\mathcal N(0,1).
$$

Write $z=z_{1-\alpha/2}$ for the standard-normal quantile with cumulative probability $1-\alpha/2$. Solving the event $-z\leq Z\leq z$ for $\mu$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
h&=z\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n},\\
C(X)&=[\bar X-h,\bar X+h].
\end{aligned}
$$

This is an exact normal-model interval. For general iid observations with finite positive variance, the central limit theorem motivates an approximate interval at large $n$. A finite sample does not acquire exact Gaussian tails merely because we substitute an estimated standard error.

In an illustrative known-noise setting, the observed mean is 2.8, $n=25$, and $\sigma=1.5$. The standard error is 0.3. Using $z\approx1.959964$, the 95% interval is $[2.2120,3.3880]$. These bounds concern the mean; a future individual measurement has much more variation than an average of twenty-five measurements.

When $n\geq2$ normal observations have unknown variance, use the sample variance $S^2$ with divisor $n-1$. Replacing $\sigma$ by $S$ adds randomness to the denominator. Under the normal model,

$$
T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}.
$$

The heavier tails account for this uncertainty ([Student, 1908](#references), §III). To see the structure, rotate the standardized Gaussian sample into its mean direction and $n-1$ perpendicular residual directions. The coordinates remain independent standard normals. Their residual squared length has a chi-squared distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom and is independent of the mean coordinate. The quotient of that coordinate by the square root of the residual squared length divided by $n-1$ defines the Student distribution.

For hypothetical normal measurements $6,7,9,10,13$, the calculation is:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Sample mean | 9 |
| Sample variance | 7.5 |
| Standard error | 1.224745 |
| 97.5% quantile of $t_4$ | 2.776445 |
| 95% interval | $[5.5996,12.4004]$ |

The larger critical value matters with only five observations. This small-sample guarantee relies on normality. Using a Student quantile does not make a severely skewed five-point sample behave like a normal sample.

For Bernoulli observations, the sample proportion is $\hat p=K/n$. The plug-in **Wald interval** uses

$$
\hat p\ \pm\ z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}.
$$

At $K=0$, it has zero width. The failure comes from treating an observed absence as evidence of no sampling variation. A **Wilson interval** keeps the candidate probability $p$ in the standard error and retains candidates satisfying

$$
(\hat p-p)^2\leq\frac{z^2p(1-p)}{n}.
$$

Let $a=z^2/n$. Expanding and collecting powers of $p$ yields

$$
(1+a)p^2-(2\hat p+a)p+\hat p^2\leq0.
$$

The retained values lie between the quadratic's roots. Their center $c$ and half-width $h$ are

$$
\begin{aligned}
c&=\frac{\hat p+a/2}{1+a},\\
h&=\frac{z}{1+a}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}+\frac{z^2}{4n^2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the score-inversion construction of [Wilson (1927)](#references), pp. 209–211. The normal critical value still makes it approximate. For $K=2,n=16$, Wald gives $[-0.0370,0.2870]$ and Wilson gives $[0.0350,0.3602]$.

We can check coverage without simulation. At a fixed true probability $p$, each possible count has a binomial probability. Add the probabilities of counts whose intervals cover $p$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_k(p)&=\binom{16}{k}p^k(1-p)^{16-k},\\
\Pr_p\{p\in C(K)\}
&=\sum_{k=0}^{16}\mathbf1\{p\in C(k)\}b_k(p).
\end{aligned}
$$

At $p=0.1$, the exact sums are 0.8114 for Wald and 0.9316 for Wilson. Wilson improves this example substantially, but still falls below the nominal 0.95. Intersecting a Wald interval with $[0,1]$ does not change whether it contains a valid $p$; cosmetic clipping cannot fix coverage.

## The bootstrap

A complicated statistic may lack a convenient sampling formula. The **nonparametric bootstrap** replaces the unknown population distribution by the empirical distribution, which assigns mass $1/n$ to each observed row. It then draws $n$ rows independently with replacement and recalculates the statistic ([Efron, 1979](#references), §2).

Conditionally on the observed sample, the resampling distribution is completely specified. Repeated appearances of a row are allowed. Drawing every row once in a different order would leave a sample mean unchanged and would not estimate its sampling variation.

For the newly chosen values $1,2,4,8$, there are $4^4=256$ equally likely ordered bootstrap samples. Their means form a finite distribution with mean 3.75. Each resampled observation has the empirical variance, so independence gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}_*(X^*)&=\frac14\sum_{i=1}^4(x_i-3.75)^2\\
&=7.1875,\\
\operatorname{Var}_*(\bar X^*)&=\frac{7.1875}{4}\\
&=1.796875.
\end{aligned}
$$

The star means that probability is conditional on the fixed observed values. The bootstrap standard error is 1.340476. It uses the variance of the empirical distribution, with divisor $n$; the familiar estimated variance of the population uses divisor $n-1$. Their difference is visible with four observations.

A **percentile interval** takes the lower and upper quantiles of the bootstrap statistic. Define a quantile as the smallest value where cumulative probability reaches its requested level. Enumerating all 256 samples gives 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of 1.5 and 6.5. The resulting interval is $[1.5,6.5]$. This exact enumeration removes numerical resampling error. It does not establish 95% population coverage for such a small dataset.

If enumeration is infeasible, generate $B$ independent bootstrap samples. Increasing $B$ improves the numerical approximation to the distribution implied by the observed sample. Increasing the original sample size $n$ supplies more information about the population. The two operations address different uncertainties. Bias and asymmetry can make a simple percentile interval unreliable even when $B$ is very large.

## When the bootstrap fails

Consider twelve independent Bernoulli observations with true success probability 0.08. The chance of seeing only zeros is

$$
\Pr\{K=0\}=0.92^{12}\approx0.367666.
$$

Conditional on that observed sample, every bootstrap mean is zero. Its percentile interval is $[0,0]$, which excludes 0.08. More resamples cannot create a success absent from the empirical distribution. This is a concrete small-sample support problem; a reported zero standard error need not imply knowledge of the true parameter.

The resampling unit must also match independence. Repeated measurements from one person cannot be treated as unrelated people. A suitable cluster bootstrap resamples independent clusters together; a time series needs a design that preserves relevant serial dependence. The choice of cluster or block requires assumptions and enough independent units. Arbitrary grouping does not produce a guarantee.

## Comparing two models on one test set

When two fixed models predict the same iid test cases, their losses are paired. Let $D_i=L_{A,i}-L_{B,i}$; positive values favor model B. The target is the expected loss difference on a new case from the same population. Its estimator and standard error are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar D&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nD_i,\\
s_D^2&=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(D_i-\bar D)^2,\\
\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\bar D)&=\frac{s_D}{\sqrt n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For twelve fictional cases, take three differences of $+1$, one of $-1$, and eight zeros. The mean difference is $1/6$, the sample variance is $1/3$, and the standard error is $1/6$. A normal-approximation interval is about $[-0.1600,0.4933]$. With only twelve discrete differences, this is a rough uncertainty summary, not an exact 95% guarantee.

Pairing matters because

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(L_A-L_B)
&=\operatorname{Var}(L_A)+\operatorname{Var}(L_B)\\
&\quad-2\operatorname{Cov}(L_A,L_B).
\end{aligned}
$$

Resample complete rows containing both losses, or resample their differences. Resampling the models' losses separately discards covariance and answers a different sampling question. The interval is conditional on the fitted models: it omits variation from retraining. Selecting the models after inspecting this same test set introduces another source of optimism that row resampling does not undo.

## What changed

A point estimate becomes a procedure with a measurable error rate. A pivot derives the interval from a known distribution; score inversion changes which parameter values remain compatible with an approximate pivot; the bootstrap estimates sampling variation using the empirical distribution. Each route needs its own calibration check.

## Limitations

All intervals here assume that the observations or resampling units represent the target population. Neither narrow bounds nor large samples compensate for systematic missingness, leakage or distribution shift. The normal and percentile approximations can be inaccurate in small samples, near parameter boundaries or with unstable statistics. Reporting the method, target, sample size and sampling unit makes the uncertainty interpretable.

## What you should remember

- Coverage is a property of the complete interval-building procedure.
- Exact normal and Student intervals require their stated distributional assumptions.
- A plausible-looking endpoint does not establish nominal coverage.
- Bootstrap precision cannot exceed the information in the observed sample.
- Paired comparisons retain the joint row structure.

## Code

This small standard-library calculation enumerates the complete bootstrap mean distribution and checks the paired standard error. The printed values are 1.340476, $[1.5,6.5]$, and 0.166667.

```python
from collections import Counter
from itertools import product
from math import sqrt

x = [1, 2, 4, 8]
counts = Counter(sum(row) / 4 for row in product(x, repeat=4))
mean = sum(value * count for value, count in counts.items()) / 256
variance = sum((value - mean)**2 * count for value, count in counts.items()) / 256

def quantile(level):
    cumulative = 0
    for value, count in sorted(counts.items()):
        cumulative += count
        if cumulative / 256 >= level:
            return value

print(f"Bootstrap SE: {sqrt(variance):.6f}")
print("Percentile interval:", [quantile(.025), quantile(.975)])
d = [1, 1, 1, -1] + [0] * 8
average = sum(d) / len(d)
s2 = sum((value - average)**2 for value in d) / (len(d) - 1)
print(f"Paired SE: {sqrt(s2 / len(d)):.6f}")
```

```text
Bootstrap SE: 1.340476
Percentile interval: [1.5, 6.5]
Paired SE: 0.166667
```
