Probability goes from a known distribution to the data it produces: given a fair coin, how many heads? Statistics goes the other way: given the heads, what is the coin? The unknown is a **parameter** $\theta$ of a family of distributions, and a rule that turns data into a guess is an **estimator** $\hat\theta$. Every number fitted in machine learning, from a mean to the weights of a network, is an estimator. This note introduces the vocabulary that judges them: bias, variance, mean squared error, consistency and the standard error, and ends with the bias–variance trade-off that governs the choice of a model's complexity.

**Notation.** $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are i.i.d. from a distribution $P_\theta$ with unknown parameter $\theta$. An estimator $\hat\theta = \hat\theta(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ is a function of the data. $E_\theta$ and $\operatorname{Var}_\theta$ are computed under $P_\theta$.

## Estimators

The sample mean $\bar X$ estimates the mean, the share of successes estimates a probability, the sample variance estimates the variance, a fitted regression line estimates the regression function of the [conditional expectation note](/notes/probability/conditional-expectation). Because the data are random, an estimator is a **random variable**: a different sample gives a different value. Its distribution over repeated samples, the **sampling distribution**, is what makes it good or bad. The average of $n$ observations, for instance, has sampling distribution approximately $N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ by the [central limit theorem](/notes/probability/central-limit-theorem).

The same parameter usually has many estimators. For a sample from $U(0, \theta)$, the end point $\theta$ can be estimated by twice the sample mean, since $E[X] = \theta/2$, or by the sample maximum, the largest value seen, or by the maximum scaled up slightly. They behave very differently.

## Bias, variance and mean squared error

The **bias** is the systematic error, the **variance** the random one:

$$
\operatorname{bias}(\hat\theta) = E_\theta[\hat\theta] - \theta, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) = E_\theta\big[(\hat\theta - E_\theta[\hat\theta])^2\big] .
$$

The **mean squared error** combines them. Adding and subtracting $E_\theta[\hat\theta]$ inside the square, the cross term has mean zero:

$$
\operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) = E_\theta\big[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2\big] = \operatorname{bias}(\hat\theta)^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) .
$$

For the uniform end point with $n = 10$ and $\theta = 1$:

Unbiasedness is not the goal. The maximum is always too small, yet its mean squared error is less than half that of the unbiased $2\bar X$, because the maximum uses the one observation that carries the most information about the end point. Correcting its bias keeps that advantage and gives the best of the three; its variance even falls like $1/n^2$, faster than the $1/n$ of any average.

## The sample variance and n − 1 \{#the-sample-variance-and-n-1\}

The natural estimator of $\sigma^2$ averages the squared deviations from the sample mean. It is biased:

$$
E\Big[\frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2\Big] = \frac{n-1}{n}\, \sigma^2 ,
$$

because the deviations are measured from $\bar X$, which is fitted to the same data and so sits closer to them than the true mean $\mu$ does. Dividing by $n - 1$ instead (**Bessel's correction**) removes the bias; this $s^2$ is what statistical software reports. But for normal data the mean squared error is smallest with yet another divisor, $n + 1$: for $n = 10$ the divisors $9$, $10$ and $11$ give mean squared errors $0.222\sigma^4$, $0.190\sigma^4$ and $0.182\sigma^4$. Again the unbiased choice is not the most accurate one, though for moderate $n$ the differences are small.

## Consistency

An estimator is **consistent** if it converges in probability to the true value as $n$ grows: $\hat\theta_n \to \theta$. A sufficient condition is that both the bias and the variance go to zero, since then the mean squared error does. The sample mean is consistent by the [law of large numbers](/notes/probability/law-of-large-numbers). The maximum of a uniform sample is biased for every $n$ and still consistent. The first observation alone, $\hat\theta = X_1$, is unbiased for the mean and not consistent: it ignores all the other data. Consistency is the minimum requirement; bias, variance and their rates are what distinguish consistent estimators.

## Standard errors

The standard deviation of an estimator is its **standard error**, the size of a typical deviation from the truth. For the sample mean it is $\sigma/\sqrt n$, estimated by $s/\sqrt n$. Quoting an estimate without its standard error says nothing about its precision; with it, the central limit theorem gives an approximate interval, the subject of the [confidence intervals note](/notes/probability/confidence-intervals-and-bootstrap). For estimators without a simple formula, the standard error can be estimated by resampling the data, the bootstrap of the same note.

## The bias–variance trade-off \{#the-bias-variance-trade-off\}

A trained model $\hat f$ is an estimator of the regression function $m(x) = E[Y \mid X = x]$, and its sampling distribution comes from the randomness of the training set. At a point $x$, with a new $Y$ independent of the training data, the expected squared error splits in three:

$$
E\big[(Y - \hat f(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X = x)}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\big(E[\hat f(x)] - m(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big(\hat f(x)\big)}_{\text{variance}} .
$$

The noise is the irreducible error of the conditional expectation note. A model class too simple for $m$ has high bias: every training set gives about the same wrong answer. A class flexible enough to follow $m$ also follows the noise of each training set and has high variance:

With 12 training points, degree 1 has total expected error $0.218 + 0.014 + 0.09 = 0.32$, degree 3 has $0.006 + 0.026 + 0.09 = 0.12$, degree 9 has $0.000 + 0.116 + 0.09 = 0.21$. The best complexity balances the two, and it depends on the amount of data: more data lowers the variance of every class and moves the balance toward more flexible models. Regularization (ridge, weight decay, early stopping, dropout) is a deliberate trade of a little bias for a large cut in variance, the same trade that made the uniform maximum beat $2\bar X$. In high dimensions it is unavoidable: the James–Stein estimator shows that, for three or more normal means, shrinking all sample means toward a common point lowers the total mean squared error for every value of the true means.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Known | the distribution, $\theta$ | the data |
| Unknown | the data | $\theta$ |
| Object of study | a random variable | an estimator, a random variable through the data |
| Quality | | bias, variance, $\operatorname{MSE} = \operatorname{bias}^2 + \operatorname{variance}$, consistency, standard error |
| For a trained model | | noise + bias² + variance; complexity and regularization set the balance |

## Limitations

- **Bias and MSE depend on the unknown $\theta$.** An estimator can be better for some values of the parameter and worse for others; comparing them needs a criterion such as the worst case or an average over a prior.
- **Mean squared error is one loss among many.** Absolute error, coverage of intervals or the cost of a wrong decision can rank estimators differently.
- **The decomposition for models is pointwise and over training sets.** In practice there is one training set; bias and variance are estimated by resampling or by validation curves, not observed directly.

## What you should remember

- An estimator is a function of the data and so a random variable; its sampling distribution decides its quality.
- $\operatorname{MSE} = \operatorname{bias}^2 + \operatorname{variance}$; a biased estimator can have a smaller MSE than an unbiased one.
- The sample variance with $1/n$ underestimates $\sigma^2$ by the factor $(n-1)/n$; $1/(n-1)$ removes the bias.
- Consistent: $\hat\theta_n \to \theta$ in probability; vanishing bias and variance suffice.
- The standard error is the standard deviation of the estimator; for a mean, $\sigma/\sqrt n$.
- Expected prediction error = noise + bias² + variance; flexibility lowers bias and raises variance; regularization trades bias for variance.

## Code

The uniform estimators, the divisors of the sample variance and the polynomial fits, by simulation:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# three estimators of theta = 1 from n = 10 draws of U(0, theta): bias, variance, MSE over 200,000 samples
n = 10
X = rng.random((200_000, n))
for name, est in [('2 x mean', 2 * X.mean(axis=1)), ('max', X.max(axis=1)), ('max x 11/10', X.max(axis=1) * (n + 1) / n)]:
    print(name, round(float(est.mean() - 1), 3), round(float(est.var()), 4), round(float(((est - 1) ** 2).mean()), 4))
# 2 x mean 0.0 0.0333 0.0333 | max -0.091 0.0069 0.0151 | max x 11/10 0.0 0.0083 0.0083

# the sample variance of 10 standard normals with divisors n - 1, n, n + 1: bias and MSE
Z = rng.normal(size=(200_000, n))
ss = ((Z - Z.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)) ** 2).sum(axis=1)
for d in (n - 1, n, n + 1):
    print(d, round(float((ss / d).mean() - 1), 3), round(float(((ss / d - 1) ** 2).mean()), 3))
# 9 -0.001 0.221 | 10 -0.101 0.189 | 11 -0.182 0.181

# squared bias and variance of polynomial fits: 12 points of sin(2 pi x) + N(0, 0.3^2), 2,000 datasets
f = lambda x: np.sin(2 * np.pi * x)
x, grid = np.linspace(0, 1, 12), np.linspace(0, 1, 101)
for deg in (1, 3, 9):
    P = np.array([np.polynomial.polynomial.polyval(2 * grid - 1, np.polynomial.polynomial.polyfit(2 * x - 1, f(x) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=12), deg)) for _ in range(2000)])
    print(deg, round(float(((P.mean(axis=0) - f(grid)) ** 2).mean()), 3), round(float(P.var(axis=0).mean()), 3))
# 1 0.218 0.014 | 3 0.006 0.026 | 9 0.0 0.116
```
