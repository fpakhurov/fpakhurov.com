Seven independent binary observations contain three ones. The observed fraction is about 0.43. A second rule adds two to the count and five to the sample size, giving about 0.42. Both produce plausible probabilities. To choose between them, we need to consider the answers they would give on other samples from the same population.

## Estimators

A **parameter** describes the data-generating distribution. An **estimator** is a rule that maps observations to a proposed parameter value. Before sampling, its input and output are random. After sampling, the output is an **estimate**, a particular number. In the frequentist calculations here, the parameter is fixed; the randomness comes from drawing the observations ([Fan, 2016, Lecture 12](#references)).

Let the independent variables $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ have the same Bernoulli distribution with success probability $p$. Write $K$ for their sum. Define two estimators:

$$
\begin{aligned}
K &= \sum_{i=1}^n X_i,\\
A_n &= \frac{K}{n},\\
B_n &= \frac{K+2}{n+5}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The constants in the second rule are fixed in advance. We can rewrite it as a weighted average of the sample fraction and the fixed target 0.4:

$$
B_n=\frac{n}{n+5}A_n+\frac{5}{n+5}\cdot\frac25.
$$

This operation is called **shrinkage**. It reduces how far the estimate can move when the observed count changes. It also moves the expected estimate towards the chosen target. Neither consequence establishes whether it improves estimation.

For a fully specified comparison, suppose $n=7$ and the true probability is $p=2/7$. The count has eight possible values. Each sequence with $k$ ones has probability $p^k(1-p)^{7-k}$; choosing the positions of those ones gives the binomial mass:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_p(K=k)&=\binom7k p^k(1-p)^{7-k},\\
k&\in\{0,\ldots,7\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying each rule to these eight counts gives its **sampling distribution**. The bars below use exact binomial probabilities. This comparison does not require a large simulation or a normal approximation. The true probability is known only because we specified a model for the comparison; it remains unknown in an actual estimation problem.

## Bias, variance and mean squared error

The center of a sampling distribution can miss the target, and individual estimates can spread around that center. For an estimator $T$ with finite second moment, define:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Bias}_p(T)&=\mathbb E_p[T]-p,\\
\operatorname{MSE}_p(T)&=\mathbb E_p[(T-p)^2].
\end{aligned}
$$

The subscript means that expectations use repeated samples generated at the specified parameter. An estimator is **unbiased** when its expectation equals the target for every allowed parameter value. Being centered correctly at one value is insufficient. Mean squared error assigns a quadratic cost to missing the target ([Fan, 2016, §12.2](#references)).

To separate the two sources of error, let $m=\mathbb E_p[T]$. Expanding the square gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(T-p)^2&=(T-m)^2\\
&\quad+2(T-m)(m-p)\\
&\quad+(m-p)^2,\\
\mathbb E_p[T-m]&=0,\\
\operatorname{MSE}_p(T)&=\operatorname{Var}_p(T)+(m-p)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

The middle term vanishes because its random factor has mean zero. Thus a smaller variance can compensate for a nonzero bias. We must compare their magnitudes under the stated loss.

For Bernoulli observations, expectation adds and independence makes variances add. Therefore $K$ has mean $np$ and variance $np(1-p)$. Scaling and shifting $K$ yields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_p[A_n]&=p,\\
\operatorname{Var}_p(A_n)&=\frac{p(1-p)}n,\\
\operatorname{Bias}_p(B_n)&=\frac{2-5p}{n+5},\\
\operatorname{Var}_p(B_n)&=\frac{np(1-p)}{(n+5)^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

At the chosen probability and sample size, the resulting error budget is:

| Rule | Bias | Variance | MSE |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Sample fraction $A_7$ | 0 | 0.029155 | 0.029155 |
| Shrunk fraction $B_7$ | 0.047619 | 0.009921 | 0.012188 |

The second rule is biased upward here, but its MSE is smaller. This is a statement about repeated samples at $p=2/7$, not a promise that its answer is closer on every sample. For the observed count three, the true parameter is unavailable to decide which particular answer is closer.

## The sample variance and n − 1 \{#the-sample-variance-and-n-1\}

Estimating spread presents another bias question. Suppose now that $X_i$ are iid with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. The distribution need not be Gaussian. If we center the observations at their fitted sample mean $\bar X$, the average squared residual is systematically smaller than the population variance.

The exact identity behind the correction is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)^2\\
&\quad=\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)^2\\
&\quad-n(\bar X-\mu)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

To obtain it, write each centered residual as $(X_i-\mu)-(\bar X-\mu)$, expand, and use the fact that the sum of $X_i-\mu$ is $n(\bar X-\mu)$. The sample mean absorbs part of the observed variation. Taking expectations quantifies the amount:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)^2\right]
&=n\sigma^2-n\operatorname{Var}(\bar X),\\
&=n\sigma^2-\sigma^2,\\
&=(n-1)\sigma^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

We used unbiasedness of the sample mean and its variance $\sigma^2/n$. For $n>1$, division by $n-1$ therefore produces an unbiased estimator of the population variance:

$$
S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)^2.
$$

For the deliberately chosen observations $(-2,1,1,4)$, the calculation is:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | ---: |
| Sample mean | 1 |
| Squared residuals | $9,0,0,9$ |
| Sum of squared residuals | 18 |
| Divide by four | 4.5 |
| Divide by three | 6 |

The correction concerns expectation over samples, so it does not tell us whether 4.5 or 6 is nearer the unknown variance for this particular dataset. Taking the square root also does not preserve unbiasedness: the square root is nonlinear. Unbiased variance estimation and unbiased standard-deviation estimation are different requirements.

## Consistency

An estimator sequence $T_n$ is **consistent** for a parameter $\theta$ when it converges to that parameter in probability. For every fixed tolerance $\varepsilon>0$:

$$
P_\theta(|T_n-\theta|>\varepsilon)\longrightarrow0.
$$

The [law of large numbers](/notes/probability/law-of-large-numbers#the-weak-law) supplies this conclusion for the sample mean under its stated assumptions. Consistency describes growing sample sizes; unbiasedness describes expectation at a given size. They answer different questions ([Fan, 2016, §14.1](#references)).

Our shrunk estimator is consistent for every Bernoulli probability. Its squared bias and variance both tend to zero by the formulas above. Applying Markov's inequality to the nonnegative squared error gives a direct proof:

$$
P_p(|B_n-p|>\varepsilon)
\leq\frac{\operatorname{MSE}_p(B_n)}{\varepsilon^2}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

In contrast, the rule $T_n=X_1$ is unbiased for $p$ for every sample size and ignores every new observation. When $0<p<1$, choose a tolerance smaller than both $p$ and $1-p$. Its error exceeds that tolerance with probability one for every $n$, so it is inconsistent. Increasing the available sample does not help a rule that discards the added information.

## Standard errors

A **standard error** is the standard deviation of an estimator's sampling distribution. It describes how much the estimate changes between repeated samples. It differs from the standard deviation of one observation. For the Bernoulli sample fraction:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{SE}_p(A_n)&=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}n},\\
\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(A_n)&=\sqrt{\frac{A_n(1-A_n)}n}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line replaces the unknown probability by its estimate. It is a **plug-in estimate** of uncertainty, rather than the exact standard error. At $p=2/7$ and $n=7$, the exact standard error is 0.170747. With the observed count three, the plug-in value is 0.187044. Those values differ because the uncertainty estimate also depends on the observed data.

A standard error is not automatically a confidence interval. The [central limit theorem](/notes/probability/central-limit-theorem#the-theorem) can justify a normal approximation for iid Bernoulli observations with fixed interior probability as the sample grows. A sample of seven can remain visibly discrete. In particular, an all-zero sample gives plug-in standard error zero even though a positive probability could have generated that sample. Interval construction must handle that limitation explicitly.

## The bias–variance trade-off \{#the-bias-variance-trade-off\}

Shrinkage towards 0.4 helps most when that target is reasonably close to the truth. Combining the bias and variance formulas gives the complete MSE function:

$$
\operatorname{MSE}_p(B_n)
=\frac{np(1-p)+(2-5p)^2}{(n+5)^2}.
$$

At $p=4/5$ with seven observations, the sample fraction has MSE 0.022857 and the shrunk fraction has MSE 0.035556. Shrinkage still reduces variance, but its bias is now about −0.166667, large enough to reverse the comparison. At $p=0$ or $p=1$, the sample fraction is exact while the fixed shrinkage rule introduces error.

The curves compare rules at each possible truth. They do not let us look up the unknown truth from a single sample. Choosing a shrinkage target requires information or a modeling decision beyond the observed fraction. The fixed five-unit contribution becomes less influential as $n$ grows. At $p=2/7$, the shrunk rule's MSE falls from 0.012188 at seven observations to 0.005547 at 28 and 0.001694 at 112. Both rules are consistent, while their finite-sample risks differ.

## What changed

An observed estimate is now accompanied by a repeated-sampling question. Bias asks where the rule is centered; variance asks how much it fluctuates; MSE combines them under squared loss. We can compare two rules even when both produce valid parameter values, and can derive a correction such as $n-1$ from the quantity it is intended to estimate.

## Limitations

These calculations assume independent observations from the specified common distribution. For correlated observations with finite second moments, expanding the variance of the mean gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)
&=\frac1{n^2}\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(X_i)\\
&\quad+\frac2{n^2}\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j).
\end{aligned}
$$

Repeatedly recording the same Bernoulli draw gives $X_i=X_1$ and variance $p(1-p)$ for the mean, regardless of the number of records. The iid standard error would shrink incorrectly. Similarly, a changing success probability changes the parameter being estimated. A correct formula cannot repair an incorrect sampling model.

MSE itself encodes a choice of loss. If large overestimates have a different cost from equally large underestimates, squared error may not represent the decision. An estimator can be preferable under one loss and worse under another.

## What you should remember

- Evaluate a rule over possible samples, with the parameter held fixed.
- MSE equals variance plus squared bias when the second moment exists.
- The $n-1$ correction follows from fitting the sample mean.
- Consistency and unbiasedness impose different requirements.
- A plug-in standard error can be unreliable on small or dependent samples.

## Code

The following standard-library calculation enumerates every possible count in the seven-trial example. The weights sum to one. The displayed MSE values above come from the same exact rational calculation; the decimal conversion happens only at output.

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import comb

n, p = 7, F(2, 7)
weights = [comb(n, k) * p**k * (1-p)**(n-k)
           for k in range(n+1)]
for values in ([F(k, n) for k in range(n+1)],
               [F(k+2, n+5) for k in range(n+1)]):
    mse = sum(w * (t-p)**2 for w, t in zip(weights, values))
    print(f"{float(mse):.6f}")
```

```text
0.029155
0.012188
```
