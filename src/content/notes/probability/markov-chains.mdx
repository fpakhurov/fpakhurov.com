Our sampling-based guarantees so far mostly assumed independent draws. Many random processes are not like that, and their dependence unfolds in time: tomorrow's weather depends on today's, the next word on the previous ones, the next page a reader visits on the current page, the next state of a sampler on the current state. A **Markov chain** is the simplest model of such dependence: the future depends on the past only through the present. It is simple enough to analyse completely and rich enough to describe n-gram language models, web search and the samplers of the last two notes of the course. With actions and rewards added, it becomes the [Markov decision process](/notes/agents/markov-decision-processes#markov-decision-processes) of reinforcement learning.

**Notation.** $X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots$ take values in a finite set of states $\{1, \dots, K\}$. $P$ is the $K \times K$ **transition matrix**, $P_{ij} = P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i)$; each row sums to 1. A distribution over states is a row vector $\pi$.

## The Markov property

A sequence is a Markov chain if

$$
P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i, X_{t-1}, \dots, X_0) = P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i) = P_{ij} .
$$

The first equality is the **Markov property**: the present state contains everything about the past that matters for the future. The second is a separate assumption, **time homogeneity**: the transition rule $P_{ij}$ is the same at every time $t$. All chains in this note are time-homogeneous, and all are finite except one example with a density and the [last subsection](#beyond-finite-chains) of the next section. The reverse process that generates samples in a diffusion model is Markov but not homogeneous, since its transition rule changes from step to step ([MCMC note](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#langevin-dynamics)). A three-state weather model, with sunny days followed by sunny days 70% of the time, is a Markov chain with

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

(rows and columns: sunny, cloudy, rainy). By the law of total probability, the distribution of the next state is the current distribution times the matrix:

$$
\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t P, \qquad \pi_t = \pi_0 P^t .
$$

The entry $(P^t)_{ij}$ is the probability of being in $j$ after $t$ steps from $i$. On a continuous state space the matrix becomes a **transition density** $K(y \mid x)$, the density of $X_{t+1}$ at $y$ given $X_t = x$. If $X_t$ has density $p_t$, the law of total probability, with an integral over $x$ in place of the sum over $i$, gives

$$
p_{t+1}(y) = \int K(y \mid x)\, p_t(x)\, dx .
$$

For example, $X_{t+1} = a X_t + \sqrt{1 - a^2}\, \varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t \sim N(0, 1)$ independent of the past has $K(y \mid x) = N(y;\, a x,\, 1 - a^2)$. If $X_t \sim N(m, v)$, then $X_{t+1}$ is a sum of two independent normal variables, so it is normal with mean $a m$ and variance $a^2 v + 1 - a^2$. With $a = 0.7$ and $X_0 = 3$ the first three steps give $N(2.1, 0.51)$, $N(1.47, 0.76)$ and $N(1.029, 0.882)$.

## Stationary distributions

A distribution $\pi$ with

$$
\pi P = \pi
$$

is **stationary**: if the chain starts in it, it stays in it. It is a left eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue 1, normalized to sum to 1, and it can be found by solving the linear system $\pi P = \pi$, $\sum_i \pi_i = 1$. For the weather chain $\pi = (21/46, 13/46, 12/46) = (0.457, 0.283, 0.261)$: in the long run 46% of days are sunny.

With a transition density the same condition reads

$$
\pi(y) = \int K(y \mid x)\, \pi(x)\, dx .
$$

For the normal chain above, $N(m, v)$ maps to $N(a m, a^2 v + 1 - a^2)$. It is stationary when $m = a m$ and $v = a^2 v + 1 - a^2$, which for $\lvert a \rvert < 1$ means $m = 0$ and $v = 1$: the chain keeps $N(0, 1)$.

Stationarity is a statement about the distribution of each $X_t$, not about their dependence. In the stationary normal chain every $X_t$ is $N(0, 1)$, yet $\operatorname{Corr}(X_t, X_{t+1}) = a$. In the weather chain started from $\pi$, a sunny day has probability $0.457$, but a sunny day after a sunny day has probability $0.7$. Samples from a stationary chain are correlated, and the [effective sample size](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#mixing-and-diagnostics) measures what this costs.

The figure suggests more: from a sunny start the distribution approaches $\pi$ within ten days. That holds for every starting distribution under two conditions:

- **Irreducible**: every state can be reached from every other.
- **Aperiodic**: the chain does not cycle with a fixed period. A chain that alternates deterministically between two states has the stationary distribution $(1/2, 1/2)$ but never approaches it from a fixed start.

The **period** of a state $i$ is

$$
d(i) = \gcd\{\, n \ge 1 : (P^n)_{ii} > 0 \,\} ,
$$

the greatest common divisor of the times at which a return to $i$ is possible. In an irreducible chain all states have the same period ([Levin, Peres and Wilmer](#references), Lemma 1.6), and the chain is aperiodic when that period is 1. The alternating chain returns only at times $2, 4, 6, \dots$, so its period is 2.

A single self-loop is enough. If $P_{ii} > 0$, then $1$ belongs to the set of return times, the gcd is 1, and by the lemma the whole irreducible chain is aperiodic. The weather chain has $P_{ii} > 0$ on the whole diagonal. Positive entries everywhere are more than needed. The chain $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$ with an added self-loop $P_{11} = P_{12} = 1/2$ has five zero entries, yet it is irreducible and aperiodic. Solving $\pi P = \pi$ gives $\pi_2 = \pi_1 / 2$, $\pi_3 = \pi_2$ and $\pi = (1/2, 1/4, 1/4)$, and $P^4$ already has all entries positive. Every finite irreducible aperiodic chain has some power with all entries positive (Proposition 1.7 of the same book).

A finite irreducible chain has exactly one stationary distribution ([Levin, Peres and Wilmer](#references), Corollary 1.17). If it is also aperiodic, $\pi_t \to \pi$ from any start, at a geometric rate (the convergence theorem, Theorem 4.9 there). The intuition is coupling: run a second copy of the chain from $\pi$. Because some power of $P$ has all entries positive, the two copies meet with positive probability in every block of that many steps, so they eventually meet. After that they can move together, and the first copy is then distributed as $\pi$.

The **ergodic theorem**, the law of large numbers for Markov chains, concerns a single long run. For every function $f$ on the states and every start,

$$
\frac1n \sum_{t=0}^{n-1} f(X_t) \to E_\pi f = \sum_i f(i)\, \pi_i
$$

with probability 1 (Theorem C.1 of the same book). With $f$ the indicator of a state, the share of time spent in that state converges to its stationary probability. On a finite space every $f$ has a finite mean; on infinite spaces the theorem needs $E_\pi \lvert f \rvert < \infty$. The intuition: the returns to a fixed state cut the path into excursions that, by the Markov property, are independent and identically distributed, and the ordinary law of large numbers applies to them.

The ergodic theorem needs only irreducibility. The alternating two-state chain started in state 1 visits $1, 2, 1, 2, \dots$, so the share of time in state 1 tends to $1/2 = \pi_1$, while $P(X_t = 1)$ alternates between 1 and 0 and has no limit. Aperiodicity is needed for the distribution of $X_t$, not for time averages; MCMC estimates are time averages.

The speed of convergence of $\pi_t$ is set by the second largest absolute eigenvalue of $P$, as [diagonalizing $P$](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#powers-of-a-markov-transition-matrix) shows: the distance to $\pi$ shrinks roughly like $\lvert \lambda_2 \rvert^t$, here $0.473^t$, so ten steps reduce it by a factor of about 1,800. The number of steps needed to get close is the **mixing time**, the central quantity for the samplers of the [MCMC note](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo).

### Beyond finite chains

On a countably infinite state space irreducibility is defined as before, but it no longer guarantees a stationary distribution. Let $\tau_i^+ = \min\{t \ge 1 : X_t = i\}$ be the return time to $i$. A state is **recurrent** if the chain started there returns with probability 1, and **positive recurrent** if moreover the mean return time $E_i \tau_i^+$ is finite. An irreducible chain on a countable space has a stationary distribution if and only if it is positive recurrent ([Levin, Peres and Wilmer](#references), Theorem 21.13). The distribution is then unique, with

$$
\pi_i = \frac{1}{E_i \tau_i^+}
$$

(Lemma 21.12, Kac), and with aperiodicity $P(X_t = j \mid X_0 = i) \to \pi_j$ from every start (Theorem 21.16). The intuition is the excursion picture: the chain visits $i$ once per excursion, so the long-run share of time at $i$ is one over the mean excursion length. An infinite mean return time leaves every state a share of zero, and nothing remains to normalize.

The symmetric random walk on the integers, $\pm 1$ with probability $1/2$ each, is irreducible and returns to its start with probability 1 (Example 21.4), but it has no stationary distribution. A stationary $\pi$ would satisfy $\pi_i = \frac12 \pi_{i-1} + \frac12 \pi_{i+1}$, so $\pi_{i+1} - \pi_i = \pi_i - \pi_{i-1}$: the differences are a constant $c$ and $\pi_i = \pi_0 + c\, i$. Nonnegativity for all $i \in \mathbb Z$ forces $c = 0$, and a constant $\pi$ sums to $0$ or to $\infty$, never to 1. By the theorem the walk is not positive recurrent: its mean return time is infinite (Example 21.10).

On a continuous space a single point is usually reached with probability 0, so conditions are stated for sets. Following [Roberts and Rosenthal (2004)](#references), Section 3.2:

- **φ-irreducible**: there is a nonzero measure $\varphi$ such that from every $x$, every set $A$ with $\varphi(A) > 0$ has $P^n(x, A) > 0$ for some $n$. For MCMC, $\varphi$ is typically the target or the volume on its support.
- **Aperiodic**: there are no $d \ge 2$ disjoint sets $\mathcal X_1, \dots, \mathcal X_d$ with $\pi(\mathcal X_1) > 0$ that the chain visits in a fixed cycle, $\mathcal X_1 \to \mathcal X_2 \to \dots \to \mathcal X_d \to \mathcal X_1$, with probability 1.
- **Harris recurrent**: from every $x$, every set $B$ with $\pi(B) > 0$ is reached with probability 1.

If a chain on $\mathbb R^d$ is φ-irreducible and aperiodic and has a stationary distribution $\pi$, then the distribution of $X_n$ converges to $\pi$ in total variation from $\pi$-almost every start (Theorem 4), and $\frac1n \sum_t h(X_t) \to E_\pi h$ with probability 1 for every $h$ with $E_\pi \lvert h \rvert < \infty$ (Fact 5). If the chain is also Harris recurrent, the convergence holds from every start (the discussion after Example 3). The intuition is the coupling argument again, with a set of states that share a common piece of their next-step distribution playing the part of a single state.

The exceptional starts are real. On the states $1, 2, 3, \dots$ let state 1 be absorbing, and from $x \ge 2$ jump to 1 with probability $1/x^2$, otherwise to $x + 1$ (Example 3 there). The stationary distribution is the point mass at 1, and the chain is irreducible in the sense above with $\varphi$ that point mass. From $x = 2$, the probability of never reaching 1 is a product. We factor $1 - 1/j^2 = (j-1)(j+1)/j^2$, and the products of $(j-1)/j$ and of $(j+1)/j$ telescope to $1/N$ and $(N+1)/2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\prod_{j=2}^{N} \Big(1 - \frac{1}{j^2}\Big) &= \prod_{j=2}^{N} \frac{(j-1)(j+1)}{j \cdot j} \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{N+1}{N} \to \frac12 .
\end{aligned}
$$

With probability $1/2$ the chain runs off to infinity and never converges. Harris recurrence rules this out. A Metropolis–Hastings chain whose proposal density is positive and continuous, with a finite $\tilde p$, is irreducible in this sense, aperiodic, and converges from every start (Section 3.2 there), and the [MCMC note](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo#metropolis-hastings) shows how a proposal with gaps can break irreducibility.

## Language models as Markov chains

A bigram language model is a Markov chain whose states are words: $P_{ij}$ is the probability that word $j$ follows word $i$, estimated from counts. Generating text from it is running the chain. For a bigram chain fitted to one long text, the stationary distribution is approximately the unigram word frequency: each word is followed by a word, and preceded by one, as often as it occurs, except at the two ends of the text, so the unigram frequencies satisfy the stationarity equation up to these boundary terms. Text made of separate sentences needs an end-of-sentence token that leads back to a start state, or the chain stops. An n-gram model with $n > 2$ is a Markov chain whose states are contexts of $n - 1$ words; its stationary distribution is over contexts, and the unigram frequency is its marginal for a single position of the context. Shannon's 1948 paper generated "English" this way, from letter and word chains of increasing order, and the [n-gram language models note](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models) builds such models from data.

A transformer language model is a Markov chain too, but on a far larger state: the whole context window. The Markov property is a statement about what the state contains, and making the state richer is how sequence models acquire longer memory.

## PageRank

A reader who follows a random link from each page performs a random walk, a Markov chain on the pages. Pages that many walks visit often are important; their importance is the stationary distribution. Two fixes make the chain well defined, irreducible and aperiodic: with probability $0.15$ the reader jumps to a random page instead of following a link (and always jumps from a page without links). The stationary distribution of that chain is **PageRank**, the ranking signal behind the early Google search engine:

## Hitting probabilities: the gambler's ruin

Some chains have **absorbing** states that are never left, and the question is which one is reached first. A gambler with 3 coins bets one coin at a time on a fair game and stops at 0 or at 10. Let $h_a$ be the probability of reaching 10 from $a$; conditioning on the first bet gives $h_a = \frac12 h_{a+1} + \frac12 h_{a-1}$ with $h_0 = 0$, $h_{10} = 1$, so $h_a = a/10$ and $h_3 = 0.3$. The expected number of bets is $a(10 - a) = 21$. A slightly unfavourable game, winning each bet with probability $0.49$, lowers the chance to $0.259$; starting from 50 coins with a target of 100 it is only $0.119$, not one half: over a long game a small edge compounds. The same first-step equations give hitting times and absorption probabilities for any chain, from customer funnels to the expected length of a random walk.

## What changed

| | Independent samples | Markov chains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dependence | none | the next state depends on the current one: $P_{ij}$ |
| Distribution over time | constant | $\pi_t = \pi_0 P^t$ |
| Long run | the law of large numbers | stationary $\pi = \pi P$; the ergodic theorem for time averages |
| Speed | $1/\sqrt n$ | also the mixing time, set by $\lvert \lambda_2 \rvert$ |
| Examples | | n-gram models, PageRank, random walks, MCMC |

## Limitations

- **The Markov property is a modelling choice.** Real processes have longer memory; enlarging the state (the last $n$ words, the full context) restores the property at the cost of a much larger state space.
- **Convergence can be slow.** Chains with bottlenecks, such as two clusters of states joined by rare transitions, can take a very long time to mix, and a single run may show no sign of it.
- **Finite states hide subtleties.** Infinite or continuous state spaces need conditions (positive recurrence, φ-irreducibility, Harris recurrence) that the finite case gets for free; see [beyond finite chains](#beyond-finite-chains).

## What you should remember

- Markov property: the future depends on the past only through the present; $P_{ij} = P(X_{t+1} = j \mid X_t = i)$.
- Distributions evolve by $\pi_{t+1} = \pi_t P$; $(P^t)_{ij}$ is the $t$-step transition probability.
- Stationary: $\pi P = \pi$. For finite irreducible aperiodic chains, $\pi_t \to \pi$ from any start; time averages converge to $E_\pi f$ under irreducibility alone.
- The second largest eigenvalue in absolute value sets the speed: distance to $\pi$ $\approx \lvert \lambda_2 \rvert^t$.
- n-gram models and PageRank are Markov chains; PageRank is the stationary distribution of a random surfer with teleportation.
- Absorption probabilities and hitting times solve first-step equations; in the fair gambler's ruin with target $N$, $h_a = a/N$.

## Code

The weather chain, its stationary distribution and a long run, PageRank, and the gambler's ruin:

```python
import numpy as np

# the weather chain: distributions after 1, 2 and 10 days from a sunny start
P = np.array([[0.7, 0.2, 0.1], [0.3, 0.4, 0.3], [0.2, 0.3, 0.5]])
pi = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
for t in range(11):
    if t in (1, 2, 10):
        print(t, pi.round(3))
    pi = pi @ P
# 1 [0.7 0.2 0.1] | 2 [0.57 0.25 0.18] | 10 [0.457 0.283 0.261]

# the stationary distribution solves pi P = pi with sum 1; the eigenvalues set the speed
A = np.vstack([P.T - np.eye(3), np.ones(3)])
stationary = np.linalg.lstsq(A, np.array([0, 0, 0, 1.0]), rcond=None)[0]
print(stationary.round(4), (np.array([21, 13, 12]) / 46).round(4))   # [0.4565 0.2826 0.2609] twice
print(np.sort(np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(P))).round(3))                # [0.127 0.473 1.   ]

# the ergodic theorem: the share of days in each state along one long run
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x, counts = 0, np.zeros(3)
for _ in range(100_000):
    counts[x] += 1
    x = rng.choice(3, p=P[x])
print((counts / counts.sum()).round(3))                               # [0.459 0.281 0.259]

# PageRank of five pages by power iteration, damping 0.85
links = {0: [1, 2], 1: [2], 2: [0], 3: [0, 2], 4: [3, 2]}
M = np.zeros((5, 5))
for i, outs in links.items():
    M[i, outs] = 1 / len(outs)
G = 0.85 * M + 0.15 / 5
r = np.full(5, 0.2)
for _ in range(200):
    r = r @ G
print(r.round(3))                                                     # [0.367 0.186 0.375 0.043 0.03 ]

# gambler's ruin: the probability of reaching N before 0 from a, winning each bet with probability p
ruin = lambda p, a, N: a / N if p == 0.5 else (1 - ((1 - p) / p) ** a) / (1 - ((1 - p) / p) ** N)
print(ruin(0.5, 3, 10), round(ruin(0.49, 3, 10), 3), round(ruin(0.49, 50, 100), 3))   # 0.3 0.259 0.119
```
