The [previous note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions) showed that for a convex function a zero gradient certifies a global minimum, and it measured how curved a function is with two constants, the strong convexity $\mu$ and the smoothness $L$. This note turns those facts into algorithms with guarantees. It states when a point can be a minimum at all, proves how many steps gradient descent needs on smooth and on strongly convex functions, and derives Newton's method, which uses the Hessian to remove the dependence on the condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$. The machine learning course uses gradient descent on least squares, where everything is an exact computation on a quadratic ([gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent)); here are the general proofs behind the rates it quotes.

**Notation.** $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$ is twice differentiable, $x^*$ a minimizer and $f^* = f(x^*)$. The iterates are $x_k$, with gradient $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)$ and Hessian $\nabla^2 f(x_k)$. A method moves along a **search direction** $d_k$ with a **step length** $t_k$, $x_{k+1} = x_k + t_k d_k$; gradient descent with a fixed step $\eta$ has $d_k = -g_k$ and $t_k = \eta$. $\mu$, $L$ and $\kappa = L/\mu$ are as in the previous note: $\mu I \preceq \nabla^2 f \preceq LI$. The error of an iterate is $e_k = x_k - x^*$.

## Baseline: a fixed step and a long wait

Take an L2-penalized logistic loss on 40 points with two features, the second one ten times larger than the first (the Code section builds the data):

$$
f(w) = \frac1{40}\sum_{i=1}^{40} \log\big(1 + e^{-y_i w^\top x_i}\big) + \frac{0.01}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2 .
$$

The penalty makes it $\mu$-strongly convex with $\mu = 0.01$, and the bound $p(1 - p) \le \frac14$ of the previous note makes it $L$-smooth with $L = \lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)/160 + 0.01 = 7.898$. Gradient descent with the safe step $\eta = 1/L$ from $w = 0$ lowers $f - f^*$ from $0.38$ to $0.23$ in 10 steps, to $0.069$ in 100 and to $3.8 \cdot 10^{-5}$ in 1,000, and needs 2,727 steps to reach $10^{-10}$. Every step decreases the loss, and nothing is wrong with the code. Three questions follow. What does gradient descent guarantee on a loss that is not a quadratic? Why does it need thousands of steps on a problem with two unknowns? And can the curvature, which the gradient ignores, be used to do better? The same problem solved by Newton's method needs 5 steps.

## Optimality conditions

Before an algorithm, a test: what must hold at a minimizer, and what is enough to be sure of one? The conditions come from the Taylor expansion of the [derivatives note](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series) in $n$ variables, $f(x^* + h) = f(x^*) + \nabla f(x^*)^\top h + \frac12h^\top\nabla^2 f(x^*)h + o(\lVert h\rVert^2)$.

- **First-order necessary.** If $x^*$ is a local minimum, then $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$. Otherwise the step $h = -t\nabla f(x^*)$ changes $f$ by $-t\lVert\nabla f(x^*)\rVert^2 + o(t)$, which is negative for small $t > 0$.
- **Second-order necessary.** If $x^*$ is a local minimum, then also $\nabla^2 f(x^*) \succeq 0$. Otherwise some $v$ has $v^\top\nabla^2 f(x^*)v \lt 0$, and with the gradient zero, $f(x^* + t v) - f(x^*) = \frac{t^2}{2}v^\top\nabla^2 f(x^*)v + o(t^2) \lt 0$ for small $t$.
- **Second-order sufficient.** If $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ and $\nabla^2 f(x^*) \succ 0$, then $x^*$ is a strict local minimum: the quadratic term is at least $\frac12\lambda_{\min}\lVert h\rVert^2$ with $\lambda_{\min} \gt 0$, which beats the $o(\lVert h\rVert^2)$ remainder for small $h$.
- **Convex.** If $f$ is convex, $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ alone is sufficient, for a global minimum, by the tangent bound of the previous note.

The gap between necessary and sufficient is real. $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + x_2^4$ and $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 - x_2^4$ both have zero gradient and the Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(2, 0)$ at the origin, which is positive semidefinite but singular. The first has a minimum there and the second a saddle: along $x_2$ the quadratic term vanishes and the quartic decides. The [gradients note](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians) classifies critical points by the signs of the Hessian eigenvalues; an algorithm that only drives the gradient to zero can stop at any of them unless the function is convex.

## Gradient descent on a smooth function

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Smoothness guarantees that each gradient step lowers the loss by at least $\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2/(2L)$; strong convexity guarantees that the gradient is large while the loss is far from optimal. Together they shrink $f - f^*$ by the factor $1 - 1/\kappa$ per step, so gradient descent needs about $\kappa$ steps per factor $e$. Newton's method rescales the step by the inverse Hessian, which removes $\kappa$ near the minimum.</aside>

The idea: an $L$-smooth function lies below the parabola of curvature $L$ that touches it at the current point (the [upper bound](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#strong-convexity-and-smoothness) of the previous note), and a gradient step goes downhill on that parabola. For any step $\eta \gt 0$,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
f(x - \eta\nabla f(x)) &\le f(x) - \eta\Big(1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}\Big)\lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2 , \\
\eta &= \frac1L:\ f(x_{k+1}) \le f(x_k) - \frac{1}{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x - \eta\nabla f) &\le f(x) + \nabla f^\top(-\eta\nabla f) + \frac L2\lVert -\eta\nabla f\rVert^2 && \text{the upper parabola with } y - x = -\eta\nabla f(x) \\
&= f(x) - \eta\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2 + \frac{L\eta^2}{2}\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2 && \text{expand both terms} \\
&= f(x) - \eta\Big(1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}\Big)\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2 && \text{factor out } \eta\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the **descent lemma**, the many-variable form of the one-variable bound in the [derivatives note](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series#why-first-and-second-order-matter-for-optimization). Every $\eta \in (0, 2/L)$ decreases $f$ whenever the gradient is nonzero, and the guaranteed decrease $\eta(1 - L\eta/2)$ is largest at $\eta = 1/L$. Summing it over $K$ steps telescopes: $\frac{1}{2L}\sum_{k \lt K}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 \le f(x_0) - f(x_K) \le f(x_0) - \inf f$. Without convexity this is all that holds: the smallest gradient norm among the first $K$ steps is at most $\sqrt{2L(f(x_0) - \inf f)/K}$, so the iterates approach a critical point at rate $1/\sqrt K$ in the gradient norm, with no promise about which kind ([gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#beyond-quadratics-what-is-guaranteed) in the machine learning course states the same three regimes).

### Convex and smooth: the excess loss falls like 1/k

The idea: convexity says how far the loss is above its minimum in terms of the gradient and the distance to $x^*$, and each step trades that excess for a decrease of the squared distance. With $\eta = 1/L$ on a convex $L$-smooth $f$,

$$
\boxed{\; f(x_k) - f^* \le \frac{L\,\lVert x_0 - x^*\rVert^2}{2k} . \;}
$$

One step, with $e_k = x_k - x^*$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x_{k+1}) &\le f(x_k) - \frac{1}{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 && \text{descent lemma} \\
&\le f^* + g_k^\top e_k - \frac{1}{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 && \text{tangent bound } f^* \ge f(x_k) + g_k^\top(x^* - x_k) \\
&= f^* + \frac L2\Big(\lVert e_k\rVert^2 - \big\lVert e_k - \tfrac1L g_k\big\rVert^2\Big) && \text{expand the square: the two sides agree} \\
&= f^* + \frac L2\big(\lVert e_k\rVert^2 - \lVert e_{k+1}\rVert^2\big) && e_{k+1} = e_k - \tfrac1L g_k .
\end{aligned}
$$

The third line is checked by expanding: $\frac L2\big(\lVert e\rVert^2 - \lVert e\rVert^2 + \frac2L g^\top e - \frac1{L^2}\lVert g\rVert^2\big) = g^\top e - \frac1{2L}\lVert g\rVert^2$. Summing over $k = 0, \dots, K - 1$ telescopes the right side to $\frac L2(\lVert e_0\rVert^2 - \lVert e_K\rVert^2) \le \frac L2\lVert e_0\rVert^2$. The left side is a sum of $K$ excesses $f(x_{k+1}) - f^*$, each at least the last one because the loss never increases, so $K\big(f(x_K) - f^*\big) \le \frac L2\lVert e_0\rVert^2$. On the way, the chain shows $\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert \le \lVert e_k\rVert$: the iterates never move away from the minimizer.

**Worked example.** For the baseline problem $\lVert w^*\rVert^2 = 3.468^2 + 0.380^2 = 12.17$, so the bound is $7.898 \cdot 12.17/(2k) = 48.1/k$: at most $0.048$ after 1,000 steps. The run reaches $3.8 \cdot 10^{-5}$. The bound is valid and pessimistic, because it uses neither the penalty nor the curvature near the minimum.

### Strongly convex: a linear rate set by κ

The idea: strong convexity guarantees a large gradient while the loss is far from its minimum, through the bound $f(x) - f^* \le \lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2/(2\mu)$ of the [previous note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#strong-convexity-and-smoothness); the descent lemma converts a large gradient into a large decrease. With $\eta = 1/L$ on a $\mu$-strongly convex, $L$-smooth $f$,

$$
\boxed{\; f(x_k) - f^* \le \Big(1 - \frac1\kappa\Big)^k \big(f(x_0) - f^*\big) \le e^{-k/\kappa}\big(f(x_0) - f^*\big) . \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x_{k+1}) - f^* &\le f(x_k) - f^* - \frac1{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 && \text{descent lemma, minus } f^* \text{ on both sides} \\
&\le f(x_k) - f^* - \frac{\mu}{L}\big(f(x_k) - f^*\big) && \lVert g_k\rVert^2 \ge 2\mu\big(f(x_k) - f^*\big) \\
&= \Big(1 - \frac1\kappa\Big)\big(f(x_k) - f^*\big) && \kappa = L/\mu .
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying the step $k$ times gives the first bound, and $1 - u \le e^{-u}$ gives the second. To shrink the excess loss by a factor $\varepsilon$ it is enough to take $k \ge \kappa\ln(1/\varepsilon)$ steps: the number of steps is **linear in the condition number**. For $\kappa = 100$ and $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$ that is $\ln 10^6/{-\ln 0.99} = 1{,}375$ steps by the first bound and $100 \ln 10^6 = 1{,}382$ by the second. Each step costs one gradient, so on badly conditioned problems gradient descent is slow however cheap its steps are.

Why the condition number? Near the minimum $f$ is close to a quadratic with Hessian $\nabla^2 f(x^*)$, whose level sets are ellipsoids with axes in the ratio $\sqrt\kappa$. The gradient is perpendicular to the level set, so it points at the minimum only when the level sets are round. On an elongated bowl the safe step is limited by the steep direction ($\eta \le 1/L$), and along the flat direction that step moves only a fraction $\mu/L$ of the way. The [exact analysis on a quadratic](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case) gives the best constant step $2/(L + \mu)$ with the error factor $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ per step, the same linear dependence on $\kappa$ with a better constant, and [momentum](/notes/ml/stochastic-and-adaptive-optimizers#momentum-remember-the-direction) reduces it to $\sqrt\kappa$, the best any method built from gradients alone can do.

**Worked example.** For the baseline the constants give $\kappa = 7.898/0.01 = 790$, and the bound promises $f - f^* \le 10^{-10}$ after $790 \ln(0.38/10^{-10}) = 17{,}431$ steps. The run needed 2,727. The global $\mu$ and $L$ are worst cases over the whole space; near the minimum the Hessian has eigenvalues $0.029$ and $3.22$, a local condition number of 110, and the observed speed follows the local value.

## Line search: the Armijo condition

$L$ is rarely known, and $1/L$ is often far smaller than the steps the function allows. A **line search** chooses the step length at every iteration. The idea: accept a step if it achieves at least a fixed fraction $c$ of the decrease that the slope at $t = 0$ promises; start with a long step and shrink it until it is accepted. For a descent direction ($g_k^\top d_k \lt 0$) and a constant $c \in (0, 1)$, the **Armijo condition** ([Armijo, 1966](#references)) is

$$
\boxed{\; f(x_k + t\,d_k) \le f(x_k) + c\, t\, g_k^\top d_k , \;}
$$

and **backtracking** tries $t = 1, \frac12, \frac14, \dots$ until it holds; $c = 10^{-4}$ is the usual choice. Why it ends: by Taylor, $f(x_k + t d_k) = f(x_k) + t\,g_k^\top d_k + o(t)$, and since $c \lt 1$ and $g_k^\top d_k \lt 0$, the actual decrease beats the required one for all small enough $t$. How long the accepted step is, for gradient descent on an $L$-smooth function: with $d = -g$ the descent lemma gives $f(x - t g) \le f(x) - t(1 - Lt/2)\lVert g\rVert^2$, and this meets the Armijo condition, $-ct\lVert g\rVert^2$, whenever $1 - Lt/2 \ge c$, that is $t \le 2(1 - c)/L$. Halving therefore stops at some $t \ge \min\big(1, (1 - c)/L\big)$, and every accepted step lowers $f$ by at least $c\,t\,\lVert g\rVert^2$: the rates above hold with a constant that depends on $c$, without knowing $L$. On the baseline problem gradient descent with backtracking ($c = 10^{-4}$) needs 526 steps instead of 2,727, because it takes steps longer than $1/L$ wherever the local curvature allows.

A stronger version, the **Wolfe conditions**, adds a curvature condition $\nabla f(x_k + t d_k)^\top d_k \ge c_2\,g_k^\top d_k$ with $c \lt c_2 \lt 1$ (typically $0.9$), which rules out steps so short that the slope has hardly changed. Quasi-Newton methods need it, as shown below.

## Newton's method

Gradient descent uses a linear model of $f$ and therefore needs a step size. The idea of Newton's method is to use the quadratic model instead, whose minimizer is a complete step, length and direction included:

$$
m_k(d) = f(x_k) + g_k^\top d + \tfrac12d^\top\nabla^2 f(x_k)\,d .
$$

When $\nabla^2 f(x_k) \succ 0$, $m_k$ is a strictly convex quadratic and its minimum is where its gradient $g_k + \nabla^2 f(x_k)d$ vanishes:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^2 f(x_k)\,d_k &= -g_k , \\
x_{k+1} &= x_k + d_k = x_k - \big[\nabla^2 f(x_k)\big]^{-1}\nabla f(x_k) .
\end{aligned}
}
$$

The Newton direction is computed by solving a linear system, by a Cholesky factorization when the Hessian is positive definite, never by forming the inverse. On a quadratic $f(x) = \tfrac12x^\top A x - b^\top x$ the model is the function itself, and one step from any point lands on $x^* = A^{-1}b$, whatever $\kappa$ is. More generally, Newton's method is **affine invariant**: in coordinates $x = A z$ with an invertible $A$, the Hessian becomes $A^\top\nabla^2 f A$ and the gradient $A^\top\nabla f$, and the Newton step in $z$ is exactly the Newton step in $x$ mapped back. Rescaling or rotating the features, which changes $\kappa$ and the speed of gradient descent, changes nothing for Newton. [Logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs) is fitted this way, as iteratively reweighted least squares.

### Quadratic convergence

The idea: the Newton step solves the linearized equation $\nabla f = 0$, so its error comes only from the change of the Hessian between $x_k$ and $x^*$, which is proportional to the distance; the new error is the old one times a factor proportional to the old one. Assume that near $x^*$ the Hessian satisfies $\nabla^2 f \succeq \mu I$ and is $M$-Lipschitz, $\lVert\nabla^2 f(x) - \nabla^2 f(y)\rVert \le M\lVert x - y\rVert$. Then

$$
\boxed{\; \lVert e_{k+1}\rVert \le \frac{M}{2\mu}\lVert e_k\rVert^2 . \;}
$$

Write $H_k = \nabla^2 f(x_k)$ and use $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
e_{k+1} &= e_k - H_k^{-1}\big(\nabla f(x_k) - \nabla f(x^*)\big) && \text{the Newton step, minus } x^* \\
&= H_k^{-1}\Big(H_k e_k - \int_0^1 \nabla^2 f(x^* + s\,e_k)\,e_k\, ds\Big) && \text{fundamental theorem of calculus for } \nabla f \text{ along the segment} \\
&= H_k^{-1}\int_0^1 \big(\nabla^2 f(x_k) - \nabla^2 f(x^* + s\,e_k)\big)e_k\, ds && H_k e_k = \textstyle\int_0^1 H_k e_k\, ds \\
\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert &\le \frac1\mu\int_0^1 M(1 - s)\lVert e_k\rVert\,\lVert e_k\rVert\, ds = \frac{M}{2\mu}\lVert e_k\rVert^2 && \lVert H_k^{-1}\rVert \le 1/\mu;\ x_k - (x^* + s e_k) = (1 - s)e_k .
\end{aligned}
$$

Once $\lVert e_k\rVert \lt \mu/M$, the factor $\frac{M}{2\mu}\lVert e_k\rVert$ is below $\frac12$ and shrinks at every step: the number of correct digits roughly doubles per step. This is **quadratic convergence**, against the linear convergence of gradient descent, which adds a fixed number of digits per step.

**Worked example.** $f(x) = x - \ln x$ on $x \gt 0$ has $f'(x) = 1 - 1/x$, $f''(x) = 1/x^2$ and its minimum at $x^* = 1$. The Newton step is $x - (1 - 1/x)\,x^2 = 2x - x^2$, so $x_{k+1} - 1 = -(x_k - 1)^2$ exactly: the error is squared at every step, which matches the bound with $\mu = f''(1) = 1$ and $M = \lvert f'''(1)\rvert = 2$. From $x_0 = 0.5$ the errors are $-0.5$, $-0.25$, $-0.0625$, $-3.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$, $-1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ and $-2.3 \cdot 10^{-10}$.

### Damping

Far from the minimum the quadratic model can be a poor guide. In the example, from $x_0 = 3$ the full step lands at $2 \cdot 3 - 9 = -3$, outside the domain. **Damped Newton** keeps the Newton direction and chooses its length by backtracking with the Armijo condition, starting from $t = 1$. The Newton direction is a descent direction whenever the Hessian is positive definite, since $g^\top d = -g^\top[\nabla^2 f]^{-1}g \lt 0$. From $x_0 = 3$: $d = -(1 - \frac13) \cdot 9 = -6$; $t = 1$ and $t = \frac12$ leave the domain, and $t = \frac14$ gives $x = 1.5$ with $f = 1.095$, below $f(3) = 1.901$ by more than the Armijo threshold. From $1.5$ the full step is accepted at every iteration, $0.75$, $0.9375$, $0.9961, \dots$, and the quadratic phase begins. Near the minimum the full step always passes the Armijo test with $c \lt \frac12$, so damping costs nothing there ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), §9.5.3).

Two costs remain. Each step forms the $n \times n$ Hessian and solves a linear system, $O(n^3)$ operations and $O(n^2)$ memory, which is out of reach for $n$ in the millions. And on a non-convex function the Hessian can be indefinite, the model has no minimum and the Newton direction may point uphill; practical codes then add a multiple of the identity to the Hessian or restrict the step to a region where the model is trusted ([Nocedal and Wright, 2006](#references), §3.4 and Chapter 4).

## Quasi-Newton methods: BFGS

The idea: keep Newton's step $d_k = -H_k g_k$, but replace the inverse Hessian by an approximation $H_k$ that is built from gradients only and corrected after every step. The information available after a step is the pair

$$
s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k , \qquad y_k = g_{k+1} - g_k ,
$$

and along the step the true Hessian maps $s_k$ approximately to $y_k$, as the slope of a secant does in one variable. The **secant equation** asks the new approximation to reproduce it exactly, $H_{k+1}y_k = s_k$. Among the symmetric matrices that satisfy it, the **BFGS** update (Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb and Shanno, 1970) is the one closest to $H_k$ in a weighted norm ([Nocedal and Wright, 2006](#references), §6.1):

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
H_{k+1} &= \big(I - \rho_k s_k y_k^\top\big)\, H_k\, \big(I - \rho_k y_k s_k^\top\big) + \rho_k s_k s_k^\top , \\
\rho_k &= \frac{1}{y_k^\top s_k} .
\end{aligned}
}
$$

The secant equation is a one-line check: $(I - \rho y s^\top)y = y - \rho y(s^\top y) = 0$, so the first term vanishes on $y$, and $\rho s s^\top y = s$. The update keeps $H$ positive definite exactly when $y_k^\top s_k \gt 0$; if $y^\top s \le 0$, no positive definite matrix can satisfy the secant equation, because $y^\top H y = y^\top s$ would have to be non-positive. For a strongly convex function $y_k^\top s_k \ge \mu\lVert s_k\rVert^2 \gt 0$ automatically, and in general the Wolfe curvature condition guarantees it.

Each BFGS step costs $O(n^2)$, a few matrix–vector products, and the method converges superlinearly: the ratio $\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert/\lVert e_k\rVert$ tends to 0, though not as fast as Newton's square. **L-BFGS** ([Liu and Nocedal, 1989](#references)) stores only the last $m$ pairs $(s, y)$, typically 5 to 20, and applies the update implicitly, at $O(mn)$ per step; it is the default solver of scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` and a standard choice for smooth deterministic problems of moderate size.

## Limitations

- **The constants are rarely known**, and the global $\mu$ and $L$ can be far from the local curvature: the bound on the baseline promised 17,431 steps where 2,727 sufficed.
- **Non-convex functions** get only a small gradient from gradient descent and an indefinite Hessian from Newton; both can stop at saddle points without safeguards.
- **Newton's method does not scale** to the dimensions of deep learning: the Hessian of a network with $10^8$ weights has $10^{16}$ entries. Hessian-free variants use Hessian–vector products instead.
- **Deterministic analysis.** Every rate here assumes exact gradients. With minibatch gradients the noise changes the picture, as the [stochastic optimizers note](/notes/ml/stochastic-and-adaptive-optimizers) shows, and BFGS updates built from noisy pairs $(s, y)$ become unreliable.
- **Non-smooth losses** such as the hinge or the L1 penalty break the descent lemma; they need subgradient or proximal methods.

## What you should remember

- At a local minimum $\nabla f = 0$ and $\nabla^2 f \succeq 0$; $\nabla f = 0$ with $\nabla^2 f \succ 0$ is sufficient for a strict local minimum, and $\nabla f = 0$ alone is sufficient for convex $f$.
- Descent lemma: with $\eta = 1/L$, every step lowers $f$ by at least $\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2/(2L)$.
- Convex and $L$-smooth: $f(x_k) - f^* \le L\lVert x_0 - x^*\rVert^2/(2k)$. Strongly convex: $f(x_k) - f^* \le (1 - 1/\kappa)^k(f(x_0) - f^*)$, about $\kappa\ln(1/\varepsilon)$ steps.
- Armijo backtracking finds a step with a guaranteed decrease without knowing $L$.
- Newton's method solves $\nabla^2 f\,d = -\nabla f$, is exact on quadratics, ignores $\kappa$ and converges quadratically near a minimum; damping with a line search makes it safe far away.
- BFGS builds an inverse-Hessian approximation from gradient differences through the secant equation $H y = s$; L-BFGS does it in $O(mn)$ memory.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

# Newton's method on f(x) = x - log x, minimum at 1: x <- 2x - x^2, and the error is squared at every step
x = 0.5
for k in range(6):
    print(k, f'{x - 1:.3e}', end=' | ')
    x = 2 * x - x * x
print()
# 0 -5.000e-01 | 1 -2.500e-01 | 2 -6.250e-02 | 3 -3.906e-03 | 4 -1.526e-05 | 5 -2.328e-10

# from x = 3 the full step lands at -3, outside the domain; backtracking (Armijo, c = 1e-4) halves it twice
f = lambda x: x - np.log(x) if x > 0 else np.inf
x = 3.0
for k in range(7):
    g, h = 1 - 1 / x, 1 / x**2
    d, t = -g / h, 1.0
    while f(x + t * d) > f(x) + 1e-4 * t * g * d:
        t /= 2
    print(k, round(x, 6), t, end=' | ')
    x += t * d
print()
# 0 3.0 0.25 | 1 1.5 1.0 | 2 0.75 1.0 | 3 0.9375 1.0 | 4 0.996094 1.0 | 5 0.999985 1.0 | 6 1.0 1.0

# an elongated quadratic: eigenvalues mu = 1 and L = 10, axes turned by 30 degrees, minimum at 0
th = np.deg2rad(30); Rot = np.array([[np.cos(th), -np.sin(th)], [np.sin(th), np.cos(th)]])
mu, L = 1.0, 10.0
A = Rot @ np.diag([mu, L]) @ Rot.T
q = lambda x: 0.5 * x @ A @ x
x0 = Rot @ np.array([2.0, 0.2])
def count(step):
    x, k = x0.copy(), 0
    while q(x) > 1e-6 * q(x0):
        g = A @ x
        x, k = x - step(g) * g, k + 1
    return k
print(count(lambda g: 1 / L), count(lambda g: 2 / (L + mu)), count(lambda g: g @ g / (g @ A @ g)),

# steps with 1/L, with 2/(L + mu) and with exact line search; the factor per step of exact line search;
# Newton lands on the minimum in one step; the steps the strongly convex guarantee asks for

# a logistic loss with an L2 penalty on 40 deterministic points; the second feature is ten times larger
n, lam = 40, 0.01
k_ = np.arange(1, n + 1)
X = np.column_stack([2 * ((k_ * 0.6180339887498949) % 1) - 1, 10 * (2 * ((k_ * 0.7548776662466927) % 1) - 1)])
noise = (k_ * 0.4142135623730951) % 1 - 0.5
y = np.where(X[:, 0] + 0.1 * X[:, 1] + noise > 0, 1.0, -1.0)
F = lambda w: np.mean(np.maximum(-y * (X @ w), 0) + np.log1p(np.exp(-np.abs(y * (X @ w))))) + lam / 2 * w @ w
G = lambda w: -X.T @ (y / (1 + np.exp(y * (X @ w)))) / n + lam * w
def Hess(w):
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w)))
    return (X.T * (p * (1 - p))) @ X / n + lam * np.eye(2)
Lb = np.linalg.eigvalsh(X.T @ X)[-1] / (4 * n) + lam          # a global bound on the Hessian
w = np.zeros(2)
for _ in range(30):
    w = w - np.linalg.solve(Hess(w), G(w))
ws, Fs = w, F(w)
print(ws.round(4), round(Fs, 5), round(Lb, 3), round(Lb / lam), np.linalg.eigvalsh(Hess(ws)).round(4), round(np.linalg.cond(Hess(ws))))
# [3.4682 0.3803] 0.30901 7.898 790 [0.0293 3.2176] 110

def run(step, tol=1e-10):
    w, k, gaps = np.zeros(2), 0, [F(np.zeros(2)) - Fs]
    while gaps[-1] > tol:
        w, k = step(w), k + 1
        gaps.append(F(w) - Fs)
    return k, gaps
def armijo(w):
    g, t = G(w), 1.0
    while F(w - t * g) > F(w) - 1e-4 * t * g @ g:
        t /= 2
    return w - t * g
k_gd, gaps = run(lambda w: w - G(w) / Lb)
k_ar, _ = run(armijo)
k_nt, gn = run(lambda w: w - np.linalg.solve(Hess(w), G(w)))
print(k_gd, k_ar, k_nt, [f'{g:.1e}' for g in gn], [f'{gaps[i]:.1e}' for i in (10, 100, 1000)])

print(all(gaps[k] <= (1 - lam / Lb) ** k * gaps[0] + 1e-15 for k in range(len(gaps))),
      all(gaps[k] <= Lb * ws @ ws / (2 * k) for k in range(1, len(gaps))),
      round(np.log(gaps[0] / 1e-10) * Lb / lam))

# BFGS: the inverse-Hessian approximation Hinv is updated from s = w+ - w and y = g+ - g, and satisfies Hinv y = s
def bfgs(w, tol=1e-10):
    Hinv, k = np.eye(2), 0
    while F(w) - Fs > tol:
        g = G(w); d = -Hinv @ g; t = 1.0
        while F(w + t * d) > F(w) + 1e-4 * t * g @ d:
            t /= 2
        s = t * d; yv = G(w + s) - g; rho = 1 / (yv @ s)
        V = np.eye(2) - rho * np.outer(s, yv)
        Hinv = V @ Hinv @ V.T + rho * np.outer(s, s)
        w, k = w + s, k + 1
    return k, Hinv @ yv - s
k_bf, secant = bfgs(np.zeros(2))
r = minimize(F, np.zeros(2), jac=G, method='BFGS', options={'gtol': 1e-9})
print(k_bf, np.abs(secant).max() < 1e-12, r.nit, f'{r.fun - Fs:.0e}')
```

## Next

Every problem in this note was free: any $x \in \mathbb R^n$ was allowed. Many problems in machine learning are not. Probabilities must stay on the simplex, the SVM requires every point to be on the correct side of its margin, and PCA looks for the best direction among unit vectors. The [next note](/notes/math/lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt) handles constraints: at a constrained minimum the gradient need not vanish, only be balanced by the constraints, and the Lagrange multipliers and KKT conditions say exactly how.
