Fit a logistic regression with an L2 penalty to 40 points with two features, the second ten times larger than the first (the Code section builds the data). The loss is

$$
f(w) = \frac1{40}\sum_{i=1}^{40} \log\big(1 + e^{-y_i w^\top x_i}\big) + \frac{0.01}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2 ,
$$

where $x_i$ are the points and $y_i = \pm1$ their labels. In the language of the [previous note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions), the penalty makes $f$ strongly convex with $\mu = 0.01$, and the bound $p(1 - p) \le \frac14$ makes it $L$-smooth with $L = \lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)/160 + 0.01 = 7.898$.

Gradient descent with the safe step $1/L$, started at $w = 0$, lowers the excess loss $f - f^*$ from $0.38$ to $0.23$ in 10 steps, to $0.069$ in 100 and to $3.8 \cdot 10^{-5}$ in 1,000. It needs 2,727 steps to reach $10^{-10}$. Every step decreases the loss, and nothing is wrong with the code. Newton's method solves the same problem in 5 steps.

Why does gradient descent need thousands of steps for two unknowns, and what does it guarantee on a loss that is not a quadratic? The answers come from the constants $\mu$ and $L$, and the cure from the curvature, which the gradient ignores.

## Optimality conditions

Before an algorithm, a test: what must hold at a minimizer, and what is enough to be sure of one? Write $x^*$ for a candidate and, for $f$ with continuous second derivatives, expand it to second order, as in the [gradients note](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians#the-hessian-and-the-second-order-taylor-expansion):

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x^* + h) ={}& f(x^*) + \nabla f(x^*)^\top h \\
&+ \tfrac12 h^\top\nabla^2 f(x^*)\,h + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Each condition reads one term of the expansion:

| Condition | Statement | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| first-order, necessary | local minimum $\Rightarrow \nabla f(x^*) = 0$ | otherwise $h = -t\nabla f(x^*)$ lowers $f$ by $t\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2 + o(t)$ |
| second-order, necessary | local minimum $\Rightarrow \nabla^2 f(x^*) \succeq 0$ | otherwise some $v$ with $v^\top\nabla^2 f\, v \lt 0$ lowers $f$ along $tv$ |
| second-order, sufficient | $\nabla f = 0$ and $\nabla^2 f \succ 0 \Rightarrow$ strict local minimum | the quadratic term is at least $\frac12\lambda_{\min}\lVert h\rVert^2$ (Rayleigh quotient), and for small $h$ the $o(\lVert h\rVert^2)$ term is below half of that |
| convex $f$ | $\nabla f(x^*) = 0 \Rightarrow$ global minimum | the tangent bound |

The gap between necessary and sufficient is real. $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + x_2^4$ and $f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 - x_2^4$ both have zero gradient and the Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(2, 0)$ at the origin, positive semidefinite but singular. The first has a minimum there and the second a saddle: along $x_2$ the quadratic term vanishes and the quartic decides. An algorithm that only drives the gradient to zero can stop at any critical point unless the function is convex.

## Gradient descent on a smooth function

Gradient descent moves against the gradient, $x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta\, g_k$, where $g_k = \nabla f(x_k)$ and $\eta$ is the step. How much does one step gain? An $L$-smooth function lies below the parabola of curvature $L$ that touches it at the current point, the [upper bound](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#strong-convexity-and-smoothness) of the previous note. Put $y - x = -\eta g$ into it, with $g = \nabla f(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x - \eta g) &\le f(x) - \eta\lVert g\rVert^2 + \frac{L\eta^2}{2}\lVert g\rVert^2 \\
&= f(x) - \eta\Big(1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}\Big)\lVert g\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the **descent lemma**, the many-variable form of the one-variable bound in the [derivatives note](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series#why-first-and-second-order-matter-for-optimization). Every $\eta \in (0, 2/L)$ decreases $f$ while the gradient is nonzero, because then $1 - L\eta/2 \gt 0$. The guaranteed decrease $\eta - L\eta^2/2$ has derivative $1 - L\eta$ in $\eta$, so it is largest at $\eta = 1/L$, where it equals $1/(2L)$:

$$
f(x_{k+1}) \le f(x_k) - \frac{1}{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 .
$$

Summed over $K$ steps, the decreases telescope, and the smallest of $K$ numbers is at most their average:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{K}{2L}\min_{k \lt K}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 &\le \frac{1}{2L}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 \\
&\le f(x_0) - f(x_K) \le f(x_0) - \inf f .
\end{aligned}
$$

Without convexity this is all that holds: the smallest gradient among the first $K$ steps satisfies

$$
\min_{k \lt K} \lVert g_k\rVert \le \sqrt{\frac{2L\,\big(f(x_0) - \inf f\big)}{K}} .
$$

The iterates approach a critical point at the rate $1/\sqrt K$ in the gradient norm, with no promise about which kind. The [gradient descent note](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#beyond-quadratics-what-is-guaranteed) of the machine learning course quotes the same three regimes this section proves.

### Convex and smooth: the excess loss falls like 1/k

Convexity bounds how far the loss is above its minimum in terms of the gradient and the distance to $x^*$. Each step then trades that excess for a decrease of the squared distance. Assume a minimizer $x^*$ exists, write $e_k = x_k - x^*$ for the error of an iterate and take $\eta = 1/L$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x_{k+1}) &\le f(x_k) - \tfrac{1}{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 \\
&\le f^* + g_k^\top e_k - \tfrac{1}{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 \\
&= f^* + \tfrac L2\big(\lVert e_k\rVert^2 - \lVert e_k - \tfrac1L g_k\rVert^2\big) \\
&= f^* + \tfrac L2\big(\lVert e_k\rVert^2 - \lVert e_{k+1}\rVert^2\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

1. The descent lemma.
2. The tangent bound at $x_k$ towards $x^*$, $f^* \ge f(x_k) - g_k^\top e_k$.
3. Expanding the square, $\lVert e_k - g_k/L\rVert^2 = \lVert e_k\rVert^2 - 2g_k^\top e_k/L + \lVert g_k\rVert^2/L^2$, gives back the previous line.
4. The step itself, $e_{k+1} = e_k - \tfrac1L g_k$.

Summing over $k = 0, \dots, K - 1$ telescopes the right side to at most $\frac L2\lVert e_0\rVert^2$. The left side is a sum of $K$ excesses $f(x_{k+1}) - f^*$, each at least the last one, because the loss never increases. Hence

$$
f(x_K) - f^* \le \frac{L\,\lVert x_0 - x^*\rVert^2}{2K} .
$$

On the way, the chain shows $\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert \le \lVert e_k\rVert$, because its left side minus $f^*$ is non-negative: the iterates never move away from the minimizer. [Merkulov (2024)](#references), lecture 9, proves the bound the same way.

For the opening problem, $\lVert w^*\rVert^2 = 3.468^2 + 0.380^2 = 12.17$, so the bound is $7.898 \cdot 12.17/(2k) = 48.1/k$, at most $0.048$ after 1,000 steps. The run reaches $3.8 \cdot 10^{-5}$. The bound is valid and pessimistic, because it uses neither the penalty nor the curvature near the minimum.

### Strongly convex: a linear rate set by κ

Strong convexity keeps the gradient large while the loss is far from its minimum, through the Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality $\lVert g\rVert^2 \ge 2\mu\big(f(x) - f^*\big)$ of the [previous note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#strong-convexity-and-smoothness). Put it into the descent lemma, after subtracting $f^*$ from both sides:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x_{k+1}) - f^* &\le f(x_k) - f^* - \tfrac{1}{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2 \\
&\le f(x_k) - f^* - \tfrac{2\mu}{2L}\big(f(x_k) - f^*\big) \\
&= \Big(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\Big)\big(f(x_k) - f^*\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line replaces $\lVert g_k\rVert^2$ by the smaller $2\mu\big(f(x_k) - f^*\big)$.

Applying this $k$ times, with the condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$:

$$
\boxed{\; f(x_k) - f^* \le \Big(1 - \frac1\kappa\Big)^k \big(f(x_0) - f^*\big) \;}
$$

Since $1 - u \le e^{-u}$, the [tangent bound](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-first-order-condition-the-tangent-lies-below) of the convex $e^{-u}$ at 0, the right side is at most $e^{-k/\kappa}\big(f(x_0) - f^*\big)$. To shrink the excess loss by a factor $\varepsilon$ it is enough that $e^{-k/\kappa} \le \varepsilon$, that is, $k \ge \kappa\ln(1/\varepsilon)$ steps: the number of steps is **linear in the condition number**. For $\kappa = 100$ and $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$ that is 1,375 steps by the first bound, $\ln 10^6/(-\ln 0.99) = 1374.6$, and 1,382 by the second, $100 \ln 10^6 = 1381.6$. Each step costs one gradient, so on badly conditioned problems gradient descent is slow however cheap its steps are. The proof used only the Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality, not convexity, so the linear rate holds for every smooth function that satisfies it ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 9; [Karimi, Nutini and Schmidt, 2016](#references), Theorem 1).

Why the condition number? Near the minimum $f$ is close to a quadratic with Hessian $\nabla^2 f(x^*)$. In its eigenvector coordinates the level set $\tfrac12\sum_i \lambda_i z_i^2 = c$ has half-axes $\sqrt{2c/\lambda_i}$, in the ratio $\sqrt{L/\mu} = \sqrt\kappa$. The gradient is [perpendicular to the level set](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians#the-gradient-points-uphill-across-the-level-sets), so it points at the minimum only when the level sets are round. On a quadratic, a step $\eta$ multiplies the error along an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$ by $1 - \eta\lambda$. The safe step is limited by the steep direction, $\eta \le 1/L$, and along the flat direction it then removes only a fraction $\mu/L$ of the error. [Strang (2018)](#references) draws this as a zigzag down a narrow valley.

The [exact analysis on a quadratic](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case) gives the best constant step $2/(L + \mu)$, with the error factor $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ per step: the same linear dependence on $\kappa$ with a better constant. [Momentum](/notes/ml/stochastic-and-adaptive-optimizers#momentum-remember-the-direction) reduces it to $\sqrt\kappa$, and no better dependence is possible for methods that move within the span of past gradients. [Bubeck (2015)](#references), Theorem 3.15, builds an $L$-smooth, $\mu$-strongly convex function on the infinite-dimensional space $\ell_2$ on which every such method has $f(x_t) - f^* \ge \tfrac\mu2\big((\sqrt\kappa - 1)/(\sqrt\kappa + 1)\big)^{2(t - 1)}\lVert x_1 - x^*\rVert^2$.

For the opening problem, $\kappa = 7.898/0.01 = 790$, and the bound promises $f - f^* \le 10^{-10}$ after $790 \ln(0.38/10^{-10}) = 17{,}431$ steps. The run needed 2,727. The global $\mu$ and $L$ are worst cases over the whole space. Near the minimum the Hessian has eigenvalues $0.029$ and $3.22$, a local condition number of 110. With the step $1/L$, the error along the flat eigenvector shrinks by $1 - 0.0293/7.898$ per step, and the excess loss, quadratic in the error, by the square of that, $0.99260$. That predicts $22.07/0.00743 = 2{,}970$ steps from $0.38$ to $10^{-10}$; the run needs 2,727 because its early steps are faster, and its late steps shrink the excess by $0.99259$, as predicted.

## Line search: the Armijo condition

$L$ is rarely known, and $1/L$ is often far shorter than the steps the function allows. A **line search** chooses the step length at every iteration, along a search direction $d_k$ that points downhill, $g_k^\top d_k \lt 0$. The slope at the start promises a decrease of $t\, g_k^\top d_k$ for a step $t$; the **Armijo condition** accepts the step if it achieves at least a fixed fraction $c \in (0, 1)$ of that:

$$
f(x_k + t\,d_k) \le f(x_k) + c\, t\, g_k^\top d_k .
$$

**Backtracking** tries $t = 1, \frac12, \frac14, \dots$ until the condition holds. [Armijo (1966)](#references), Corollary 2, proposed exactly this halving with $c = \frac12$; a much smaller $c$, about $10^{-4}$, is typical in practice ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 2). The search always ends: by Taylor, $f(x_k + t d_k) = f(x_k) + t\,g_k^\top d_k + o(t)$, and since $c \lt 1$ and $g_k^\top d_k \lt 0$, the actual decrease beats the required one for all small enough $t$.

How long is the accepted step? For gradient descent, $d = -g$. The descent lemma gives the first inequality below, and the second is the Armijo test:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x - tg) &\le f(x) - t\big(1 - \tfrac{Lt}{2}\big)\lVert g\rVert^2 \\
&\le f(x) - c\,t\,\lVert g\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second inequality holds whenever $1 - Lt/2 \ge c$, that is, $t \le 2(1 - c)/L$. Halving therefore stops at some $t \ge \min\big(1, (1 - c)/L\big) = t_{\min}$: the first trial step below $2(1 - c)/L$ is more than half of it. Every accepted step lowers $f$ by at least $c\,t_{\min}\lVert g\rVert^2$, and the Polyak–Łojasiewicz argument above, with $\tfrac1{2L}$ replaced by $c\,t_{\min}$, gives $f(x_{k+1}) - f^* \le (1 - 2\mu c\, t_{\min})\big(f(x_k) - f^*\big)$, without knowing $L$. On the opening problem, gradient descent with backtracking ($c = 10^{-4}$) needs 526 steps instead of 2,727, because it takes steps longer than $1/L$ wherever the local curvature allows.

A step can satisfy Armijo and still be too short to make progress. The **Wolfe conditions** add a curvature condition that rules out steps after which the slope has hardly changed:

$$
\nabla f(x_k + t d_k)^\top d_k \ge c_2\, g_k^\top d_k ,
$$

with $c \lt c_2 \lt 1$, typically $c_2 = 0.9$ ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 2). Quasi-Newton methods need it, as shown below.

## Newton's method

Gradient descent uses a linear model of $f$, and a linear model has no minimum, so it needs a step size. Newton's method uses the quadratic model instead:

$$
m_k(d) = f(x_k) + g_k^\top d + \tfrac12d^\top\nabla^2 f(x_k)\,d .
$$

When $\nabla^2 f(x_k) \succ 0$, $m_k$ is a strictly convex quadratic, and its minimum is where its gradient $g_k + \nabla^2 f(x_k)\,d$ vanishes. That gives a complete step, length and direction included:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^2 f(x_k)\,d_k &= -g_k , \\
x_{k+1} &= x_k + d_k .
\end{aligned}
$$

The same step solves the linearized optimality condition $\nabla f(x_k + d) \approx g_k + \nabla^2 f(x_k)\,d = 0$: Newton's method for minimization is Newton's root-finding method applied to $\nabla f$. A third reading explains why the condition number disappears. Gradient descent takes the steepest direction in the Euclidean norm. Measured in the norm $\lVert v\rVert_H = \sqrt{v^\top H v}$ of the local Hessian $H \succ 0$, the steepest direction becomes $-H^{-1}\nabla f$, the Newton direction, and in that norm the level sets near the minimum are round ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, EE364a](#references), lecture 9, the interpretation of the Newton step). To see it, substitute $u = H^{1/2}v$: the slope $g^\top v = (H^{-1/2}g)^\top u$ over $\lVert u\rVert \le 1$ is most negative for $u$ opposite to $H^{-1/2}g$, by Cauchy–Schwarz, that is, for $v$ along $-H^{-1}g$. The quadratic model's level sets $\tfrac12 h^\top H h = c$ are spheres $\lVert h\rVert_H = \sqrt{2c}$ in that norm.

The Newton direction is computed by solving a linear system, by a Cholesky factorization when the Hessian is positive definite, never by forming the inverse. On a quadratic $f(x) = \tfrac12x^\top A x - b^\top x$ the model is the function itself, and one step from any point lands on $x^* = A^{-1}b$, whatever $\kappa$ is. When $n$ is too large to factor $A$, the [iterative methods note](/notes/math/iterative-methods) minimizes the same quadratic by conjugate gradients, which need only products with $A$.

Newton's method is **affine invariant**. In coordinates $x = Az$ with an invertible $A$, the chain rule turns the Hessian into $A^\top\nabla^2 f A$ and the gradient into $A^\top\nabla f$, and the Newton step in $z$ is exactly the Newton step in $x$ mapped back:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_z &= -\big(A^\top H A\big)^{-1}A^\top g \\
&= -A^{-1}H^{-1}A^{-\top}A^\top g = A^{-1}\big(-H^{-1}g\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$ Rescaling or rotating the features, which changes $\kappa$ and the speed of gradient descent, changes nothing for Newton. [Logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs) is fitted this way, as iteratively reweighted least squares.

### Quadratic convergence

The Newton step solves a linearized equation, so its error comes only from the change of the Hessian between $x_k$ and $x^*$, which is proportional to the distance. Assume that on a ball around $x^*$ the Hessian satisfies $\nabla^2 f \succeq \mu I$ and is $M$-Lipschitz, $\lVert\nabla^2 f(x) - \nabla^2 f(y)\rVert \le M\lVert x - y\rVert$, and that $x_k$ lies in that ball. Write $H_k = \nabla^2 f(x_k)$ and use $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$. The difference $\nabla f(x_k) - \nabla f(x^*)$ is the integral of the Hessian along the segment from $x^*$ to $x_k$, and $H_k e_k$ is the integral of a constant:

$$
\begin{aligned}
e_{k+1} &= e_k - H_k^{-1}\big(\nabla f(x_k) - \nabla f(x^*)\big) \\
&= H_k^{-1}\int_0^1 \big(H_k - \nabla^2 f(x^* + s\,e_k)\big)\,e_k\, ds .
\end{aligned}
$$

Now take norms: $\lVert H_k^{-1}\rVert \le 1/\mu$, since the eigenvalues of $H_k^{-1}$ are the reciprocals of those of $H_k$, and the two Hessians in the integral are evaluated at points $(1 - s)\lVert e_k\rVert$ apart:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert &\le \frac1\mu \int_0^1 M(1 - s)\,\lVert e_k\rVert^2\, ds \\
&= \frac{M}{2\mu}\lVert e_k\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Once $\lVert e_k\rVert \lt \mu/M$, the factor $\frac{M}{2\mu}\lVert e_k\rVert$ is below $\frac12$, so $\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert \lt \frac12\lVert e_k\rVert$: the next iterate stays in the ball, the assumptions hold again, and the factor shrinks at every step. Taking logarithms, $\log\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert \le 2\log\lVert e_k\rVert + \log\frac{M}{2\mu}$: the number of correct digits roughly doubles per step. This is **quadratic convergence**, against the linear convergence of gradient descent, which adds a fixed number of digits per step. [Merkulov (2024)](#references), lecture 15, assumes the bound $\mu$ only at $x^*$ and finds that the error shrinks from any start with $\lVert e_0\rVert \lt 2\mu/(3M)$.

The function $f(x) = x - \ln x$ on $x \gt 0$ has $f'(x) = 1 - 1/x$, $f''(x) = 1/x^2$ and its minimum at $x^* = 1$. The Newton step is $x - (1 - 1/x)\,x^2 = 2x - x^2$, so $x_{k+1} - 1 = -(x_k - 1)^2$ exactly: the error is squared at every step. This matches the bound in the limit: on a shrinking interval around 1, $\mu \to f''(1) = 1$ and $M \to \lvert f'''(1)\rvert = 2$, so $M/(2\mu) \to 1$, the exact constant. From $x_0 = 0.5$:

| $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x_k - 1$ | $-0.5$ | $-0.25$ | $-0.0625$ | $-3.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $-1.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ | $-2.3 \cdot 10^{-10}$ |

### Damping

Far from the minimum the quadratic model can be a poor guide. In the example, from $x_0 = 3$ the full step lands at $2 \cdot 3 - 9 = -3$, outside the domain. **Damped Newton** keeps the Newton direction and chooses its length by backtracking with the Armijo condition, starting from $t = 1$. The Newton direction is a descent direction whenever the Hessian is positive definite, since $g^\top d = -g^\top[\nabla^2 f]^{-1}g \lt 0$.

From $x_0 = 3$ the direction is $d = -(1 - \frac13) \cdot 9 = -6$. The steps $t = 1$ and $t = \frac12$ leave the domain, and $t = \frac14$ gives $x = 1.5$ with $f = 1.095$, below $f(3) = 1.901$ by more than the Armijo threshold. From $1.5$ the full step is accepted at every iteration, $0.75$, $0.9375$, $0.9961, \dots$, and the quadratic phase begins. The two phases are general. For a strongly convex $f$ with Lipschitz Hessian, [Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004)](#references), Section 9.5.3, prove that while $\lVert\nabla f\rVert$ is above a threshold every damped step lowers $f$ by at least a fixed amount, and below it every step is a full step with quadratic convergence. Why the full step passes near the minimum is visible on a quadratic, where the model is exact: with $Hd = -g$, $f(x + d) = f(x) + g^\top d + \tfrac12 d^\top H d = f(x) + \tfrac12 g^\top d$. Since $g^\top d \lt 0$, this meets the Armijo threshold $f(x) + c\,g^\top d$ exactly when $c \le \frac12$, so damping costs nothing there.

Each step still forms the $n \times n$ Hessian and solves a linear system: $O(n^3)$ operations and $O(n^2)$ memory, out of reach for $n$ in the millions.

## Quasi-Newton methods: BFGS

Can we keep Newton's step without the Hessian? A quasi-Newton method uses $d_k = -H_k g_k$, where $H_k$ approximates the inverse Hessian. It is built from gradients only and corrected after every step. The information available after a step is the pair

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_k &= x_{k+1} - x_k , \\
y_k &= g_{k+1} - g_k .
\end{aligned}
$$

Along the step the true Hessian maps $s_k$ approximately to $y_k$, as the slope of a secant does in one variable. The **secant equation** asks the new approximation to reproduce this exactly, $H_{k+1}y_k = s_k$. A symmetric rank-one correction can satisfy it but may lose positive definiteness, since it can subtract a rank-one term; the rank-two **BFGS** update (Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb and Shanno, 1970) keeps it ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 15). With $\rho_k = 1/(y_k^\top s_k)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_{k+1} ={}& \big(I - \rho_k s_k y_k^\top\big)\, H_k\, \big(I - \rho_k y_k s_k^\top\big) \\
&+ \rho_k s_k s_k^\top .
\end{aligned}
$$

The secant equation is a one-line check: $(I - \rho y s^\top)y = y - \rho y(s^\top y) = 0$, so the first term vanishes on $y$, and $\rho s s^\top y = s$. The update keeps $H$ positive definite exactly when $y_k^\top s_k \gt 0$. If $\rho \gt 0$ and $v \ne 0$, put $u = (I - \rho y s^\top)v$; then $v^\top H_{k+1} v = u^\top H_k u + \rho\,(s^\top v)^2 \ge 0$. Both terms vanish only if $s^\top v = 0$ and $u = 0$, but $s^\top v = 0$ makes $u = v \ne 0$, so the sum is positive. If $y^\top s \le 0$, no positive definite matrix can satisfy the secant equation, because $y^\top H y = y^\top s$ would have to be non-positive. For a $\mu$-strongly convex function, adding its lower parabolas at $x_k$ towards $x_{k+1}$ and at $x_{k+1}$ towards $x_k$ gives $y_k^\top s_k \ge \mu\lVert s_k\rVert^2 \gt 0$. In general the Wolfe curvature condition guarantees it: with $s_k = t\,d_k$, $y_k^\top s_k \ge t\,(c_2 - 1)\, g_k^\top d_k \gt 0$, because $c_2 \lt 1$ and $g_k^\top d_k \lt 0$.

Each BFGS step costs $O(n^2)$, a few matrix–vector products and outer products. For a strongly convex $f$ with Lipschitz Hessian, BFGS with backtracking converges from any start and, near the minimum, superlinearly: the ratio $\lVert e_{k+1}\rVert/\lVert e_k\rVert$ tends to 0, though not as fast as Newton's square ([Vandenberghe, 2022](#references), the convergence result, stated there without proof). **L-BFGS** stores only the last few pairs $(s, y)$, typically 5 to 20, and applies the update implicitly, at $O(mn)$ per step for $m$ pairs. It is the default solver of scikit-learn's `LogisticRegression` (`solver='lbfgs'` in version 1.9.1) and a standard choice for smooth deterministic problems of moderate size.

Even with the best step along the gradient, found by an exact line search, gradient descent zigzags, because each step ends where the new gradient is perpendicular to the old one: the step $t$ minimizes $\varphi(t) = f(x - tg)$, and $\varphi'(t) = -\nabla f(x - tg)^\top g = 0$ there. The start is the worst case for which Boyd and Vandenberghe give the iterates in closed form (lecture 9, the quadratic example: $f = \tfrac12(x_1^2 + \gamma x_2^2)$ from $(\gamma, 1)$, here with $\gamma = 10$ and the axes turned), and $f$ falls by the factor $\big((\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)\big)^2 = 0.669$ per step. A millionfold reduction takes 35 steps, 66 with the fixed step $1/L$; the guarantee $(1 - 1/\kappa)^k$ promises 132. Newton's method takes one step.

The right panel is the opening logistic problem, where no method is exact in one step. Newton needs 5 steps to bring $f - f^*$ to about $10^{-15}$, BFGS with Armijo backtracking 11 steps to reach $10^{-10}$, gradient descent with backtracking 526 and with the fixed step $1/L$ 2,727. Per step, Newton pays an $O(n^3)$ solve, BFGS $O(n^2)$ and gradient descent $O(n)$ beyond the gradient itself; at $n = 2$ the solve is free, and at $n = 10^8$ it is impossible.

## Where it breaks

Newton's method looks for a zero of the gradient and does not care which kind. Take the non-convex function $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2 + \tfrac12x$ of the previous note and start at $x = 0.3$, where $f' = -0.592$ and $f'' = -2.92$. The function decreases to the right, but with negative curvature the Newton step $-f'/f''$ points left, uphill:

| $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x_k$ | $0.3$ | $0.0973$ | $0.12676$ | $0.12705$ |
| $f(x_k)$ | $-0.022$ | $0.030$ | $0.032$ | $0.032$ |

Pure Newton converges, quadratically, to the local maximum at $0.127$: as a root finder for $f'$, the convergence proof above needs only $\lvert f''\rvert$ bounded away from 0 near the root, not its sign. Gradient descent with step $0.05$ from the same point goes downhill to the local minimum at $0.930$. Practical codes guard against this by adding a multiple of the identity to the Hessian until it is positive definite, or by trusting the quadratic model only within a region around the current point. BFGS avoids it by construction: its $H_k$ stays positive definite, so $-H_k g_k$ is always a descent direction, and the line search makes $f$ decrease at every step, which rules out climbing to a maximum.

The analysis has other limits. Every rate here assumes exact gradients; with minibatch gradients the noise changes the picture, as the [stochastic optimizers note](/notes/ml/stochastic-and-adaptive-optimizers) shows, and BFGS pairs built from noisy gradients become unreliable. Losses with kinks, such as the hinge or the L1 penalty, break the descent lemma and need subgradient or proximal methods.

## What you should remember

- At a local minimum $\nabla f = 0$ and $\nabla^2 f \succeq 0$; for convex $f$, $\nabla f = 0$ suffices.
- Descent lemma: with $\eta = 1/L$, each step lowers $f$ by at least $\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2/(2L)$.
- Convex: $f(x_k) - f^* \le L\lVert x_0 - x^*\rVert^2/(2k)$. Strongly convex: about $\kappa\ln(1/\varepsilon)$ steps.
- Armijo backtracking finds a step with guaranteed decrease without knowing $L$.
- Newton solves $\nabla^2 f\,d = -\nabla f$: affine invariant, quadratic near a minimum, damped far from it.
- BFGS builds the inverse Hessian from gradient differences through $H_{k+1} y_k = s_k$.

## Code

Newton's method on $x - \ln x$ with and without damping, gradient descent and Newton on the elongated quadratic, four methods on the logistic loss of the opening with the guarantees checked, and Newton on a non-convex function. Exact digits can differ slightly across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

# Newton's method on f(x) = x - log x, minimum at 1: x <- 2x - x^2, and the error is squared at every step
x = 0.5
for k in range(6):
    print(k, f'{x - 1:.3e}', end=' | ')
    x = 2 * x - x * x
print()
# 0 -5.000e-01 | 1 -2.500e-01 | 2 -6.250e-02 | 3 -3.906e-03 | 4 -1.526e-05 | 5 -2.328e-10

# from x = 3 the full step lands at -3, outside the domain; backtracking (Armijo, c = 1e-4) halves it twice
f = lambda x: x - np.log(x) if x > 0 else np.inf
x = 3.0
for k in range(7):
    g, h = 1 - 1 / x, 1 / x**2
    d, t = -g / h, 1.0
    while f(x + t * d) > f(x) + 1e-4 * t * g * d:
        t /= 2
    print(k, round(x, 6), t, end=' | ')
    x += t * d
print()
# 0 3.0 0.25 | 1 1.5 1.0 | 2 0.75 1.0 | 3 0.9375 1.0 | 4 0.996094 1.0 | 5 0.999985 1.0 | 6 1.0 1.0

# an elongated quadratic: eigenvalues mu = 1 and L = 10, axes turned by 30 degrees, minimum at 0
th = np.deg2rad(30); Rot = np.array([[np.cos(th), -np.sin(th)], [np.sin(th), np.cos(th)]])
mu, L = 1.0, 10.0
A = Rot @ np.diag([mu, L]) @ Rot.T
q = lambda x: 0.5 * x @ A @ x
x0 = Rot @ np.array([2.0, 0.2])
def count(step):
    x, k = x0.copy(), 0
    while q(x) > 1e-6 * q(x0):
        g = A @ x
        x, k = x - step(g) * g, k + 1
    return k
print(count(lambda g: 1 / L), count(lambda g: 2 / (L + mu)), count(lambda g: g @ g / (g @ A @ g)),
      ((L - mu) / (L + mu)) ** 2, np.abs(x0 - np.linalg.solve(A, A @ x0)).max(), np.ceil(np.log(1e6) / -np.log(1 - mu / L)))
# 66 35 35 0.6694214876033059 2.220446049250313e-16 132.0
# steps with 1/L, with 2/(L + mu) and with exact line search; the factor per step of exact line search;
# Newton lands on the minimum in one step; the steps the strongly convex guarantee asks for

# a logistic loss with an L2 penalty on 40 deterministic points; the second feature is ten times larger
n, lam = 40, 0.01
k_ = np.arange(1, n + 1)
X = np.column_stack([2 * ((k_ * 0.6180339887498949) % 1) - 1, 10 * (2 * ((k_ * 0.7548776662466927) % 1) - 1)])
noise = (k_ * 0.4142135623730951) % 1 - 0.5
y = np.where(X[:, 0] + 0.1 * X[:, 1] + noise > 0, 1.0, -1.0)
F = lambda w: np.mean(np.maximum(-y * (X @ w), 0) + np.log1p(np.exp(-np.abs(y * (X @ w))))) + lam / 2 * w @ w
G = lambda w: -X.T @ (y / (1 + np.exp(y * (X @ w)))) / n + lam * w
def Hess(w):
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w)))
    return (X.T * (p * (1 - p))) @ X / n + lam * np.eye(2)
Lb = np.linalg.eigvalsh(X.T @ X)[-1] / (4 * n) + lam          # a global bound on the Hessian
w = np.zeros(2)
for _ in range(30):
    w = w - np.linalg.solve(Hess(w), G(w))
ws, Fs = w, F(w)
print(ws.round(4), round(Fs, 5), round(Lb, 3), round(Lb / lam), np.linalg.eigvalsh(Hess(ws)).round(4), round(np.linalg.cond(Hess(ws))))
# [3.4682 0.3803] 0.30901 7.898 790 [0.0293 3.2176] 110

def run(step, tol=1e-10):
    w, k, gaps = np.zeros(2), 0, [F(np.zeros(2)) - Fs]
    while gaps[-1] > tol:
        w, k = step(w), k + 1
        gaps.append(F(w) - Fs)
    return k, gaps
def armijo(w):
    g, t = G(w), 1.0
    while F(w - t * g) > F(w) - 1e-4 * t * g @ g:
        t /= 2
    return w - t * g
k_gd, gaps = run(lambda w: w - G(w) / Lb)
k_ar, _ = run(armijo)
k_nt, gn = run(lambda w: w - np.linalg.solve(Hess(w), G(w)))
print(k_gd, k_ar, k_nt, [f'{g:.1e}' for g in gn], [f'{gaps[i]:.1e}' for i in (10, 100, 1000)])
# 2727 526 5 ['3.8e-01', '5.3e-02', '5.2e-03', '7.7e-05', '2.0e-08', '1.3e-15'] ['2.3e-01', '6.9e-02', '3.8e-05']

# the guarantees: f_k - f* <= (1 - mu/L)^k (f_0 - f*) and f_k - f* <= L |w_0 - w*|^2 / (2k), both hold
print(all(gaps[k] <= (1 - lam / Lb) ** k * gaps[0] + 1e-15 for k in range(len(gaps))),
      all(gaps[k] <= Lb * ws @ ws / (2 * k) for k in range(1, len(gaps))),
      round(np.log(gaps[0] / 1e-10) * Lb / lam))
# True True 17431: the steps the strongly convex bound asks for, against 2727 taken

# BFGS: the inverse-Hessian approximation Hinv is updated from s = w+ - w and y = g+ - g, and satisfies Hinv y = s
def bfgs(w, tol=1e-10):
    Hinv, k = np.eye(2), 0
    while F(w) - Fs > tol:
        g = G(w); d = -Hinv @ g; t = 1.0
        while F(w + t * d) > F(w) + 1e-4 * t * g @ d:
            t /= 2
        s = t * d; yv = G(w + s) - g; rho = 1 / (yv @ s)
        V = np.eye(2) - rho * np.outer(s, yv)
        Hinv = V @ Hinv @ V.T + rho * np.outer(s, s)
        w, k = w + s, k + 1
    return k, Hinv @ yv - s
k_bf, secant = bfgs(np.zeros(2))
r = minimize(F, np.zeros(2), jac=G, method='BFGS', options={'gtol': 1e-9})
print(k_bf, np.abs(secant).max() < 1e-12, r.nit, f'{r.fun - Fs:.0e}')
# 11 True 14 0e+00: our BFGS, the secant equation after the last update, SciPy's BFGS (Wolfe line search)

# where it breaks: pure Newton on x^4 - 2x^2 + x/2 from 0.3 climbs to the local maximum; gradient descent does not
fn = lambda x: x**4 - 2 * x**2 + 0.5 * x
d1, d2 = lambda x: 4 * x**3 - 4 * x + 0.5, lambda x: 12 * x**2 - 4
x = 0.3
for k in range(4):
    print(k, round(x, 5), round(fn(x), 3), end=' | ')
    x -= d1(x) / d2(x)
x_gd = 0.3
for _ in range(200):
    x_gd -= 0.05 * d1(x_gd)
print(round(x_gd, 3), round(fn(x_gd), 3))
# 0 0.3 -0.022 | 1 0.09726 0.03 | 2 0.12676 0.032 | 3 0.12705 0.032 | 0.93 -0.517
```
