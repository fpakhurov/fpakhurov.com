The [previous note](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians) differentiated functions coordinate by coordinate and assembled the results into gradients, Jacobians and Hessians. Machine learning writes its losses in whole vectors and matrices: $\lVert Xw - y\rVert^2$, the log-likelihood of a Gaussian with covariance $\Sigma$, the cross-entropy of $\operatorname{softmax}(Wx)$. Writing such an expression with indices, differentiating entry by entry and reassembling a matrix is slow and easy to get wrong. This note first fixes what shape a derivative has, then introduces the **differential**, a calculus that works on whole matrices: compute the first-order change of the expression, rearrange it with the trace, and read the gradient off. Every standard identity is derived that way and checked against finite differences, and the gradients of least squares and logistic regression come out exactly as the machine learning notes use them.

**Notation.** Vectors are lowercase letters, written as columns, and matrices are uppercase: $x \in \mathbb R^n$ and $X \in \mathbb R^{m \times n}$ are variables; $a$, $b$, $A$, $B$ are constants. $f$ is a scalar function. $\operatorname{tr}$ is the trace, and $\langle A, B\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(A^\top B) = \sum_{ij} A_{ij} B_{ij}$ is the inner product of two matrices of the same shape, with $\lVert A\rVert_F^2 = \langle A, A\rangle$. $X^{-\top} = (X^{-1})^\top = (X^\top)^{-1}$. $E_{ij}$ is the matrix with a 1 in position $(i, j)$ and zeros elsewhere, $\mathbf 1$ the vector of ones, and $\odot$ the elementwise product. $dX$ is a small change of $X$, a matrix of the same shape, and $df$ the first-order change of $f$ that it causes.

## Baseline: index by index

The quadratic form $x^\top Ax = \sum_i \sum_j x_i A_{ij} x_j$ can be differentiated entry by entry. The derivative with respect to $x_k$ collects the terms with $i = k$, which give $\sum_j A_{kj} x_j$, and the terms with $j = k$, which give $\sum_i x_i A_{ik}$:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\, x^\top Ax = (Ax)_k + (A^\top x)_k, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \nabla_{x}\, x^\top Ax = (A + A^\top)x .
$$

This works here, and it stops working quickly. The determinant needs its cofactor expansion, the inverse is defined only implicitly, and a product of four matrices is a sum over four indices. Even when the entries are right, they must be put back into a matrix, and that raises the question of which shape the result should have.

## Layout: what shape a derivative has

Texts disagree on whether the derivative of a scalar with respect to a column vector is a column or a row. This course, like every course on the site, uses one convention:

- for a scalar $f$ of a vector $x$, the gradient $\partial f/\partial x = \nabla f(x)$ is a **column** with the shape of $x$;
- for a scalar $f$ of a matrix $X$, $\partial f/\partial X$ is a matrix **with the shape of $X$**, $\big(\partial f/\partial X\big)_{ij} = \partial f/\partial X_{ij}$;
- for a vector function $f$ of $x$, the **Jacobian** $\partial f/\partial x$ is $m \times n$, outputs × inputs.

For gradients this is the **denominator layout**: the derivative is laid out like the variable in the denominator. For Jacobians it is the **numerator layout**, so the gradient of a scalar is the transpose of its $1 \times n$ Jacobian. It is the convention of the [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations), where $\nabla_{w} = 2X^\top(Xw - y)$ is a column, of the [logistic regression note](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs), where $\nabla = X^\top(p - y)$, and of the [adjoints of backpropagation](/notes/ml/backpropagation#the-chain-rule-in-matrix-form), which have the shapes of their variables; the Matrix Cookbook uses it for scalars too ([Petersen and Pedersen, 2012](#references)). The reason to prefer it: the update $W \leftarrow W - \eta\, \partial L/\partial W$ only makes sense when $\partial L/\partial W$ has the shape of $W$. A source written in the pure numerator layout gives row-vector gradients and carries extra transposes, for example $\partial(a^\top x)/\partial x = a^\top$. That one formula is a quick test of which layout a source uses.

## The differential method

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> The first-order change of a scalar function under a small change $dX$ is linear in $dX$, and every linear function of a matrix is an inner product with a fixed matrix of the same shape. Write the change as $df = \operatorname{tr}(G^\top dX)$, using only a few rules for differentials and the cyclic property of the trace, and $G$ is the gradient. No index is ever written.</aside>

The **differential** $df$ is the linear part of the change: $f(X + dX) = f(X) + df + o(\lVert dX\rVert)$. The idea of the method: the linear part has one coefficient per entry of $dX$, and the trace inner product arranges those coefficients into a matrix:

$$
\boxed{\; df = \operatorname{tr}\big(G^\top dX\big) \ \text{ for every } dX \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial X} = G \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
df &= \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial f}{\partial X_{ij}}\, dX_{ij} && \text{the linear part, one coordinate at a time} \\
&= \sum_{i,j} \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial X}\Big)_{ij} dX_{ij} && \text{the layout convention} \\
&= \operatorname{tr}\Big(\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial X}\Big)^{\!\top} dX\Big) && \operatorname{tr}(A^\top B) = \textstyle\sum_{ij} A_{ij} B_{ij} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Conversely, if $df = \operatorname{tr}(G^\top dX)$ for every $dX$, then $dX = E_{ij}$ gives $G_{ij} = \partial f/\partial X_{ij}$. For a vector variable the same statement reads $df = g^\top dx \Leftrightarrow \nabla f = g$.

Differentials follow a few rules, each obtained by expanding $f(X + dX)$ and dropping products of two small changes:

| Rule | Why |
| --- | --- |
| $dA = 0$ for a constant $A$ | it does not change |
| $d(\alpha X + \beta Y) = \alpha\, dX + \beta\, dY$ | linearity |
| $d(XY) = (dX)\,Y + X\,(dY)$ | $(X + dX)(Y + dY) - XY = (dX)Y + X(dY) + (dX)(dY)$, and the last term is second order |
| $d(X^\top) = (dX)^\top$, $\ d\operatorname{tr}X = \operatorname{tr}dX$ | transposing and the trace are linear |
| $d\,\varphi(x) = \varphi'(x) \odot dx$ for an elementwise $\varphi$ | one variable per entry |

Three facts about the trace do the rearranging: a scalar is its own trace, $\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)$ (so $\operatorname{tr}(ABC) = \operatorname{tr}(CAB) = \operatorname{tr}(BCA)$, the **cyclic property**), and $\operatorname{tr}(A^\top) = \operatorname{tr}A$. The recipe: compute $df$ with the rules, move $dX$ to the right end of a trace with the cyclic property so that $df = \operatorname{tr}(M\, dX)$, and read off $\partial f/\partial X = M^\top$.

## Linear and quadratic forms

The idea: the differential of a linear form is the form applied to $dx$, and a quadratic form contributes one term for each of the two places where $x$ appears.

$$
\boxed{\; \frac{\partial\, a^\top x}{\partial x} = a, \qquad \frac{\partial\, x^\top Ax}{\partial x} = (A + A^\top)\,x \ \ \big(= 2Ax \text{ for a symmetric } A\big) \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(a^\top x) &= a^\top dx && a \text{ is constant} \\
d(x^\top Ax) &= (dx)^\top Ax + x^\top A\, dx && \text{the product rule} \\
&= x^\top A^\top dx + x^\top A\, dx && \text{a scalar equals its transpose} \\
&= \big((A + A^\top)x\big)^\top dx && \text{collect, and read off the gradient.}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example.** $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}$ and $x = (1, 2)$: $x^\top Ax = x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2 = 17$, and $(A + A^\top)x = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 6\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} = (6, 14)$. The partial derivatives $2x_1 + 2x_2 = 6$ and $2x_1 + 6x_2 = 14$ agree. The shortcut $2Ax = (10, 12)$ would be wrong: it needs a symmetric $A$.

## Least squares

The idea: go through the residual $r = Ax - b$. The squared norm changes by $2r^\top dr$, and the residual changes by $A\, dx$.

$$
\boxed{\; \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\lVert Ax - b\rVert^2 = 2A^\top(Ax - b), \qquad \nabla^2 = 2A^\top A \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(r^\top r) &= (dr)^\top r + r^\top dr = 2\,r^\top dr && \text{the product rule; a scalar equals its transpose} \\
&= 2\,r^\top A\, dx && dr = A\, dx \\
&= \big(2A^\top r\big)^\top dx && \text{read off the gradient} \\
d\big(2A^\top r\big) &= 2A^\top A\, dx && \text{the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient.}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example.** With the same $A$, $b = (1, 1)$ and $x = (1, 2)$: $Ax = (5, 6)$, $r = (4, 5)$, $\lVert r\rVert^2 = 41$ and the gradient is $2A^\top r = 2\,(4,\ 2 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot 5) = (8, 46)$.

**The check against the least-squares note.** With $A = X$, the design matrix, and $b = y$, this is the gradient $2X^\top(Xw - y)$ and the Hessian $2X^\top X$ of the [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations), and setting the gradient to zero gives its normal equations. In its four-point example, $X$ has columns $(1, 1, 1, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 2, 3)$ and $y = (1, 3, 2, 5)$. The gradient at $w = 0$ is $-2X^\top y = (-22, -44)$, and at $\hat{w} = (1.1, 1.1)$ it is $0$.

## Traces of matrix products

The idea: when the variable is a matrix, the trace already has the form $\operatorname{tr}(M\, dX)$ or can be brought to it with the cyclic property.

$$
\boxed{\; \frac{\partial \operatorname{tr}(AX)}{\partial X} = A^\top, \qquad \frac{\partial \operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X)}{\partial X} = (A + A^\top)\,X, \qquad \frac{\partial \lVert X\rVert_F^2}{\partial X} = 2X \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\operatorname{tr}(AX) &= \operatorname{tr}(A\, dX) && \text{linearity; read off } M = A \\
d\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X) &= \operatorname{tr}\big((dX)^\top A X\big) + \operatorname{tr}\big(X^\top A\, dX\big) && \text{the product rule} \\
&= \operatorname{tr}\big(X^\top A^\top dX\big) + \operatorname{tr}\big(X^\top A\, dX\big) && \operatorname{tr}(B^\top) = \operatorname{tr}B \\
&= \operatorname{tr}\big(X^\top (A + A^\top)\, dX\big) && \text{collect; } M = X^\top(A + A^\top) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The gradients are $M^\top$: $A^\top$ and $(A + A^\top)X$. The squared Frobenius norm is $\operatorname{tr}(X^\top X)$, the case $A = I$, with gradient $2X$; it is the derivative of the weight-decay penalty $\lambda\lVert W\rVert_F^2$ of [regularization](/notes/ml/regularization).

**Worked example.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4\end{pmatrix}$, $\operatorname{tr}(AX) = \sum_{ij} A_{ji} X_{ij}$, so the coefficient of $X_{12}$ is $A_{21} = 3$, which is the entry $(1, 2)$ of $A^\top$. Without the convention one would guess $A$ and get 2.

## The log-determinant

The idea: factor $X + dX = X(I + X^{-1}dX)$, and use that, to first order, the determinant of a matrix close to the identity is 1 plus the trace of its difference from the identity. That fact comes first. For a small $E$,

$$
\det(I + E) = 1 + \operatorname{tr}E + O\big(\lVert E\rVert^2\big) .
$$

By the Leibniz formula $\det M = \sum_\sigma \operatorname{sgn}\sigma \prod_i M_{i\sigma(i)}$, the identity permutation contributes $\prod_i (1 + E_{ii}) = 1 + \sum_i E_{ii} + \dots$, where the dots are products of two or more entries of $E$. Every other permutation moves at least two indices, so its product contains at least two off-diagonal entries of $E$ and is second order. For invertible $X$:

$$
\boxed{\; d\det X = \det X \cdot \operatorname{tr}\big(X^{-1} dX\big), \qquad \frac{\partial \log\lvert\det X\rvert}{\partial X} = X^{-\top} \ \ \big(= X^{-1} \text{ for a symmetric } X\big) \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(X + dX) &= \det\big(X(I + X^{-1}dX)\big) && X \text{ is invertible} \\
&= \det X \cdot \det\big(I + X^{-1}dX\big) && \text{the determinant of a product} \\
&= \det X \cdot \big(1 + \operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}dX) + O(\lVert dX\rVert^2)\big) && \text{the fact above with } E = X^{-1}dX \\
d\log\lvert\det X\rvert &= \frac{d\det X}{\det X} = \operatorname{tr}\big(X^{-1}dX\big) && d\log\lvert t\rvert = dt/t \text{ for } t \ne 0 ;\ M = X^{-1} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Reading off, $\partial \det X/\partial X = \det X \cdot X^{-\top}$, the matrix of cofactors, and $\partial \log\lvert\det X\rvert/\partial X = X^{-\top}$. The product rule for determinants and their meaning as volume are in the [determinants note](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#the-determinant-how-a-map-scales-volume).

**Worked example.** $X = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0.5 & 1.5\end{pmatrix}$ has $\det X = 2.5$ and $X^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 & -0.4 \\ -0.2 & 0.8\end{pmatrix}$, so $\partial \det X/\partial X = 2.5\, X^{-\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & -0.5 \\ -1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$, the cofactors. Raising $X_{11}$ by $0.1$ raises the determinant by exactly $0.15$, to $2.65$, because a determinant is linear in each single entry. For a change of all entries, $dX = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 & 0.05 \\ -0.05 & 0.1\end{pmatrix}$, the trace is $\operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}dX) = 0.6 \cdot 0.1 + (-0.4)(-0.05) + (-0.2)(0.05) + 0.8 \cdot 0.1 = 0.15$, and the first-order prediction is $2.5 \cdot 1.15 = 2.875$. The exact value is $\det(X + dX) = 2.8875$. The difference $0.0125$ is $\det(dX)$: for $2 \times 2$ matrices, $\det(X + E) = \det X + \det X \operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}E) + \det E$ exactly, and the last term is the second-order remainder.

The log-determinant appears wherever a density is normalized or transformed: $-\tfrac12\log\det\Sigma$ in the log-likelihood of the [multivariate Gaussian](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#the-density), $\log\lvert\det J\rvert$ in [normalizing flows](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows#change-of-variables), and the evidence of [Bayesian linear regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#tools-matrix-derivatives-and-gaussian-identities) and of [Gaussian processes](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#the-gradient), whose gradients use exactly this identity and the next one.

## The inverse

The idea: differentiate the identity $XX^{-1} = I$, whose right side does not change.

$$
\boxed{\; d\big(X^{-1}\big) = -X^{-1}\,(dX)\,X^{-1}, \qquad \frac{\partial\, a^\top X^{-1}b}{\partial X} = -X^{-\top}a\,b^\top X^{-\top} \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &= dI = d\big(XX^{-1}\big) && I \text{ is constant} \\
&= (dX)\,X^{-1} + X\, d\big(X^{-1}\big) && \text{the product rule} \\
d\big(X^{-1}\big) &= -X^{-1}(dX)\,X^{-1} && \text{multiply by } X^{-1} \text{ on the left} \\
d\big(a^\top X^{-1}b\big) &= -a^\top X^{-1}(dX)\,X^{-1}b = -\operatorname{tr}\big(X^{-1}b\,a^\top X^{-1}\, dX\big) && \text{a scalar is its trace; cyclic property.}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $M = -X^{-1}ba^\top X^{-1}$, and the gradient is $M^\top = -X^{-\top}ab^\top X^{-\top}$. For $1 \times 1$ matrices the first formula is the familiar $d(1/x) = -dx/x^2$.

**Worked example.** With $X$ as above, $a = (1, 2)$ and $b = (1, 1)$: $X^{-1}b = (0.2, 0.6)$, so $a^\top X^{-1}b = 0.2 + 1.2 = 1.4$. The gradient is minus the outer product of $X^{-\top}a = (0.2, 1.2)$ and $X^{-1}b = (0.2, 0.6)$:

$$
\frac{\partial\, a^\top X^{-1}b}{\partial X} = -\begin{pmatrix} 0.2 \\ 1.2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0.2 & 0.6\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.04 & -0.12 \\ -0.24 & -0.72\end{pmatrix} .
$$

Raising $X_{22}$ by $0.01$ should therefore lower the value by about $0.0072$.

**Application: the covariance of a Gaussian by maximum likelihood.** The two identities together give the maximum-likelihood covariance in two lines. For points $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with known mean $\mu$, write $S = \sum_i (x_i - \mu)(x_i - \mu)^\top$. The idea: the log-likelihood is a log-determinant plus a trace of $\Sigma^{-1}S$, and its differential vanishes when the two terms balance:

$$
\boxed{\; \hat\Sigma = \frac1n S = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)(x_i - \mu)^\top \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell(\Sigma) &= -\tfrac n2 \log\det\Sigma - \tfrac12 \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1}(x_i - \mu) + \text{const} && \text{the Gaussian log-density, summed} \\
&= -\tfrac n2 \log\det\Sigma - \tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}\big(\Sigma^{-1}S\big) + \text{const} && \text{a scalar is its trace; cyclic property} \\
d\ell &= -\tfrac n2 \operatorname{tr}\big(\Sigma^{-1}d\Sigma\big) + \tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}\big(\Sigma^{-1}(d\Sigma)\,\Sigma^{-1}S\big) && \text{the log-determinant and the inverse} \\
&= \operatorname{tr}\Big(\big(-\tfrac n2 \Sigma^{-1} + \tfrac12 \Sigma^{-1}S\Sigma^{-1}\big)\, d\Sigma\Big) && \text{cyclic property} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The differential vanishes for every $d\Sigma$ when $\Sigma^{-1}S\Sigma^{-1} = n\Sigma^{-1}$, that is, $\Sigma = S/n$. For the three points $(1, 0)$, $(-1, 1)$ and $(0, -1)$, whose mean is $0$, $S = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ and $\hat\Sigma = S/3$. The $1/n$ rather than $1/(n - 1)$ is the bias of maximum likelihood discussed in the [estimators note](/notes/probability/estimators#the-sample-variance-and-n-1), and the same estimate is the M-step of the [EM algorithm](/notes/ml/em-algorithm) for each component.

## The softmax cross-entropy

For scores $z \in \mathbb R^K$, the probabilities are $p = \operatorname{softmax}(z)$ with $p_k = e^{z_k}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$, and the loss for a one-hot label $y$ is $L = -\sum_k y_k \log p_k$. The idea: $\log p_k$ is $z_k$ minus the log-sum-exp $\operatorname{lse}(z) = \log\sum_j e^{z_j}$, whose differential is $p^\top dz$, and the label term is linear. For a linear layer $z = Wx + b$:

$$
\boxed{\; \nabla_{z} L = p - y, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = (p - y)\,x^\top, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = p - y \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
L &= -\sum_k y_k \big(z_k - \operatorname{lse}(z)\big) = -y^\top z + \operatorname{lse}(z) && \log p_k = z_k - \operatorname{lse}(z);\ \textstyle\sum_k y_k = 1 \\
dL &= -y^\top dz + \frac{\sum_k e^{z_k} dz_k}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} && d\log u = du/u,\ d e^{z} = e^{z} dz \\
&= (p - y)^\top dz && \text{the definition of } p \\
&= (p - y)^\top (dW)\,x + (p - y)^\top db && dz = (dW)\,x + db \\
&= \operatorname{tr}\big(x\,(p - y)^\top dW\big) + (p - y)^\top db && \text{cyclic property; } M = x(p - y)^\top .
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example.** $z = (2, 1, 0.1)$ with the true class first, $y = (1, 0, 0)$: $p = (0.659, 0.242, 0.099)$, $L = -\log 0.659 = 0.417$, and $\nabla_{z} L = (-0.341, 0.242, 0.099)$. The entries sum to 0, because $\mathbf 1^\top p = \mathbf 1^\top y = 1$: adding the same constant to every score changes nothing. With the input $x = (1, -2)$, the weight gradient is the outer product $(p - y)x^\top$, with rows $(-0.341, 0.682)$, $(0.242, -0.485)$ and $(0.099, -0.197)$.

The route through the softmax Jacobian gives the same result with more work: $dp = \big(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\big) dz$, the Hessian of the log-sum-exp from the [convexity note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions), multiplied by the gradient $-y/p$ of the loss in $p$. Frameworks therefore implement softmax and cross-entropy as one fused operation, which is also more stable numerically. For a batch with the examples as the rows of $X$, $Z = XW^\top + \mathbf 1b^\top$, the same steps give $\partial L/\partial W = (P - Y)^\top X$, which is the $X^\top(P - Y)$ of the [softmax section of the logistic regression note](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#several-classes-the-softmax) with the weights stored as columns instead of rows.

## The check against logistic regression

Binary logistic regression has $p_i = \sigma(z_i)$, $z = Xw$ and $L(w) = -\sum_i \big[y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\big]$. The idea: each term has the differential $(p_i - y_i)\, dz_i$, the binary case of $p - y$, and the probabilities change by $p_i(1 - p_i)\, dz_i$:

$$
\boxed{\; \nabla L(w) = X^\top(p - y), \qquad \nabla^2 L(w) = X^\top S X, \quad S = \operatorname{diag}\big(p_i(1 - p_i)\big) \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\big[-\log\sigma(z)\big] &= -(1 - \sigma(z))\, dz, \quad d\big[-\log(1 - \sigma(z))\big] = \sigma(z)\, dz && \sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma) \\
dL &= \sum_i (p_i - y_i)\, dz_i = (p - y)^\top X\, dw && \text{both cases are } (p_i - y_i)\,dz_i;\ dz = X dw \\
d\big(X^\top(p - y)\big) &= X^\top dp = X^\top S\, dz = X^\top S X\, dw && dp_i = p_i(1 - p_i)\, dz_i .
\end{aligned}
$$

These are the gradient and the Hessian of the [logistic regression note](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs). In its worked example, one feature $x = (2, -1, 0.5)$, labels $y = (1, 0, 0)$ and $w = 1$, the residuals are $p - y = (-0.119, 0.269, 0.622)$, the gradient is $-0.196$, and the Hessian is $\sum_i p_i(1 - p_i)\, x_i^2 = 0.675$.

## What changed

| | Index by index (baseline) | The differential method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is written | every entry $\partial f/\partial X_{ij}$ as a sum over indices | one matrix expression $df$ |
| Products of matrices | a sum over every index of the product | the product rule $d(XY) = (dX)Y + X\,dY$ |
| Determinant, inverse | cofactor expansions, implicit equations | $d\log\det X = \operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}dX)$, $d(X^{-1}) = -X^{-1}dX\,X^{-1}$ |
| Shape of the result | assembled by hand | read off as $M^\top$ from $df = \operatorname{tr}(M\,dX)$ |
| Second derivatives | a second round of indices | the differential of the gradient |
| Checking | by finite differences | by finite differences, and by shapes |

## Limitations

- **Layouts differ between sources.** Copying an identity from a text in the other layout silently transposes it; check with $\partial(a^\top x)/\partial x$ and by the shapes.
- **Structured matrices.** The formulas treat all entries of $X$ as independent. When $X$ is constrained to be symmetric, the derivative with respect to its distinct entries counts each off-diagonal entry twice, and texts differ on how they write it ([Petersen and Pedersen, 2012](#references), Section 2.8). For an optimizer that updates all $n^2$ entries, the formulas here are the right ones, and for a symmetric $X$ the gradients $X^{-1}$ and $\Sigma^{-1}S\Sigma^{-1}$ are symmetric anyway.
- **Derivatives of matrix-valued functions of matrices are four-index arrays.** The method avoids them by keeping a scalar at the end of every chain, as a loss always is. Eigenvalues and singular values are differentiable only where they are distinct.
- **A formula is not an algorithm.** $X^{-1}$ and $\det X$ should not be formed explicitly in numerical code: a linear solve replaces $X^{-1}b$, and a Cholesky factorization gives $\log\det$ as twice the sum of the logarithms of its diagonal, as the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) explains.
- **For code, automatic differentiation is the tool.** The derivations give closed forms, Hessians for Newton's method, and a way to check what autograd returns.

## What you should remember

- Gradients have the shape of the variable; Jacobians are outputs × inputs.
- $df = \operatorname{tr}(M\, dX) \Rightarrow \partial f/\partial X = M^\top$. Use the product rule for differentials, then the cyclic property of the trace.
- The identities, each derived in the note:

| $f$ | $\partial f/\partial x$ or $\partial f/\partial X$ |
| --- | --- |
| $a^\top x$ | $a$ |
| $x^\top Ax$ | $(A + A^\top)x$ |
| $\lVert Ax - b\rVert^2$ | $2A^\top(Ax - b)$ |
| $\operatorname{tr}(AX)$ | $A^\top$ |
| $\operatorname{tr}(X^\top AX)$ | $(A + A^\top)X$ |
| $\log\lvert\det X\rvert$ | $X^{-\top}$ |
| $a^\top X^{-1}b$ | $-X^{-\top}ab^\top X^{-\top}$ |
| $-y^\top \log\operatorname{softmax}(Wx + b)$ | $(p - y)x^\top$ for $W$, $\ p - y$ for $b$ |

- Least squares: $\nabla = 2X^\top(Xw - y)$, $\nabla^2 = 2X^\top X$. Logistic regression: $\nabla = X^\top(p - y)$, $\nabla^2 = X^\top SX$. The Gaussian covariance by maximum likelihood is $S/n$.

## Code

Every identity of the note is checked against central differences, with a helper that perturbs one entry of a vector or a matrix at a time; the random matrices are fixed by a seed. Digits in the last places may vary across platforms.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def num_grad(f, X, h=1e-6):
    """Central differences in every entry of X; the result has the shape of X."""
    G = np.zeros_like(X, dtype=float)
    for idx in np.ndindex(X.shape):
        E = np.zeros_like(X, dtype=float); E[idx] = h
        G[idx] = (f(X + E) - f(X - E)) / (2 * h)
    return G

def check(name, f, X, G):
    print(f'{name:14s} max error {np.abs(num_grad(f, X) - G).max():.1e}')

# the worked examples
A = np.array([[1., 2], [0, 3]]); x = np.array([1., 2]); b = np.array([1., 1])
print(x @ A @ x, (A + A.T) @ x, 2 * A @ x, 2 * A.T @ (A @ x - b))   # 17.0 [ 6. 14.] [10. 12.] [ 8. 46.]
X = np.array([[2., 1], [0.5, 1.5]]); Xi = np.linalg.inv(X); dX = np.array([[0.1, 0.05], [-0.05, 0.1]])
print(np.linalg.det(X).round(6), (np.linalg.det(X) * Xi.T).round(6), np.trace(Xi @ dX).round(6),
      (np.linalg.det(X) * (1 + np.trace(Xi @ dX))).round(6), np.linalg.det(X + dX).round(6), np.linalg.det(dX).round(6))
# 2.5 [[ 1.5 -0.5] [-1.   2. ]] 0.15 2.875 2.8875 0.0125
a = np.array([1., 2]); print(round(a @ Xi @ b, 6), (-Xi.T @ np.outer(a, b) @ Xi.T).round(6))   # 1.4 [[-0.04 -0.12] [-0.24 -0.72]]

# every identity on random matrices
n = 4
a, b, x = rng.normal(size=n), rng.normal(size=n), rng.normal(size=n)
A, B = rng.normal(size=(n, n)), rng.normal(size=(n, n))
X = rng.normal(size=(n, n)) + n * np.eye(n)                         # comfortably invertible
check('a^T x', lambda v: a @ v, x, a)
check('x^T A x', lambda v: v @ A @ v, x, (A + A.T) @ x)
check('|Ax - b|^2', lambda v: np.sum((A @ v - b) ** 2), x, 2 * A.T @ (A @ x - b))
check('tr(AX)', lambda M: np.trace(A @ M), X, A.T)
check('tr(X^T A X)', lambda M: np.trace(M.T @ A @ M), X, (A + A.T) @ X)
check('log|det X|', lambda M: np.linalg.slogdet(M)[1], X, np.linalg.inv(X).T)
Xi = np.linalg.inv(X)
check('a^T X^-1 b', lambda M: a @ np.linalg.solve(M, b), X, -Xi.T @ np.outer(a, b) @ Xi.T)
# each line prints a max error between 3e-11 and 7e-09: agreement to the accuracy of central differences
# d(X^-1) itself: the first-order change of the inverse matches -X^-1 dX X^-1
E = 1e-6 * rng.normal(size=(n, n))
print(f'{np.abs(np.linalg.inv(X + E) - Xi - (-Xi @ E @ Xi)).max():.1e}')   # about 1e-13: second order in E

# softmax cross-entropy: the worked example, then a random layer
softmax = lambda z: np.exp(z - z.max()) / np.exp(z - z.max()).sum()
z = np.array([2., 1, 0.1]); y = np.array([1., 0, 0]); p = softmax(z)
print(p.round(3), round(-np.log(p[0]), 3), (p - y).round(3), np.outer(p - y, [1, -2]).round(3))
# [0.659 0.242 0.099] 0.417 [-0.341  0.242  0.099] [[-0.341  0.682] [ 0.242 -0.485] [ 0.099 -0.197]]
K, d = 3, 5
W, bb, xx = rng.normal(size=(K, d)), rng.normal(size=K), rng.normal(size=d)
ce = lambda W_, b_: -np.log(softmax(W_ @ xx + b_)[1])                 # true class 1
p = softmax(W @ xx + bb); y = np.eye(K)[1]
check('CE, W', lambda M: ce(M, bb), W, np.outer(p - y, xx))
check('CE, b', lambda v: ce(W, v), bb, p - y)
# CE, W max error 2.4e-10 | CE, b max error 1.5e-10

# least squares on the four points of the least-squares note
Xd = np.column_stack([np.ones(4), [0., 1, 2, 3]]); yd = np.array([1., 3, 2, 5])
print(2 * Xd.T @ (Xd @ np.zeros(2) - yd), (2 * Xd.T @ (Xd @ np.array([1.1, 1.1]) - yd)).round(12) + 0)   # [-22. -44.] [0. 0.]

# logistic regression: the worked example of its note, then a random design
sig = lambda t: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-t))
xl, yl = np.array([2., -1, 0.5]), np.array([1., 0, 0]); pl = sig(xl)
print((pl - yl).round(3), round(xl @ (pl - yl), 3), round(np.sum(pl * (1 - pl) * xl ** 2), 3))   # [-0.119  0.269  0.622] -0.196 0.675
Xr, yr, w = rng.normal(size=(20, 3)), rng.integers(0, 2, 20).astype(float), rng.normal(size=3)
nll = lambda v: -np.sum(yr * np.log(sig(Xr @ v)) + (1 - yr) * np.log(1 - sig(Xr @ v)))
pr = sig(Xr @ w)
check('logistic grad', nll, w, Xr.T @ (pr - yr))
grad_l = lambda v: Xr.T @ (sig(Xr @ v) - yr)
H_num = np.column_stack([(grad_l(w + 1e-6 * e) - grad_l(w - 1e-6 * e)) / 2e-6 for e in np.eye(3)])   # Jacobian of the gradient
print(f'{"logistic Hess":14s} max error {np.abs(H_num - Xr.T @ np.diag(pr * (1 - pr)) @ Xr).max():.1e}')
# logistic grad max error 2.0e-09 | logistic Hess max error 7.2e-10

# the Gaussian covariance by maximum likelihood: the gradient vanishes at S / n
P3 = np.array([[1., 0], [-1, 1], [0, -1]]); S = P3.T @ P3
ell = lambda Sig: -1.5 * np.linalg.slogdet(Sig)[1] - 0.5 * np.trace(np.linalg.solve(Sig, S))
G_at = lambda Sig: -1.5 * np.linalg.inv(Sig) + 0.5 * np.linalg.inv(Sig) @ S @ np.linalg.inv(Sig)
check('Gaussian ell', ell, np.eye(2), G_at(np.eye(2)))   # max error 2.6e-10
print((S / 3).round(3), np.abs(num_grad(ell, S / 3)).max() < 1e-8)   # [[ 0.667 -0.333] [-0.333  0.667]] True
```
