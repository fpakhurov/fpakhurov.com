Stand at the point $(1, 1)$ of the function

$$
q(x) = x_1^2 + x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2 .
$$

Walking along the $x_1$-axis, $q$ rises at the rate $2x_1 + x_2 = 3$; along the $x_2$-axis, at the rate $x_1 + 4x_2 = 5$. These are the **partial derivatives** $\partial_1 q$ and $\partial_2 q$: the ordinary derivatives of $t \mapsto q(x + te_i)$ at $t = 0$, where $e_1, e_2$ are the standard basis vectors. But a loss is minimized by stepping in some direction, and almost never along an axis. How fast does $q$ change along $(0.6, 0.8)$? Which direction is steepest?

A loss in machine learning is a function of a whole vector of weights, and a layer of a network maps a vector to a vector. The [previous note](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series) approximated a function of one variable by its tangent line; the same idea, with vectors and matrices in place of numbers, answers these questions.

## The gradient: the best linear approximation

Partial derivatives alone do not describe a function near a point. The function $g(x_1, x_2) = x_1x_2/(x_1^2 + x_2^2)$, extended by $g(0) = 0$, vanishes on both axes, so both of its partial derivatives at the origin are 0. Yet on the diagonal $x_1 = x_2 \ne 0$ it equals $\tfrac12$: $g$ is not even continuous at the origin, and no plane approximates it there.

The definition that works is the one of the previous note, with the increment $h$ now a vector. A function $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$ is **differentiable** at $x$ when, near $x$, it equals a linear function of $h$ plus an error small compared with the length $\lVert h\rVert$. A linear function $\ell$ of $h$ is an inner product $\langle a, h\rangle = a^\top h$ with a fixed vector $a$: by linearity $\ell(h) = \sum_i h_i\,\ell(e_i)$, so $a_i = \ell(e_i)$. That vector is the **gradient** $\nabla f(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) &= f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle + r(h), \\
\frac{r(h)}{\lVert h\rVert} &\to 0 \quad \text{as } h \to 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

We write $r(h) = o(\lVert h\rVert)$. Taking $h = te_i$ shows that the $i$-th entry of $\nabla f(x)$ is $\partial_i f(x)$, so the gradient is the column of partial derivatives, $\nabla f = (\partial_1 f, \dots, \partial_n f)$. For the quadratic $q$, $\nabla q(1, 1) = (3, 5)$.

Differentiability asks for more than the existence of the partial derivatives, as $g$ shows. A convenient sufficient condition is that all partial derivatives exist near $x$ and are continuous at $x$. Every polynomial in this note, and every smooth loss, meets it.

<details>
<summary>Proof: continuous partial derivatives give differentiability</summary>

Change one coordinate at a time. With $h^{(i)} = (h_1, \dots, h_i, 0, \dots, 0)$ and $h^{(0)} = 0$, the one-variable [mean value theorem](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series#taylor-polynomials-and-the-remainder) gives, for some $\theta_i \in (0, 1)$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) - f(x) &= \textstyle\sum_i \big(f(x + h^{(i)}) - f(x + h^{(i-1)})\big) \\
&= \textstyle\sum_i \partial_if(x + h^{(i-1)} + \theta_ih_ie_i)\,h_i .
\end{aligned}
$$

Each evaluation point is within $\lVert h\rVert$ of $x$, so by continuity at $x$ each $\partial_if$ there differs from $\partial_if(x)$ by at most any $\epsilon > 0$ once $h$ is small. The error against $\langle\nabla f(x), h\rangle$ is then at most $\epsilon\sum_i\lvert h_i\rvert \le \epsilon\sqrt n\,\lVert h\rVert$, which is $o(\lVert h\rVert)$.

</details>

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Near a point, a differentiable function of many variables is a linear map plus an error of smaller order: an inner product with the gradient for a scalar function, multiplication by the Jacobian for a vector function.</aside>

## Directional derivatives

The **directional derivative** along a unit vector $u$ is the rate of change of $f$ when walking from $x$ along $u$, $D_uf(x) = \lim_{t \to 0}\big(f(x + tu) - f(x)\big)/t$. Put $h = tu$ into the definition of differentiability and divide by $t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(x + tu) - f(x)}{t} &= \langle \nabla f(x), u\rangle + \frac{r(tu)}{t} \\
&\to \langle \nabla f(x), u\rangle .
\end{aligned}
$$

The remainder term vanishes because $\lvert r(tu)/t\rvert = \lvert r(tu)\rvert/\lVert tu\rVert$ for a unit $u$. So

$$
D_uf(x) = \langle \nabla f(x), u\rangle .
$$

**Example.** For $q$ at $(1, 1)$ and $u = (0.6, 0.8)$, $D_uq = 3 \cdot 0.6 + 5 \cdot 0.8 = 5.8$. A central difference with $t = 10^{-3}$, $\big(q(x + tu) - q(x - tu)\big)/(2t)$, gives $5.8$ to twelve digits. For a quadratic the central difference is exact up to rounding: $t \mapsto q(x + tu)$ is a quadratic in $t$, its third derivative is 0, and so is the error term $\tfrac16 f'''(\xi)h^2$ of the [central difference](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series#truncation-error).

## The gradient points uphill, across the level sets

Two geometric facts make the gradient the central object of optimization, and both come from the formula $D_uf = \langle \nabla f, u\rangle$: an inner product with a fixed vector is largest along that vector and zero across it.

**Steepest ascent.** Assume $\nabla f(x) \ne 0$. For every unit vector $u$, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_uf(x) &= \langle \nabla f(x), u\rangle \\
&\le \lVert \nabla f(x)\rVert\, \lVert u\rVert = \lVert \nabla f(x)\rVert .
\end{aligned}
$$

Equality in [Cauchy–Schwarz](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality#the-cauchyschwarz-inequality) needs $u = t\,\nabla f(x)$; then $\langle\nabla f, u\rangle = t\lVert\nabla f\rVert^2$ equals $+\lVert\nabla f\rVert$ only for $t > 0$, which for a unit vector means $u = \nabla f/\lVert \nabla f\rVert$. So the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is the rate of ascent. The same argument with $-u$ shows that the steepest descent is along $-\nabla f$, at rate $-\lVert \nabla f\rVert$: the step direction of [gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#which-way-is-downhill).

**Perpendicular to the level sets.** A **level set** of $f$ is $\{x : f(x) = c\}$, a contour line in two dimensions. Let $\gamma(t)$ be a differentiable curve that stays in the level set, with $\gamma(0) = x$ and velocity $\gamma'(0) = v$, a tangent vector of the level set at $x$. Since $f$ is constant along the curve,

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &= \frac{f(\gamma(t)) - f(x)}{t} \\
&= \Big\langle \nabla f(x), \frac{\gamma(t) - x}{t}\Big\rangle + \frac{r(\gamma(t) - x)}{t} \\
&\to \langle \nabla f(x), v\rangle .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line is differentiability with $h = \gamma(t) - x$. In the limit the difference quotient of $\gamma$ tends to $v$, and the remainder term tends to 0, because $\lVert \gamma(t) - x\rVert/\lvert t\rvert$ stays bounded while $r(h)/\lVert h\rVert \to 0$. So the gradient is orthogonal to every tangent direction of the level set.

**Example.** The level set $q = 4$ passes through $(1, 1)$. The gradient there is $(3, 5)$, with length $\sqrt{34} = 5.831$, the largest rate of increase in any direction; the direction $(0.6, 0.8)$ of the previous example, close to the gradient's direction $(0.514, 0.857)$, gave a little less, $5.8$. The tangent direction $(5, -3)/\sqrt{34}$ gives $D = (15 - 15)/\sqrt{34} = 0$: to first order $q$ does not change along the contour.

## The Jacobian of a vector function

A vector function $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$, with components $f_1, \dots, f_m$, is differentiable at $x$ when it is approximated by a linear map, now an $m \times n$ matrix $J_f(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) &= f(x) + J_f(x)\, h + o(\lVert h\rVert), \\
\big(J_f(x)\big)_{ij} &= \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Row $i$ of the **Jacobian** is $\nabla f_i^\top$, the gradient of the $i$-th output; column $j$ says how all outputs respond to the $j$-th input. The shape is outputs × inputs, as for the matrix of any linear map in the [linear maps note](/notes/math/vectors-and-linear-maps). For a scalar function the Jacobian is the $1 \times n$ row $\nabla f^\top$.

Two Jacobians make up a neural network. A linear layer $z = Wh + b$ has Jacobian $W$ with respect to $h$, and an elementwise activation $\varphi$ has the diagonal Jacobian $\operatorname{diag}\big(\varphi'(z)\big)$.

**Example.** The map $F(x_1, x_2) = (x_1^2 - x_2^2,\ 2x_1x_2)$, the squaring of the complex number $x_1 + ix_2$, has

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_F(x) &= \begin{pmatrix} 2x_1 & -2x_2 \\ 2x_2 & 2x_1 \end{pmatrix}, \\
J_F(1, 0.5) &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second matrix is $\sqrt5$ times a rotation by $\arctan(1/2) = 26.6°$, with determinant 5. Near $(1, 0.5)$, $F$ therefore turns a small square by $26.6°$, stretches it by $\sqrt5 = 2.236$ and multiplies its area by 5, the factor of the [determinant as volume](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#the-determinant-how-a-map-scales-volume).

For the square of side $0.2$ centred at $(1, 0.5)$ the exact area of the curved image is $0.2011$, against $5 \cdot 0.04 = 0.2$ for the linear map. The exact area is the integral of $\lvert\det J_F\rvert = 4(x_1^2 + x_2^2)$ over the square, by the [change of variables](/notes/math/multiple-integrals#the-change-of-variables-formula) for the map $F$, one-to-one there. For side $0.1$ the ratio of areas is $5.007$. It is the average of the continuous $\lvert\det J_F\rvert$ over the square, so it tends to $\lvert\det J_F(1, 0.5)\rvert = 5$ as the square shrinks. This local area factor is the $\lvert\det J\rvert$ of the change of variables in the [multiple integrals note](/notes/math/multiple-integrals).

## The chain rule: a product of Jacobians

Composing two maps composes their linear approximations, and the composition of linear maps is the product of their matrices. For $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ differentiable at $x$ and $g: \mathbb R^m \to \mathbb R^p$ differentiable at $y = f(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J_{g \circ f}(x) &= J_g\big(f(x)\big)\, J_f(x), \\
(p \times n) &= (p \times m)(m \times n) .
\end{aligned}
$$

To prove it, let $k = f(x + h) - f(x) = J_fh + r_f(h)$ be the step that $h$ causes in the input of $g$, with both Jacobians at their points. Differentiability of $g$ at $y$, then the substitution of $k$, give

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(f(x + h)) - g(f(x)) &= J_gk + r_g(k) \\
&= J_gJ_fh + J_gr_f(h) + r_g(k) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term is linear in $h$, and the other two are $o(\lVert h\rVert)$. The second is at most $\lVert J_g\rVert\, \lVert r_f(h)\rVert$, where $\lVert J_g\rVert$ is the largest stretch of the matrix, its spectral norm. For the third, $\lVert k\rVert \le \lVert J_f\rVert\, \lVert h\rVert + \lVert r_f(h)\rVert$, so $\lVert k\rVert/\lVert h\rVert$ stays bounded and $k \to 0$, while $r_g(k)/\lVert k\rVert \to 0$ (the term is 0 when $k = 0$). The linear part of the composition is therefore $J_gJ_f$.

When the last function is a scalar loss $\ell: \mathbb R^m \to \mathbb R$, its Jacobian is the row $\nabla\ell^\top$, and transposing the chain rule gives

$$
\nabla(\ell \circ f)(x) = J_f(x)^\top\, \nabla\ell\big(f(x)\big) .
$$

For a network, a chain of layers, the gradient of the loss is the gradient at the output multiplied by the transposed Jacobians of the layers, from the last to the first. Multiplying in that order keeps every intermediate result a vector and never forms a Jacobian as a matrix; this is reverse-mode differentiation. The [backpropagation note](/notes/ml/backpropagation#the-chain-rule-in-matrix-form) writes it out for linear layers and activations, with shapes, and explains [why it costs about one forward pass](/notes/ml/backpropagation#why-reverse-mode-costs-about-one-forward-pass); [autograd in PyTorch](/notes/deep-learning/pytorch-and-autograd#the-computational-graph) records the chain as a graph and runs it automatically.

**Example.** Take $F$ from the previous section and $\ell(v) = v_1 + v_2^2$. At $x = (1, 0.5)$, $v = F(x) = (0.75, 1)$ and $\nabla\ell(v) = (1, 2v_2) = (1, 2)$, so

$$
\nabla(\ell \circ F)(1, 0.5) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The direct route agrees. The composition is $x_1^2 - x_2^2 + 4x_1^2x_2^2$, with partial derivatives $2x_1 + 8x_1x_2^2 = 4$ and $-2x_2 + 8x_1^2x_2 = 3$.

## The Hessian and the second-order Taylor expansion

The **Hessian** $\nabla^2 f(x)$ collects the second partial derivatives, $\big(\nabla^2 f\big)_{ij} = \partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j$; it is the Jacobian of the gradient. When the second partial derivatives are continuous, the order of differentiation does not matter (Schwarz's theorem), and the Hessian is **symmetric**. Continuity is needed. For $f = x_1x_2(x_1^2 - x_2^2)/(x_1^2 + x_2^2)$, extended by 0 at the origin, $\partial_1 f(0, x_2) = \lim_h f(h, x_2)/h = -x_2$ and $\partial_2 f(x_1, 0) = x_1$, so the two mixed partial derivatives at the origin are $-1$ and $1$ ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 2).

<details>
<summary>Proof: Schwarz's theorem</summary>

Fix all coordinates but $x_i = a$ and $x_j = b$, write $f(a, b)$, and take the double difference

$$
\Delta(t) = f(a + t, b + t) - f(a + t, b) - f(a, b + t) + f(a, b) .
$$

With $\varphi(s) = f(s, b + t) - f(s, b)$, $\Delta = \varphi(a + t) - \varphi(a)$. The mean value theorem in $s$, then in the second argument, gives $\Delta = t\big(\partial_1f(\xi, b + t) - \partial_1f(\xi, b)\big) = t^2\,\partial_2\partial_1f(\xi, \eta)$, with $(\xi, \eta)$ within $t$ of $(a, b)$ in each coordinate. Grouping the four terms the other way, with $\psi(s) = f(a + t, s) - f(a, s)$, gives $\Delta = t^2\,\partial_1\partial_2f(\xi', \eta')$. Dividing by $t^2$ and letting $t \to 0$, continuity of both mixed derivatives makes them equal at $(a, b)$.

</details>

For $f$ with continuous second derivatives, the expansion adds a quadratic form to the linear approximation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) = f(x) &+ \langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle \\
&+ \tfrac12\, h^\top \nabla^2 f(x)\, h + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

To see why, restrict $f$ to the segment from $x$ to $x + h$, $\phi(t) = f(x + th)$, and use the one-variable theorem. The chain rule gives $\phi'(t) = \sum_i \partial_i f(x + th)\, h_i$, and applied once more to each $\partial_i f$,

$$
\phi''(t) = h^\top \nabla^2 f(x + th)\, h .
$$

Taylor with $n = 1$ gives $\phi(1) = \phi(0) + \phi'(0) + \tfrac12\phi''(\tau)$ for some $\tau \in (0, 1)$, that is,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) = f(x) &+ \langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle \\
&+ \tfrac12\, h^\top \nabla^2 f(x + \tau h)\, h .
\end{aligned}
$$

Replacing the Hessian at $x + \tau h$ by the one at $x$ changes the last term by at most $\tfrac12 \lVert \nabla^2 f(x + \tau h) - \nabla^2 f(x)\rVert\, \lVert h\rVert^2$, and the norm of the difference tends to 0 with $h$ because $\nabla^2 f$ is continuous. That error is $o(\lVert h\rVert^2)$.

A quadratic $f(x) = \tfrac12 x^\top Ax + b^\top x$ with symmetric $A$ has $\nabla f = Ax + b$ and the constant Hessian $A$, and its expansion is exact. Multiplying out, with $x^\top Ah = h^\top Ax$ because a number equals its transpose and $A = A^\top$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) &= \tfrac12x^\top Ax + x^\top Ah + \tfrac12h^\top Ah + b^\top x + b^\top h \\
&= f(x) + (Ax + b)^\top h + \tfrac12h^\top Ah .
\end{aligned}
$$

The Hessians of the two standard losses are of this kind or close to it: $2X^\top X$ for [least squares](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations) and $X^\top SX$ for [logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs); the [matrix calculus note](/notes/math/matrix-calculus) derives both.

**Example.** The cubic $c(x) = x_1^3 - 3x_1 + x_2^2$ has

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla c(x) &= \begin{pmatrix} 3x_1^2 - 3 \\ 2x_2 \end{pmatrix}, \\
\nabla^2 c(x) &= \begin{pmatrix} 6x_1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

At $x = (1.2, 0.5)$, take the step $h = (0.1, -0.2)$:

| quantity | value |
| --- | --- |
| $c(x)$ | $-1.622$ |
| $\nabla c(x)$ and $\nabla^2 c(x)$ | $(1.32, 1)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(7.2, 2)$ |
| linear approximation $c(x) + \langle \nabla c, h\rangle$ | $-1.690$ |
| quadratic term $\tfrac12 h^\top \nabla^2 c\, h$ | $0.076$ |
| second-order approximation | $-1.614$ |
| exact $c(1.3, 0.3)$ | $-1.613$ |

The linear approximation is off by $0.077$, the quadratic one by $0.001$, which is $h_1^3$: expanding $(x_1 + h_1)^3$ leaves exactly that term beyond the second order.

## Classifying critical points by the Hessian's eigenvalues

At a **critical point** $x^*$, where $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$, the linear term vanishes and the quadratic form $h^\top \nabla^2 f\, h$ decides what $f$ does nearby. By the [spectral theorem](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-spectral-theorem), the symmetric Hessian is $Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $q_i$ and real eigenvalues $\lambda_i$, so

$$
h^\top \nabla^2 f(x^*)\, h = \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i\, (q_i^\top h)^2 .
$$

Along each eigenvector, $f$ curves up or down according to the sign of its eigenvalue. The signs classify the point:

| eigenvalues of $\nabla^2 f(x^*)$ | $x^*$ is |
| --- | --- |
| all positive | a strict local minimum |
| all negative | a strict local maximum |
| some positive, some negative | a saddle point |
| some zero, the others of one sign | undecided by this test |

For the minimum, let $\lambda_{\min} > 0$ be the smallest eigenvalue. The second-order expansion at $x^*$, the spectral decomposition, and $\sum_i(q_i^\top h)^2 = \lVert h\rVert^2$ for an orthogonal $Q$ give

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x^* + h) - f(x^*) &= \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i (q_i^\top h)^2 + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) \\
&\ge \tfrac12 \lambda_{\min} \lVert h\rVert^2 + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

For small $h \ne 0$ the remainder is below $\tfrac14\lambda_{\min}\lVert h\rVert^2$, so the difference is positive. The maximum is the same argument for $-f$. At a saddle, the step $h = tq_+$ along an eigenvector with $\lambda_+ > 0$ changes $f$ by $\tfrac12\lambda_+t^2 + o(t^2) > 0$, and a step along an eigenvector with a negative eigenvalue changes it by a negative amount, so $x^*$ is neither a minimum nor a maximum.

With a zero eigenvalue the quadratic term is flat in some direction and higher-order terms decide: $x_1^4 + x_2^2$ and $-x_1^4 + x_2^2$ both have the Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(0, 2)$ at the origin, and the first has a minimum there, the second a saddle.

**Example.** The cubic $c$ has $\nabla c = 0$ where $x_1^2 = 1$ and $x_2 = 0$: at $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$. At $(1, 0)$ the Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(6, 2)$, both eigenvalues positive: a strict local minimum, with $c = -2$. At $(-1, 0)$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(-6, 2)$: a saddle with $c = 2$, a maximum along $x_1$ and a minimum along $x_2$. The minimum is only local, since $c \to -\infty$ as $x_1 \to -\infty$.

<HessianSaddleBowl />

A Hessian that is positive semidefinite everywhere makes the loss convex ([second-order condition](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-second-order-condition-a-positive-semidefinite-hessian)), and then every critical point is a global minimum: the tangent plane lies below a convex function ([first-order condition](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-first-order-condition-the-tangent-lies-below)), and at a critical point that plane is flat. The logistic loss, with Hessian $X^\top SX$, is the standard example. The eigenvalues also set the speed of first-order methods. On a quadratic, gradient descent needs a step below $2/\lambda_{\max}$ and converges at a rate set by $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$, and Newton's method rescales by the inverse Hessian, as the [unconstrained optimization note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization) shows.

The losses of neural networks are not convex. [Dauphin et al. (2014)](#references) argue, from statistical physics, random matrix theory and experiments, that the main difficulty of local methods in high dimension is the proliferation of saddle points, which create plateaus that slow training and can look like minima.

## Where it breaks: flat directions

The second-order test is silent exactly where overparameterized models live. Take the smallest such model, a product of two weights fitted to one target: $L(a, b) = (ab - 1)^2$. Every point of the hyperbola $ab = 1$ is a global minimum, with $L = 0$. The Hessian is

$$
\nabla^2 L(a, b) = \begin{pmatrix} 2b^2 & 2(2ab - 1) \\ 2(2ab - 1) & 2a^2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

At $(1, 1)$ it is $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalues 4 and 0. The zero eigenvalue points along $(1, -1)$, the direction of the hyperbola, where rescaling $a \to ca$, $b \to b/c$ leaves the product, and the loss, unchanged. At $(2, 0.5)$ the eigenvalues are $8.5$ and 0. On the whole hyperbola $2ab - 1 = 1$, and the determinant is $4a^2b^2 - 4 = 0$: the test says "undecided" at every minimum. A ReLU network has the same symmetry. Since $\max(0, cz) = c\max(0, z)$ for $c > 0$, scaling the weights and the bias into a unit by $c$ and the weights out of it by $1/c$ leaves the function unchanged, so its minima come in flat families and are never strict.

The Hessian is also too big to use directly. A network with $10^6$ weights has a Hessian with $10^{12}$ entries, 8 terabytes in double precision. Second-order methods work with Hessian–vector products instead, which cost about as much as a few gradients ([Pearlmutter, 1994](#references)). The Hessian is the Jacobian of $\nabla f$, so $\nabla^2 f(x)\,v$ is the directional derivative of $\nabla f$ along $v$, and a central difference of two gradients approximates it; the Code section computes one. And at a kink of a ReLU, a hinge loss or an $\ell_1$ norm, the gradient does not exist at all; the [convexity note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions) handles such points with subgradients.

## What you should remember

- $f(x + h) = f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle + o(\lVert h\rVert)$; partial derivatives alone do not guarantee differentiability.
- $D_uf = \langle \nabla f, u\rangle$: the gradient points uphill most steeply, with rate $\lVert \nabla f\rVert$, and is perpendicular to the level sets.
- The Jacobian is outputs × inputs; $\lvert\det J\rvert$ is the local factor by which the map scales volume.
- Chain rule: $J_{g \circ f} = J_gJ_f$; for a scalar loss, $\nabla(\ell \circ f) = J_f^\top\nabla\ell$, evaluated from the loss backwards.
- $f(x + h) \approx f(x) + \langle \nabla f, h\rangle + \tfrac12 h^\top\nabla^2 f\, h$; at a critical point the signs of the Hessian's eigenvalues tell minimum, maximum or saddle.
- A zero eigenvalue leaves the test undecided, and overparameterized models have such flat directions at every minimum.

Every derivative of the note is checked against central differences: the gradient and the directional derivative of $q$, the Jacobian and the chain rule for $F$, the area of the image of a small square, the second-order expansion and the critical points of $c$, the flat minimum of $(ab - 1)^2$, and a Hessian–vector product from two gradients. Digits in the last places may vary across platforms.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate

def num_grad(f, x, h=1e-6):
    """Central differences, one coordinate at a time."""
    E = np.eye(len(x))
    return np.array([(f(x + h * e) - f(x - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in E])

def num_jac(F, x, h=1e-6):
    """Column j is the central difference of F along e_j."""
    return np.column_stack([(F(x + h * e) - F(x - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(len(x))])

# the quadratic q: gradient, directional derivative, steepest ascent, the tangent of the level set
q = lambda x: x[0] ** 2 + x[0] * x[1] + 2 * x[1] ** 2
grad_q = lambda x: np.array([2 * x[0] + x[1], x[0] + 4 * x[1]])
x = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
u = np.array([0.6, 0.8]); tangent = np.array([5.0, -3.0]) / np.sqrt(34)
print(q(x), grad_q(x), num_grad(q, x).round(8), round(grad_q(x) @ u, 6), round(np.linalg.norm(grad_q(x)), 3), grad_q(x) @ tangent)
# 4.0 [3. 5.] [3. 5.] 5.8 5.831 0.0
angle = np.degrees(np.arccos(grad_q(x) @ x / np.linalg.norm(grad_q(x)) / np.linalg.norm(x)))
print(round(angle, 1))   # 14.0: the descent direction does not point at the minimum

# the Jacobian of F(x) = (x1^2 - x2^2, 2 x1 x2) and the chain rule with l(v) = v1 + v2^2
F = lambda x: np.array([x[0] ** 2 - x[1] ** 2, 2 * x[0] * x[1]])
J_F = lambda x: np.array([[2 * x[0], -2 * x[1]], [2 * x[1], 2 * x[0]]])
ell = lambda v: v[0] + v[1] ** 2
p = np.array([1.0, 0.5])
print(J_F(p).tolist(), np.abs(num_jac(F, p) - J_F(p)).max() < 1e-8, round(np.linalg.det(J_F(p)), 6))   # [[2.0, -1.0], [1.0, 2.0]] True 5.0
print(J_F(p).T @ np.array([1, 2 * F(p)[1]]), num_grad(lambda x: ell(F(x)), p).round(8))   # [4. 3.] [4. 3.]

# the image of a square of side s centred at p has area integral of |det J| = 4(x1^2 + x2^2) over the square
for s in (0.2, 0.1):
    area = integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: 4 * (x * x + y * y), 1 - s / 2, 1 + s / 2, 0.5 - s / 2, 0.5 + s / 2)[0]
    print(s, round(area, 4), round(area / s ** 2, 3))   # 0.2 0.2011 5.027 | 0.1 0.0501 5.007

# the cubic c: second-order Taylor at (1.2, 0.5) with step (0.1, -0.2), then its critical points
c = lambda x: x[0] ** 3 - 3 * x[0] + x[1] ** 2
grad_c = lambda x: np.array([3 * x[0] ** 2 - 3, 2 * x[1]])
hess_c = lambda x: np.array([[6 * x[0], 0.0], [0.0, 2.0]])
x0, h = np.array([1.2, 0.5]), np.array([0.1, -0.2])
lin = c(x0) + grad_c(x0) @ h; quad = lin + 0.5 * h @ hess_c(x0) @ h
print(round(c(x0), 3), round(lin, 3), round(quad, 3), round(c(x0 + h), 3), round(c(x0 + h) - lin, 3), round(c(x0 + h) - quad, 6))
# -1.622 -1.69 -1.614 -1.613 0.077 0.001
print(np.abs(num_jac(grad_c, x0) - hess_c(x0)).max() < 1e-6)   # True: the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient
for xs in (np.array([1.0, 0.0]), np.array([-1.0, 0.0])):
    lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(hess_c(xs))
    kind = 'minimum' if lam.min() > 0 else 'maximum' if lam.max() < 0 else 'saddle' if lam.min() < 0 < lam.max() else 'undecided'
    print(xs, grad_c(xs), lam, c(xs), kind)
# [1. 0.] [0. 0.] [2. 6.] -2.0 minimum | [-1.  0.] [0. 0.] [-6.  2.] 2.0 saddle

# a flat minimum: L(a, b) = (ab - 1)^2 has a zero Hessian eigenvalue along the hyperbola ab = 1
L = lambda w: (w[0] * w[1] - 1) ** 2
grad_L = lambda w: np.array([2 * (w[0] * w[1] - 1) * w[1], 2 * (w[0] * w[1] - 1) * w[0]])
for w in (np.array([1.0, 1.0]), np.array([2.0, 0.5])):
    H = num_jac(grad_L, w)
    print(w, L(w), np.linalg.eigvalsh(H).round(6) + 0.0)
# [1. 1.] 0.0 [0. 4.] | [2.  0.5] 0.0 [0.  8.5]

# a Hessian-vector product from two gradients, without forming the Hessian
v = np.array([0.3, -0.7]); eps = 1e-6
print(((grad_c(x0 + eps * v) - grad_c(x0 - eps * v)) / (2 * eps)).round(6), hess_c(x0) @ v)   # [ 2.16 -1.4 ] [ 2.16 -1.4 ]
```
