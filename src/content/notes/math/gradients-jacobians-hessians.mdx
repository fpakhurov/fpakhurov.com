The [previous note](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series) approximated a function of one variable by its tangent line and added curvature with Taylor's theorem. A loss in machine learning is a function of a whole vector of weights, and a layer of a network maps a vector to a vector. This note carries the same ideas into many dimensions. The derivative of a scalar function becomes a vector, the **gradient**, which points uphill most steeply and crosses the level sets at right angles. The derivative of a vector function becomes a matrix, the **Jacobian**, and the chain rule becomes a product of Jacobians, which is what backpropagation computes. The second derivative becomes a symmetric matrix, the **Hessian**, whose eigenvalues tell a minimum from a maximum and from a saddle.

**Notation.** Vectors are lowercase letters, written as columns, and matrices are uppercase. $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in \mathbb R^n$, and $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is the standard basis. $\langle a, b\rangle = a^\top b$ is the dot product and $\lVert \cdot \rVert$ the Euclidean norm of the [inner products note](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality); for a matrix, $\lVert A\rVert$ is the operator norm, the largest singular value of the [SVD note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition). A function $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$ is a scalar function; a function $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ is a vector function with components $f_1, \dots, f_m$, and the codomain stated with it says which one is meant. $\partial_i f = \partial f/\partial x_i$ is a partial derivative, $\nabla f(x)$ the gradient (a column vector), $J_{f}(x)$ the Jacobian ($m \times n$) and $\nabla^2 f(x)$ the Hessian ($n \times n$). A remainder $r(h)$ is $o(\lVert h\rVert)$ if $\lVert r(h)\rVert/\lVert h\rVert \to 0$ as $h \to 0$.

## Baseline: one coordinate at a time

The simplest way to differentiate a function of several variables is to freeze all variables but one. The **partial derivative** $\partial_i f(x)$ is the ordinary derivative of $t \mapsto f(x + te_i)$ at $t = 0$. The running example of the note is a quadratic in two variables:

$$
q(x) = x_1^2 + x_1 x_2 + 2x_2^2 .
$$

Its partial derivatives are $\partial_1 q = 2x_1 + x_2$ and $\partial_2 q = x_1 + 4x_2$; at $x = (1, 1)$ they are $3$ and $5$.

Partial derivatives only look along the axes, and that is not enough. The function $g(x_1, x_2) = x_1 x_2/(x_1^2 + x_2^2)$, extended by $g(0) = 0$, vanishes on both axes, so both of its partial derivatives at $0$ are 0. Yet on the diagonal $x_1 = x_2 \ne 0$ it equals $\tfrac12$: $g$ is not even continuous at the origin, and no plane approximates it there. A derivative in several variables must describe every direction at once.

## The gradient: the best linear approximation

The idea is the one of the previous note with the increment now a vector: $f$ is **differentiable** at $x$ when, near $x$, it equals a linear function of the increment plus an error small compared with the length of the increment. A linear function of $h \in \mathbb R^n$ is a dot product with a fixed vector, and that vector is the gradient:

$$
\boxed{\; f(x + h) = f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle + r(h), \qquad \frac{r(h)}{\lVert h\rVert} \to 0 \text{ as } h \to 0 \;}
$$

Taking $h = te_i$ shows that the $i$-th entry of $\nabla f(x)$ is $\partial_i f(x)$, so the gradient is the vector of partial derivatives, $\nabla f = (\partial_1 f, \dots, \partial_n f)$. Differentiability asks for more than their existence, as the function $g$ above shows; a convenient sufficient condition is that all partial derivatives exist near $x$ and are continuous at $x$ ([Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, 2020](#references), Section 5.2). Every function in this note, and every smooth loss, meets it. For the quadratic $q$, $\nabla q(1, 1) = (3, 5)$.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> A differentiable function of many variables is, near a point, a linear map plus an error of smaller order. For a scalar function the linear map is a dot product with the gradient; for a vector function it is multiplication by the Jacobian matrix. The gradient answers "which way is uphill and how steep", the Jacobian answers "how a small region is moved and stretched", and the chain rule composes these linear maps by multiplying matrices.</aside>

## Directional derivatives

The **directional derivative** along a unit vector $u$ is the rate of change of $f$ when walking from $x$ along $u$, $D_{u} f(x) = \lim_{t \to 0} \big(f(x + tu) - f(x)\big)/t$. The idea: along the line, the linear part of $f$ changes at the rate $\langle \nabla f, u\rangle$, and the remainder is too small to matter:

$$
\boxed{\; D_{u} f(x) = \langle \nabla f(x), u\rangle \;}
$$

Put $h = tu$ into the definition of differentiability and divide by $t$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(x + tu) - f(x)}{t} &= \frac{\langle \nabla f(x), tu\rangle + r(tu)}{t} && \text{differentiability with } h = tu \\
&= \langle \nabla f(x), u\rangle + \frac{r(tu)}{\lVert tu\rVert} \cdot \frac{\lvert t\rvert}{t} && \text{linearity; } \lVert tu\rVert = \lvert t\rvert \text{ for a unit } u \\
&\to \langle \nabla f(x), u\rangle && \text{the second factor is } \pm 1 \text{, the first tends to } 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

**Worked example.** For $q$ at $(1, 1)$ and $u = (0.6, 0.8)$, $D_{u} q = 3 \cdot 0.6 + 5 \cdot 0.8 = 5.8$. A central difference with $t = 10^{-3}$, $\big(q(x + tu) - q(x - tu)\big)/(2t)$, gives $5.8$ to twelve digits, because $q$ is quadratic and the central difference is exact for quadratics up to rounding.

## The gradient points uphill, across the level sets

Two geometric facts make the gradient the central object of optimization. The idea of both: a directional derivative is a dot product, and a dot product with a fixed vector is largest along that vector and zero across it. A **level set** of $f$ is $\{x : f(x) = c\}$, a contour line in two dimensions.

$$
\boxed{\; \max_{\lVert u\rVert = 1} D_{u} f(x) = \lVert \nabla f(x)\rVert \ \text{ at } \ u = \frac{\nabla f(x)}{\lVert \nabla f(x)\rVert}, \qquad \nabla f(x) \perp \text{ the level set through } x \;}
$$

**Steepest ascent.** Assume $\nabla f(x) \ne 0$. For every unit vector $u$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{u} f(x) &= \langle \nabla f(x), u\rangle && \text{the directional derivative} \\
&\le \lVert \nabla f(x)\rVert\, \lVert u\rVert && \text{Cauchy–Schwarz} \\
&= \lVert \nabla f(x)\rVert && \lVert u\rVert = 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz holds exactly when $u$ is a nonnegative multiple of $\nabla f(x)$, which for a unit vector means $u = \nabla f/\lVert \nabla f\rVert$. The same argument with $-u$ shows that the steepest *descent* is along $-\nabla f$, at rate $-\lVert \nabla f\rVert$: the step direction of [gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#which-way-is-downhill).

**Perpendicular to the level set.** Let $\gamma(t)$ be a differentiable curve that stays in the level set $\{f = c\}$, with $\gamma(0) = x$ and velocity $\gamma'(0) = v$, a tangent vector of the level set at $x$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &= \frac{f(\gamma(t)) - f(\gamma(0))}{t} && f = c \text{ along the curve} \\
&= \Big\langle \nabla f(x), \frac{\gamma(t) - x}{t}\Big\rangle + \frac{r\big(\gamma(t) - x\big)}{t} && \text{differentiability with } h = \gamma(t) - x \\
&\to \langle \nabla f(x), v\rangle && \text{as } t \to 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

In the last line the difference quotient of $\gamma$ tends to $v$, and the remainder term tends to 0 because $\lVert \gamma(t) - x\rVert/\lvert t\rvert$ stays bounded while $r(h)/\lVert h\rVert \to 0$. So the gradient is orthogonal to every tangent direction of the level set.

**Worked example.** The level set $q = 4$ passes through $(1, 1)$, since $1 + 1 + 2 = 4$. The gradient there is $(3, 5)$ with length $\sqrt{34} = 5.831$, the largest rate of increase in any direction; the direction $(0.6, 0.8)$ of the previous example, close to the gradient's direction $(0.514, 0.857)$, gave a little less, $5.8$. The tangent direction $(5, -3)/\sqrt{34}$ gives $D = (15 - 15)/\sqrt{34} = 0$: to first order $q$ does not change along the contour.

## The Jacobian of a vector function

A vector function $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ is differentiable at $x$ when it is approximated by a linear map, now given by an $m \times n$ matrix:

$$
f(x + h) = f(x) + J_{f}(x)\, h + r(h), \qquad \big(J_{f}(x)\big)_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}(x), \qquad r(h) = o(\lVert h\rVert) .
$$

Row $i$ of the **Jacobian** is $\nabla f_i^\top$, the gradient of the $i$-th output; column $j$ is $\partial f/\partial x_j$, how all outputs respond to the $j$-th input. The shape is outputs × inputs, as for the matrix of any linear map in the [linear maps note](/notes/math/vectors-and-linear-maps). For a scalar function the Jacobian is the $1 \times n$ row $\nabla f^\top$. Two Jacobians make up a neural network: a linear layer $z = Wh + b$ has Jacobian $W$ with respect to $h$, and an elementwise activation $\varphi$ has the diagonal Jacobian $\operatorname{diag}\big(\varphi'(z)\big)$.

**Worked example.** The map $F(x_1, x_2) = (x_1^2 - x_2^2,\ 2x_1 x_2)$, squaring of the complex number $x_1 + i x_2$, has the Jacobian

$$
J_{F}(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 2x_1 & -2x_2 \\ 2x_2 & 2x_1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J_{F}(1, 0.5) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

At $(1, 0.5)$ the matrix is $\sqrt 5$ times a rotation by $\arctan(1/2) = 26.6°$, with determinant 5. Near that point $F$ therefore turns a small square by $26.6°$, stretches it by $\sqrt 5 = 2.236$ and multiplies its area by 5, the factor of the [determinant as volume](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#the-determinant-how-a-map-scales-volume). For the square of side $0.2$ centred at $(1, 0.5)$ the exact area of the curved image is $0.2011$, against $5 \cdot 0.04 = 0.2$ for the linear map; for side $0.1$ the ratio of areas is $5.007$, and it tends to $\lvert\det J\rvert = 5$ as the square shrinks. This local area factor is the $\lvert \det J\rvert$ of the change of variables in the [multiple integrals note](/notes/math/multiple-integrals).

## The chain rule: a product of Jacobians

The idea: composing two maps composes their linear approximations, and the composition of linear maps is the product of their matrices. For $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ differentiable at $x$ and $g: \mathbb R^m \to \mathbb R^p$ differentiable at $y = f(x)$:

$$
\boxed{\; J_{g \circ f}(x) = J_{g}\big(f(x)\big)\, J_{f}(x) \qquad (p \times n) = (p \times m)(m \times n) \;}
$$

Write $k = f(x + h) - f(x) = J_{f}h + r_{f}(h)$ for the step that $h$ causes in the input of $g$, with both Jacobians evaluated at their points. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(f(x + h)) - g(f(x)) &= g(y + k) - g(y) && \text{the definitions of } y, k \\
&= J_{g}k + r_{g}(k) && \text{differentiability of } g \text{ at } y \\
&= J_{g} J_{f}h + J_{g}r_{f}(h) + r_{g}(k) && \text{substitute } k .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term is linear in $h$; the other two are $o(\lVert h\rVert)$. The second is at most $\lVert J_{g}\rVert\, \lVert r_{f}(h)\rVert$. For the third, $\lVert k\rVert \le \lVert J_{f}\rVert\, \lVert h\rVert + \lVert r_{f}(h)\rVert$, so $\lVert k\rVert/\lVert h\rVert$ stays bounded and $k \to 0$, while $r_{g}(k)/\lVert k\rVert \to 0$ (the term is $0$ when $k = 0$). The linear part of the composition is therefore $J_{g} J_{f}$.

**A scalar loss at the end.** When the last function is a scalar loss $\ell: \mathbb R^m \to \mathbb R$, its Jacobian is the row $\nabla \ell^\top$, and transposing the chain rule gives

$$
\nabla(\ell \circ f)(x) = J_{f}(x)^\top\, \nabla \ell\big(f(x)\big) .
$$

For a network, a chain of layers, the gradient of the loss is the gradient at the output multiplied by the transposed Jacobians of the layers from the last to the first. Multiplying in that order keeps every intermediate result a vector and never forms a Jacobian as a matrix; this is reverse-mode differentiation. The [backpropagation note](/notes/ml/backpropagation#the-chain-rule-in-matrix-form) writes it out for linear layers and activations, with shapes, and explains [why it costs about one forward pass](/notes/ml/backpropagation#why-reverse-mode-costs-about-one-forward-pass); [autograd in PyTorch](/notes/deep-learning/pytorch-and-autograd#the-computational-graph) records the chain as a graph and runs it automatically.

**Worked example.** Take $F$ from the previous section and $\ell(v) = v_1 + v_2^2$. At $x = (1, 0.5)$, $v = F(x) = (0.75, 1)$ and $\nabla \ell(v) = (1, 2v_2) = (1, 2)$, so

$$
\nabla(\ell \circ F)(1, 0.5) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The direct route agrees. The composition is $x_1^2 - x_2^2 + 4x_1^2 x_2^2$, with partial derivatives $2x_1 + 8x_1 x_2^2 = 2 + 2 = 4$ and $-2x_2 + 8x_1^2 x_2 = -1 + 4 = 3$.

## The Hessian and the second-order Taylor expansion

The **Hessian** collects the second partial derivatives, $\big(\nabla^2 f(x)\big)_{ij} = \partial^2 f/\partial x_i \partial x_j$; it is the Jacobian of the gradient. When the second partial derivatives are continuous, the order of differentiation does not matter (Schwarz's theorem), so the Hessian is **symmetric**. The idea of the expansion: restrict $f$ to the line from $x$ to $x + h$, apply one-variable Taylor there, and read the one-variable derivatives as the gradient and the Hessian. For $f$ with continuous second derivatives:

$$
\boxed{\; f(x + h) = f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle + \tfrac12\, h^\top \nabla^2 f(x)\, h + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) \;}
$$

Let $\phi(t) = f(x + th)$. The chain rule gives $\phi'(t) = \sum_i \partial_i f(x + th)\, h_i$, and applied once more to each $\partial_i f$, $\phi''(t) = \sum_i \sum_j \partial_j \partial_i f(x + th)\, h_j h_i = h^\top \nabla^2 f(x + th)\, h$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) &= \phi(1) && \text{the definition of } \phi \\
&= \phi(0) + \phi'(0) + \tfrac12 \phi''(\tau) && \text{one-variable Taylor with } n = 1,\ \tau \in (0, 1) \\
&= f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle + \tfrac12\, h^\top \nabla^2 f(x + \tau h)\, h && \text{the derivatives of } \phi \\
&= f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle + \tfrac12\, h^\top \nabla^2 f(x)\, h + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) && \nabla^2 f \text{ is continuous.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The last step replaces the Hessian at $x + \tau h$ by the one at $x$; the difference is at most $\tfrac12 \lVert \nabla^2 f(x + \tau h) - \nabla^2 f(x)\rVert\, \lVert h\rVert^2$, and the norm of the difference tends to 0 with $h$.

A quadratic $f(x) = \tfrac12 x^\top Ax + b^\top x$ with symmetric $A$ has $\nabla f = Ax + b$ and the constant Hessian $A$, and its expansion is exact. The Hessians of the two standard losses are of this kind or close to it: $2X^\top X$ for [least squares](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations) and $X^\top S X$ for [logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs); the [matrix calculus note](/notes/math/matrix-calculus) derives both.

**Worked example.** The cubic $c(x) = x_1^3 - 3x_1 + x_2^2$ has

$$
\nabla c(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 3x_1^2 - 3 \\ 2x_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \nabla^2 c(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 6x_1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

At $x = (1.2, 0.5)$, $c = -1.622$, $\nabla c = (1.32, 1)$ and $\nabla^2 c = \operatorname{diag}(7.2, 2)$. For the step $h = (0.1, -0.2)$ the linear approximation gives $-1.622 + 0.132 - 0.2 = -1.690$, and the quadratic term adds $\tfrac12(7.2 \cdot 0.01 + 2 \cdot 0.04) = 0.076$, giving $-1.614$. The exact value is $c(1.3, 0.3) = -1.613$. The errors are $0.077$ and $0.001$; the second equals $h_1^3 = 0.001$, the cubic term that a second-order expansion leaves out.

## Classifying critical points by the Hessian's eigenvalues

At a **critical point** $x^*$, where $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$, the linear term vanishes and the quadratic form $h^\top \nabla^2 f\, h$ decides what $f$ does nearby. By the [spectral theorem](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices), the symmetric Hessian is $Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $q_i$ and real eigenvalues $\lambda_i$, so $h^\top \nabla^2 f\, h = \sum_i \lambda_i (q_i^\top h)^2$: along each eigenvector $f$ curves up or down according to the sign of its eigenvalue. The idea of the test: if every eigenvalue is positive, $f$ curves up in every direction.

$$
\boxed{\; \nabla f(x^*) = 0 \text{ and } \begin{cases} \text{all } \lambda_i > 0 & \Rightarrow \text{strict local minimum} \\ \text{all } \lambda_i < 0 & \Rightarrow \text{strict local maximum} \\ \text{some } \lambda_i > 0,\ \text{some } \lambda_j < 0 & \Rightarrow \text{saddle point} \\ \text{otherwise (a zero eigenvalue)} & \Rightarrow \text{no conclusion} \end{cases} \;}
$$

For the minimum, with $\lambda_{\min} > 0$ the smallest eigenvalue:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x^* + h) - f(x^*) &= \tfrac12\, h^\top \nabla^2 f(x^*)\, h + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) && \text{second-order Taylor, } \nabla f(x^*) = 0 \\
&= \tfrac12 \sum_i \lambda_i\, (q_i^\top h)^2 + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) && \text{the spectral decomposition} \\
&\ge \tfrac12\, \lambda_{\min} \sum_i (q_i^\top h)^2 + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) && \lambda_i \ge \lambda_{\min} \\
&= \tfrac12\, \lambda_{\min} \lVert h\rVert^2 + o\big(\lVert h\rVert^2\big) && Q \text{ is orthogonal, so it keeps lengths} \\
&> 0 \quad \text{for small } h \ne 0 && \text{the remainder is eventually below } \tfrac14 \lambda_{\min} \lVert h\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The maximum is the same argument for $-f$. At a saddle, the step $h = tq_+$ along an eigenvector with $\lambda_+ > 0$ changes $f$ by $\tfrac12 \lambda_+ t^2 + o(t^2) > 0$, and a step along $q_-$ with $\lambda_- < 0$ by a negative amount, so $x^*$ is neither a minimum nor a maximum. With a zero eigenvalue the quadratic term is flat in some direction and the higher-order terms decide: $x_1^4 + x_2^2$ and $-x_1^4 + x_2^2$ both have the Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(0, 2)$ at the origin, and the first has a minimum there, the second a saddle.

**Worked example.** The cubic $c$ has $\nabla c = 0$ where $x_1^2 = 1$ and $x_2 = 0$: at $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$. At $(1, 0)$ the Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(6, 2)$, both eigenvalues positive: a strict local minimum, with $c = -2$. At $(-1, 0)$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(-6, 2)$: a saddle with $c = 2$, a maximum along $x_1$ and a minimum along $x_2$. The minimum is only local, since $c \to -\infty$ as $x_1 \to -\infty$.

<HessianSaddleBowl />

The test is how machine learning reads its losses. A Hessian that is positive semidefinite everywhere makes the loss convex, and then every critical point is a global minimum: the [convexity note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions) proves it, and the logistic loss with Hessian $X^\top S X$ is the standard example. The eigenvalues also set the speed of first-order methods: on a quadratic, gradient descent needs a step below $2/\lambda_{\max}$ and converges at a rate set by $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$, and Newton's method rescales by the inverse Hessian, as the [unconstrained optimization note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization) shows. The losses of neural networks are not convex. [Dauphin et al. (2014)](#references) argue, from results on random functions and from experiments on small networks, that in high dimension most critical points with a high loss are saddles rather than local minima, which turns escaping saddles into a central question for optimizers.

## Limitations

- **The Hessian is big.** A network with $10^6$ weights has a Hessian with $10^{12}$ entries, too many to form or store. Second-order methods work with Hessian–vector products, which cost about as much as a few gradients ([Pearlmutter, 1994](#references)), or with approximations of the Hessian.
- **The second-order test can be silent.** A zero eigenvalue leaves the decision to higher-order terms, and overparameterized networks have many flat directions: rescaling the weights into and out of a ReLU unit, for example, leaves the function unchanged.
- **Differentiability can fail.** The ReLU, the hinge loss and the $\ell_1$ norm have kinks, where the gradient does not exist and the theory of this note does not apply; the [convexity note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions) handles them with subgradients.
- **Everything is local.** A zero gradient and a positive definite Hessian certify a local minimum only. Without convexity, nothing in this note says how good it is.

## What you should remember

- $f$ is differentiable at $x$ when $f(x + h) = f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), h\rangle + o(\lVert h\rVert)$; the gradient is the vector of partial derivatives, but partial derivatives alone do not guarantee differentiability.
- $D_{u} f = \langle \nabla f, u\rangle$. The gradient is the direction of steepest ascent, with rate $\lVert \nabla f\rVert$, and is perpendicular to the level sets.
- The Jacobian is outputs × inputs; its determinant is the local factor by which the map scales areas or volumes.
- Chain rule: $J_{g \circ f} = J_{g} J_{f}$; for a scalar loss at the end, $\nabla(\ell \circ f) = J_{f}^\top \nabla \ell$, which backpropagation evaluates from the loss backwards.
- $f(x + h) \approx f(x) + \langle \nabla f, h\rangle + \tfrac12 h^\top \nabla^2 f\, h$; at a critical point, positive eigenvalues mean a minimum, negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle, and a zero eigenvalue no conclusion.

Every derivative of the note is checked against central differences: the gradient and the directional derivative of $q$, the Jacobian and the chain rule for $F$, the second-order expansion and the critical points of $c$, the area of the image of a small square, and a Hessian–vector product computed from two gradients. Digits in the last places may vary across platforms.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate

def num_grad(f, x, h=1e-6):
    """Central differences, one coordinate at a time."""
    E = np.eye(len(x))
    return np.array([(f(x + h * e) - f(x - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in E])

def num_jac(F, x, h=1e-6):
    """Column j is the central difference of F along e_j."""
    return np.column_stack([(F(x + h * e) - F(x - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(len(x))])

# the quadratic q: gradient, directional derivative, steepest ascent, the tangent of the level set
q = lambda x: x[0] ** 2 + x[0] * x[1] + 2 * x[1] ** 2
grad_q = lambda x: np.array([2 * x[0] + x[1], x[0] + 4 * x[1]])
x = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
u = np.array([0.6, 0.8]); tangent = np.array([5.0, -3.0]) / np.sqrt(34)
print(q(x), grad_q(x), num_grad(q, x).round(8), round(grad_q(x) @ u, 6), round(np.linalg.norm(grad_q(x)), 3), grad_q(x) @ tangent)
# 4.0 [3. 5.] [3. 5.] 5.8 5.831 0.0
angle = np.degrees(np.arccos(grad_q(x) @ x / np.linalg.norm(grad_q(x)) / np.linalg.norm(x)))
print(round(angle, 1))   # 14.0: the descent direction does not point at the minimum

# the Jacobian of F(x) = (x1^2 - x2^2, 2 x1 x2) and the chain rule with l(v) = v1 + v2^2
F = lambda x: np.array([x[0] ** 2 - x[1] ** 2, 2 * x[0] * x[1]])
J_F = lambda x: np.array([[2 * x[0], -2 * x[1]], [2 * x[1], 2 * x[0]]])
ell = lambda v: v[0] + v[1] ** 2
p = np.array([1.0, 0.5])
print(J_F(p), np.abs(num_jac(F, p) - J_F(p)).max() < 1e-8, round(np.linalg.det(J_F(p)), 6))   # [[ 2. -1.] [ 1.  2.]] True 5.0
print(J_F(p).T @ np.array([1, 2 * F(p)[1]]), num_grad(lambda x: ell(F(x)), p).round(8))   # [4. 3.] [4. 3.]

# the image of a square of side s centred at p has area integral of |det J| = 4(x1^2 + x2^2) over the square
for s in (0.2, 0.1):
    area = integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: 4 * (x * x + y * y), 1 - s / 2, 1 + s / 2, 0.5 - s / 2, 0.5 + s / 2)[0]
    print(s, round(area, 4), round(area / s ** 2, 3))   # 0.2 0.2011 5.027 | 0.1 0.0501 5.007

# the cubic c: second-order Taylor at (1.2, 0.5) with step (0.1, -0.2), then its critical points
c = lambda x: x[0] ** 3 - 3 * x[0] + x[1] ** 2
grad_c = lambda x: np.array([3 * x[0] ** 2 - 3, 2 * x[1]])
hess_c = lambda x: np.array([[6 * x[0], 0.0], [0.0, 2.0]])
x0, h = np.array([1.2, 0.5]), np.array([0.1, -0.2])
lin = c(x0) + grad_c(x0) @ h; quad = lin + 0.5 * h @ hess_c(x0) @ h
print(round(c(x0), 3), round(lin, 3), round(quad, 3), round(c(x0 + h), 3), round(c(x0 + h) - lin, 3), round(c(x0 + h) - quad, 6))
# -1.622 -1.69 -1.614 -1.613 0.077 0.001
print(np.abs(num_jac(grad_c, x0) - hess_c(x0)).max() < 1e-6)   # True: the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient
for xs in (np.array([1.0, 0.0]), np.array([-1.0, 0.0])):
    lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(hess_c(xs))
    kind = 'minimum' if lam.min() > 0 else 'maximum' if lam.max() < 0 else 'saddle' if lam.min() < 0 < lam.max() else 'undecided'
    print(xs, grad_c(xs), lam, c(xs), kind)
# [1. 0.] [0. 0.] [2. 6.] -2.0 minimum | [-1.  0.] [0. 0.] [-6.  2.] 2.0 saddle

# a Hessian-vector product from two gradients, without forming the Hessian
v = np.array([0.3, -0.7]); eps = 1e-6
print(((grad_c(x0 + eps * v) - grad_c(x0 - eps * v)) / (2 * eps)).round(6), hess_c(x0) @ v)   # [ 2.16 -1.4 ] [ 2.16 -1.4 ]
```
