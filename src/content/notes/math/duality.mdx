The [previous note](/notes/math/lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt) proved that the KKT conditions are sufficient for convex problems by comparing the objective with the Lagrangian: on the feasible set, adding the constraints with non-negative multipliers can only lower the objective. This note turns that comparison into the main object. Minimizing the Lagrangian over $x$, for fixed multipliers, gives a function of the multipliers alone, the **dual function**, and every value of it is a lower bound on the optimum. The best such bound is the **dual problem**. For convex problems it usually equals the optimum, and then the multipliers can be computed instead of the variables, which is how the SVM is solved and kernelized, and every pair of a primal and a dual point says how far from optimal both are.

**Notation.** The **primal problem** is that of the previous note: minimize $f(x)$ subject to $g_i(x) \le 0$ for $i = 1, \dots, m$ and $h_j(x) = 0$ for $j = 1, \dots, p$, with optimal value $p^*$ ($+\infty$ if no point is feasible, $-\infty$ if the objective is unbounded below). The Lagrangian is $\mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x)$ with $\lambda \in \mathbb R^m$ and $\nu \in \mathbb R^p$. Inequalities between vectors, such as $\lambda \ge 0$ or $A^\top y \le c$, hold entry by entry. The problem is **convex** if $f$ and the $g_i$ are convex and the $h_j$ affine.

## Baseline: a candidate and no certificate

A soft-margin SVM on 40 points (built in the Code section) is trained by an iterative solver. After 100 iterations its objective is $13.478$, after 1,000 iterations $13.231$, after 5,000 iterations $13.2226$. Is that the minimum? The values keep decreasing, the gradient-based tests of the [unconstrained optimization note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization) do not apply because the hinge loss has kinks, and the optimal value $p^*$ is exactly what is unknown. An upper bound on $p^*$ is easy: any feasible point gives one. What is missing is a **lower bound**, a number that $p^*$ provably cannot go below. With both, the distance to the optimum is bracketed and the solver can stop with a guarantee. Lagrange multipliers produce such lower bounds, one for every choice of multipliers.

## The Lagrange dual function

The **dual function** is the minimum of the Lagrangian over $x$, for fixed multipliers:

$$
g(\lambda, \nu) = \inf_{x} \mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu) = \inf_x \Big( f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x) \Big) .
$$

The infimum is over all $x$, feasible or not, so computing $g$ is an unconstrained problem, and the result may be $-\infty$. For each fixed $x$ the expression in brackets is an affine function of $(\lambda, \nu)$, and a pointwise infimum of affine functions is concave, by the [pointwise maximum rule](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#operations-that-preserve-convexity) applied to $-g$. So **the dual function is concave whatever $f$, $g_i$ and $h_j$ are**, convex or not.

## Weak duality

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Every choice of multipliers with $\lambda \ge 0$ gives a lower bound on the optimal value, because on the feasible set the added constraint terms are never positive. The best lower bound is the dual problem. For convex problems with a strictly feasible point it equals the optimum, and the gap between any primal value and any dual value bounds how far each is from optimal.</aside>

The idea: at a feasible point every term $\lambda_i g_i(x)$ with $\lambda_i \ge 0$ is at most zero and every term $\nu_j h_j(x)$ is zero, so the Lagrangian is at most the objective there; its minimum over all $x$ is lower still.

$$
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
g(\lambda, \nu) \le p^* \\
\text{for every } \lambda \ge 0 \text{ and every } \nu .
\end{gathered}
}
$$

Why. Take any feasible $\tilde x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(\lambda, \nu) &= \inf_x \mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu) \le \mathcal L(\tilde x, \lambda, \nu) && \text{an infimum is at most any value} \\
&= f(\tilde x) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i g_i(\tilde x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(\tilde x) && \text{definition of } \mathcal L \\
&\le f(\tilde x) && \lambda_i \ge 0,\ g_i(\tilde x) \le 0,\ h_j(\tilde x) = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The bound holds for every feasible $\tilde x$, hence for the infimum of $f$ over them, which is $p^*$. Nothing in the argument uses convexity. The **dual problem** asks for the best lower bound,

$$
d^* = \max_{\lambda \ge 0,\ \nu}\ g(\lambda, \nu) \le p^* ,
$$

a maximization of a concave function under the constraints $\lambda \ge 0$, so a convex problem even when the primal is not. The difference $p^* - d^* \ge 0$ is the **duality gap**.

**Worked example.** Minimize $x^2$ subject to $x \ge 1$, written as $g_1(x) = 1 - x \le 0$; clearly $p^* = 1$ at $x^* = 1$. The Lagrangian $x^2 + \lambda(1 - x)$ is a parabola in $x$ with its minimum at $x = \lambda/2$, so

$$
g(\lambda) = \frac{\lambda^2}{4} + \lambda\Big(1 - \frac\lambda2\Big) = \lambda - \frac{\lambda^2}{4} .
$$

At $\lambda = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ it takes the values $0$, $0.75$, $1$, $0.75$ and $0$: every one is a lower bound on $p^* = 1$, and the best one, at $\lambda^* = 2$, equals $p^*$. The minimizer of the Lagrangian at $\lambda^* = 2$ is $x = 1 = x^*$, and the stationarity condition $2x^* - \lambda^* = 0$ is the KKT condition of the previous note.

## Strong duality and Slater's condition

When $d^* = p^*$, **strong duality** holds. It fails in general, and holds for most convex problems. The standard sufficient condition is **Slater's condition**: the problem is convex and some point $\bar x$ is **strictly feasible**, $g_i(\bar x) \lt 0$ for every $i$ and $h_j(\bar x) = 0$ for every $j$. Then

$$
\boxed{\; \text{convex problem} + \text{a strictly feasible point} \ \Longrightarrow\ d^* = p^* , \ \text{and the dual optimum is attained when } p^* \text{ is finite.} \;}
$$

Inequalities that are affine need not hold strictly: a feasible point is enough for them (the refined Slater condition). In particular every feasible problem with a convex objective and only affine constraints, such as least squares with linear constraints or the SVM, has strong duality ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), §5.2.3).

<details>
<summary>Proof sketch, for one inequality constraint</summary>

Consider the set of pairs that the problem can achieve, $\mathcal A = \{(u, t) : g_1(x) \le u \text{ and } f(x) \le t \text{ for some } x\}$. It is convex, because $f$ and $g_1$ are, and it contains everything above and to the right of each of its points. The point $(0, p^*)$ lies on its boundary: $(0, p^* - \varepsilon)$ is not in $\mathcal A$ for any $\varepsilon \gt 0$. A convex set has a supporting line at each boundary point: there are $(\lambda, \mu) \ne (0, 0)$ with $\lambda u + \mu t \ge \mu p^*$ for every $(u, t) \in \mathcal A$. Since $\mathcal A$ extends to the right and upward, $\lambda \ge 0$ and $\mu \ge 0$. If $\mu = 0$, then $\lambda \gt 0$ and $\lambda u \ge 0$ on $\mathcal A$; but the Slater point gives a pair with $u = g_1(\bar x) \lt 0$, a contradiction. So $\mu \gt 0$, and dividing by it, $f(x) + (\lambda/\mu) g_1(x) \ge p^*$ for every $x$, that is $g(\lambda/\mu) \ge p^*$. With weak duality, $d^* = p^*$, attained at $\lambda/\mu$. The full proof is in [Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004)](#references), §5.3.2.

</details>

**A convex problem with a gap.** Minimize $e^{-x}$ subject to $x^2/y \le 0$, over $y \gt 0$. Only $x = 0$ is feasible, so $p^* = 1$. No point satisfies the constraint strictly, since $x^2/y$ is never negative, and Slater fails. The dual function is $g(\lambda) = \inf_{x,\, y \gt 0}\big(e^{-x} + \lambda x^2/y\big) = 0$ for every $\lambda \ge 0$: along $y = x^3$ the Lagrangian is $e^{-x} + \lambda/x$, which takes the values $1.368$, $0.207$, $0.050$ and $0.010$ at $x = 1, 5, 20, 100$ (for $\lambda = 1$) and tends to 0. So $d^* = 0 \lt 1 = p^*$ ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), Exercise 5.21).

**Strong duality gives the KKT conditions.** If $x^*$ is primal optimal, $(\lambda^*, \nu^*)$ dual optimal and $d^* = p^*$, then

$$
f(x^*) = g(\lambda^*, \nu^*) \le \mathcal L(x^*, \lambda^*, \nu^*) = f(x^*) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i^* g_i(x^*) + \sum_j \nu_j^* h_j(x^*) \le f(x^*) ,
$$

so both inequalities are equalities. The first says that $x^*$ minimizes $\mathcal L(\cdot, \lambda^*, \nu^*)$, which gives stationarity; the second says $\sum_i \lambda_i^* g_i(x^*) = 0$, a sum of non-positive terms, so every term is zero: complementary slackness. This is how the [SVM note](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines#the-kkt-conditions) derives its KKT conditions.

## The dual of a linear program

The idea: for a linear objective, the Lagrangian is linear in $x$, and its infimum is finite only when the coefficient of $x$ vanishes; that condition becomes the dual's constraint. For the **linear program**

$$
\min_x\ c^\top x \quad \text{subject to} \quad Ax \ge b,\ x \ge 0 ,
$$

the dual is again a linear program:

$$
\boxed{\; \max_y\ b^\top y \quad \text{subject to} \quad A^\top y \le c,\ y \ge 0 . \;}
$$

Give the constraints $b - Ax \le 0$ the multipliers $y \ge 0$ and $-x \le 0$ the multipliers $s \ge 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x, y, s) &= c^\top x + y^\top(b - Ax) - s^\top x && \text{objective plus multipliers times constraints} \\
&= b^\top y + (c - A^\top y - s)^\top x && \text{collect the terms in } x \\
g(y, s) &= \begin{cases} b^\top y & \text{if } c - A^\top y - s = 0 \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} && \text{a linear function of } x \text{ is bounded below only if it is constant} \\
\max_{y \ge 0,\ s \ge 0}\ g &= \max\{b^\top y : y \ge 0,\ A^\top y \le c\} && s = c - A^\top y \ge 0 \text{ is the dual constraint.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The constraints of an LP are affine, so strong duality holds whenever the primal is feasible and bounded.

**Worked example.** Minimize $3x_1 + 2x_2$ subject to $x_1 + x_2 \ge 2$, $x_1 + 3x_2 \ge 3$, $x \ge 0$. The feasible set is a polyhedron with vertices $(0, 2)$, $(1.5, 0.5)$ and $(3, 0)$, with costs 4, 5.5 and 9, so $p^* = 4$ at $x^* = (0, 2)$. The dual is: maximize $2y_1 + 3y_2$ subject to $y_1 + y_2 \le 3$, $y_1 + 3y_2 \le 2$, $y \ge 0$. The point $y^* = (2, 0)$ is feasible with value 4. Since every dual value is at most $p^*$ and every primal value at least $d^*$, the two points certify each other: both are optimal, with no search. Complementary slackness holds pair by pair: the second primal constraint is slack ($0 + 6 = 6 \gt 3$) and its price $y_2^*$ is zero; $x_2^* \gt 0$ and the second dual constraint is tight ($2 + 0 = 2$); the first dual constraint is slack ($2 \lt 3$) and $x_1^* = 0$. Read $y$ as prices of the requirements $b$: raising the first requirement from 2 to $2 + u$ raises the optimal cost by $2u$, the multiplier.

## Least squares under a norm constraint

The idea: the constraint $\lVert x\rVert^2 \le t$ enters the Lagrangian as a penalty $\lambda\lVert x\rVert^2$, so minimizing the Lagrangian is ridge regression, and the dual problem picks the penalty whose ridge solution exactly meets the constraint. For

$$
\min_x\ \lVert Ax - b\rVert^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \lVert x\rVert^2 \le t ,
$$

with $t \gt 0$,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
g(\lambda) &= \lVert Ax_\lambda - b\rVert^2 + \lambda\big(\lVert x_\lambda\rVert^2 - t\big), \\
x_\lambda &= (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1}A^\top b , \\
d^* &= \max_{\lambda \ge 0} g(\lambda) = p^* .
\end{aligned}
}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x, \lambda) &= \lVert Ax - b\rVert^2 + \lambda\big(\lVert x\rVert^2 - t\big) && \text{one inequality, multiplier } \lambda \ge 0 \\
\nabla_x\mathcal L &= 2A^\top(Ax - b) + 2\lambda x = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ x_\lambda = (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1}A^\top b && \mathcal L \text{ is convex in } x \text{: the ridge solution} \\
g'(\lambda) &= \lVert x_\lambda\rVert^2 - t && x_\lambda \text{ minimizes } \mathcal L(\cdot, \lambda) \text{, so only the explicit } \lambda \text{ counts.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line is the envelope theorem: the derivative of $\mathcal L(x_\lambda, \lambda)$ through $x_\lambda$ vanishes because $\nabla_x\mathcal L = 0$ there. The point $x = 0$ is strictly feasible, so Slater holds and $d^* = p^*$. If the least-squares solution already has $\lVert x_0\rVert^2 \le t$, then $\lambda^* = 0$; otherwise $g'(\lambda^*) = 0$ says $\lVert x_{\lambda^*}\rVert^2 = t$, which is complementary slackness. This is the duality that the [regularization note](/notes/ml/regularization#the-constrained-form-why-l1-gives-zeros) invokes: the constrained and the penalized forms of ridge regression trace the same family of solutions, with $\lambda$ the multiplier of the radius $t$.

**Worked example.** $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $b = (1, 2, 2)$, $t = 1$. The least-squares solution $(0.667, 1.667)$ has squared norm $3.22 \gt 1$, so the constraint binds. Its value $g(0) = 0.333$, the unconstrained minimum, is the first lower bound. Further bounds are $g(0.5) = 1.167$, $g(1) = 1.625$, $g(2) = 1.933$ and $g(4) = 1.400$. The maximum is at $\lambda^* = 2.085$, where $x_{\lambda^*} = (0.526, 0.850)$ has squared norm exactly 1 and $g(\lambda^*) = 1.9348$; a constrained solver applied to the primal finds the same point and the same value $1.9348$.

## The SVM dual: nearest points of two convex hulls

The [SVM note](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines#the-dual-problem) derives the dual of the soft-margin SVM step by step and reads off support vectors from it; that derivation is the scheme of this note applied to one problem. Duality adds a geometric reading of the hard-margin dual. The idea: the equality constraint $\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$ splits the multipliers into equal total weights on the two classes, so $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i$ is a scaled difference of a point in the convex hull of the positive class and a point in the hull of the negative class, and the dual becomes a nearest-points problem.

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
d^* &= \frac{2}{\delta^2} , \\
w^* &= \frac{2\,(c_+ - c_-)}{\delta^2} , \\
\gamma &= \frac{\delta}{2} , \\
\delta &= \min_{c_+ \in \operatorname{conv}_+,\ c_- \in \operatorname{conv}_-} \lVert c_+ - c_-\rVert .
\end{aligned}
}
$$

Start from the hard-margin dual $\max_\alpha \sum_i \alpha_i - \frac12\lVert\sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i\rVert^2$ subject to $\alpha \ge 0$ and $\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s &= \textstyle\sum_{y_i = +1}\alpha_i = \sum_{y_i = -1}\alpha_i , \quad \alpha_i = s\,\beta_i && \textstyle\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0 \text{; } \beta \text{ sums to 1 on each class} \\
\textstyle\sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i &= s\,(c_+ - c_-), \quad c_\pm = \textstyle\sum_{y_i = \pm1}\beta_i x_i && c_\pm \text{ are convex combinations: points of the hulls} \\
\text{dual objective} &= 2s - \tfrac12 s^2\lVert c_+ - c_-\rVert^2 && \textstyle\sum_i\alpha_i = 2s \\
\max_{s \ge 0} &= \frac{2}{\lVert c_+ - c_-\rVert^2} \ \text{ at } s = \frac{2}{\lVert c_+ - c_-\rVert^2} && \text{a parabola in } s .
\end{aligned}
$$

Maximizing over the remaining weights $\beta$ is minimizing $\lVert c_+ - c_-\rVert$ over the two hulls, so $d^* = 2/\delta^2$ and $w^* = s(c_+ - c_-)$. The margin is $\gamma = 1/\lVert w^*\rVert = \delta/2$: the maximum-margin hyperplane is the perpendicular bisector of the shortest segment between the two convex hulls ([Bennett and Bredensteiner, 2000](#references)). For the soft margin, the bound $\alpha_i \le C$ caps each $\beta_i$ and the hulls shrink to "reduced" hulls, which overlap less.

**Worked example.** The four points of the SVM note: negatives $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (2, 0)$, positives $C = (1, 2)$ and $D = (3, 3)$. The hulls are the segments $AB$ and $CD$, and the nearest points are $c_- = (1, 0)$, the midpoint of $AB$, and $c_+ = C = (1, 2)$, at distance $\delta = 2$. Then $s = 2/4 = \frac12$, so $\alpha_C = \frac12$, $\alpha_A = \alpha_B = \frac12 \cdot \frac12 = \frac14$ and $\alpha_D = 0$; $w^* = \frac12(0, 2) = (0, 1)$, $\gamma = 1$ and $d^* = \frac12$. These are the numbers that the SVM note finds from the KKT conditions.

## The duality gap as a stopping criterion

The idea: a primal feasible point gives an upper bound on $p^*$, a dual feasible point a lower bound, and the optimum lies between them. For any feasible $x$ and any $\lambda \ge 0$, $\nu$,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
f(x) - p^* \le f(x) - g(\lambda, \nu) \\
\text{and}\ \ d^* - g(\lambda, \nu) \le f(x) - g(\lambda, \nu) .
\end{gathered}
}
$$

Both follow from $g(\lambda, \nu) \le d^* \le p^* \le f(x)$. The computable difference $f(x) - g(\lambda, \nu)$ certifies how far the current point is from optimal, without knowing $p^*$. A solver that stops when the gap is below $\varepsilon$ returns a point within $\varepsilon$ of the optimum; scikit-learn's coordinate-descent `Lasso`, for example, checks a duality gap against its tolerance.

**Worked example: the SVM of the baseline.** Absorb the bias into the weights by appending a constant feature 1 to every point, the variant used by many linear SVM solvers. The primal is unconstrained, $P(w) = \frac12\lVert w\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \max(0, 1 - y_i w^\top z_i)$ with $z_i = (x_i, 1)$ and $C = 1$, and the derivation of the SVM note without $b$ gives the dual $D(\alpha) = \sum_i \alpha_i - \frac12\lVert\sum_i \alpha_i y_i z_i\rVert^2$ over the box $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$, with no equality constraint. Projected gradient ascent alternates a gradient step on $D$ with [clipping to the box](/notes/math/lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt#projection-onto-a-box-and-onto-the-simplex). Every iterate $\alpha$ is dual feasible, $w(\alpha) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i z_i$ is a primal point, and their gap bounds the error:

| Iteration | $P(w(\alpha))$ | $D(\alpha)$ | gap $P - D$ | true error $P - p^*$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 19.682 | 0.291 | 19 | 6.5 |
| 10 | 16.260 | 1.744 | 15 | 3.0 |
| 100 | 13.478 | 12.393 | 1.1 | 0.26 |
| 1,000 | 13.231 | 13.222 | $8.3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $8.2 \cdot 10^{-3}$ |
| 5,000 | 13.2226 | 13.2226 | $2 \cdot 10^{-11}$ | $2 \cdot 10^{-11}$ |

Stopping at a gap below $10^{-3}$ ends the run at iteration 1,422, where the true error is $9.9 \cdot 10^{-4}$, inside the guarantee. The gap is pessimistic early, when the dual iterate is poor, and tight near the end. At the solution 15 of the 40 points have $\alpha_i \gt 0$, the support vectors, and 12 of them sit at the bound $C$, inside the margin or misclassified.

## What changed

| | Primal problem | Dual problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Variables | $x \in \mathbb R^n$ | multipliers $\lambda \ge 0$, $\nu$: one per constraint |
| Objective | $f(x)$, minimized | $g(\lambda, \nu) = \inf_x \mathcal L$, maximized; always concave |
| Any feasible point gives | an upper bound on $p^*$ | a lower bound on $p^*$ (weak duality) |
| Optimal values | $p^*$ | $d^* \le p^*$, equal under Slater's condition |
| LP | $\min c^\top x$, $Ax \ge b$, $x \ge 0$ | $\max b^\top y$, $A^\top y \le c$, $y \ge 0$ |
| Hard-margin SVM | widest margin, $\min\frac12\lVert w\rVert^2$ | nearest points of the two convex hulls |

## Limitations

- **Strong duality can fail.** Non-convex problems usually have a gap, and even convex ones need a constraint qualification such as Slater's condition.
- **The dual function may be hard to evaluate.** It requires an unconstrained minimization for every value of the multipliers; it has a closed form only when the Lagrangian is simple (quadratic, linear, separable).
- **Recovering the primal solution** from a dual solution needs the minimizer of the Lagrangian to be unique, which fails, for example, for linear programs, where the Lagrangian is linear in $x$.
- **The dual can be larger.** The SVM dual has one variable per training point, $n$ of them, and a dense $n \times n$ matrix; for millions of points the primal is solved instead, by stochastic gradients.

## What you should remember

- The dual function $g(\lambda, \nu) = \inf_x \mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu)$ is concave, and $g(\lambda, \nu) \le p^*$ for every $\lambda \ge 0$: weak duality, for any problem.
- The dual problem $\max_{\lambda \ge 0, \nu} g$ is convex; its value $d^*$ equals $p^*$ for convex problems with a strictly feasible point (Slater).
- Strong duality forces the KKT conditions: $x^*$ minimizes the Lagrangian at the optimal multipliers, and complementary slackness holds.
- The dual of $\min\{c^\top x : Ax \ge b,\ x \ge 0\}$ is $\max\{b^\top y : A^\top y \le c,\ y \ge 0\}$; a primal and a dual point with equal values are both optimal.
- Norm-constrained least squares has ridge regression as its Lagrangian minimizer; the optimal multiplier makes the ridge solution meet the constraint.
- The hard-margin SVM dual finds the nearest points of the two classes' convex hulls; the margin is half their distance.
- The duality gap $f(x) - g(\lambda, \nu)$ bounds the suboptimality of both points and is a safe stopping rule.

## Code

The bound of the figure, a problem with a duality gap, an LP and its dual, norm-constrained least squares, the SVM dual as nearest points, and an SVM solver stopped by its duality gap, with NumPy and SciPy. Exact digits can differ slightly across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq, linprog, minimize

# min x^2 s.t. 1 - x <= 0: the dual function g(lam) = lam - lam^2/4 lies below p* = 1 and touches it at lam = 2
lam = np.linspace(0, 5, 501); g = lam - lam**2 / 4
print(g.max(), lam[g.argmax()], (g <= 1 + 1e-12).all())
# 1.0 2.0 True

# a convex problem without a Slater point: min e^{-x} s.t. x^2 / y <= 0 (y > 0); p* = 1 but g(lam) = 0
for x in (1., 5., 20., 100.):                              # along y = x^3 the Lagrangian tends to 0 for every lam
    print(x, f'{np.exp(-x) + 1.0 * x**2 / x**3:.4f}', end=' | ')
print()
# 1.0 1.3679 | 5.0 0.2067 | 20.0 0.0500 | 100.0 0.0100

# an LP and its dual: min 3x1 + 2x2 s.t. x1 + x2 >= 2, x1 + 3x2 >= 3, x >= 0;  max 2y1 + 3y2 s.t. y1 + y2 <= 3, y1 + 3y2 <= 2, y >= 0
A = np.array([[1., 1], [1, 3]]); b = np.array([2., 3]); c = np.array([3., 2])
pr = linprog(c, A_ub=-A, b_ub=-b, bounds=[(0, None)] * 2)
du = linprog(-b, A_ub=A.T, b_ub=c, bounds=[(0, None)] * 2)
print(pr.x, pr.fun, du.x, -du.fun, A @ pr.x - b, c - A.T @ du.x)   # slacks: primal constraints, dual constraints
# [0. 2.] 4.0 [2. 0.] 4.0 [0. 3.] [1. 0.]

# least squares under a norm constraint: min |Ax - b|^2 s.t. |x|^2 <= t; the dual is a function of one multiplier
A = np.array([[1., 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]]); b = np.array([1., 2, 2]); t = 1.0
x_lam = lambda l: np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + l * np.eye(2), A.T @ b)          # the minimizer of the Lagrangian: ridge
gd = lambda l: np.sum((A @ x_lam(l) - b) ** 2) + l * (x_lam(l) @ x_lam(l) - t)
ls = x_lam(0); print(ls.round(4), round(ls @ ls, 4), round(gd(0), 4))      # unconstrained solution is infeasible
lam_s = brentq(lambda l: x_lam(l) @ x_lam(l) - t, 0, 100); xs = x_lam(lam_s)
r = minimize(lambda x: np.sum((A @ x - b) ** 2), np.zeros(2), method='SLSQP',
             constraints=[{'type': 'ineq', 'fun': lambda x: t - x @ x}], options={'ftol': 1e-14})
print(round(lam_s, 4), xs.round(4), round(gd(lam_s), 6), round(r.fun, 6), [round(float(gd(l)), 4) for l in (0.5, 1, 2, 4)])
# [0.6667 1.6667] 3.2222 0.3333
# 2.0851 [0.5262 0.8504] 1.934833 1.934833 [1.1667, 1.625, 1.9333, 1.4]

# a soft-margin SVM (bias absorbed into w) solved by projected gradient ascent on its box-constrained dual;
# the duality gap P(w(alpha)) - D(alpha) bounds the suboptimality of every iterate
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, C = 40, 1.0
y = np.repeat([1., -1.], n // 2)
X = rng.normal(size=(n, 2)) + y[:, None]                     # two overlapping clouds around (1, 1) and (-1, -1)
Z = np.column_stack([X, np.ones(n)]) * y[:, None]            # rows y_i (x_i, 1)
Q = Z @ Z.T
Pobj = lambda w: 0.5 * w @ w + C * np.maximum(0, 1 - Z @ w).sum()
Dobj = lambda a: a.sum() - 0.5 * a @ Q @ a
eta = 1 / np.linalg.eigvalsh(Q)[-1]

for k in range(1, 20_001):

    hist.append((Pobj(Z.T @ a), Dobj(a)))
p_star = hist[-1][0]
print(round(p_star, 4), f'{hist[-1][0] - hist[-1][1]:.0e}', (a > 1e-8).sum(), (a > C - 1e-8).sum())
for k in (1, 10, 100, 1000, 5000):
    pk, dk = hist[k - 1]
    print(k, round(pk, 4), round(dk, 4), f'{pk - dk:.1e}', f'{pk - p_star:.1e}', end=' | ')
print()
```

## Next

Every number in this section was computed in floating point, and the notes treated the results as exact. They mostly are, but not always: the Newton step solves a linear system whose matrix can be nearly singular, least squares can be solved through $A^\top A$ or through a factorization of $A$ with very different accuracy, and the softmax in the maximum-entropy solution overflows for large scores. The [last note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) measures how errors in the data and in the arithmetic grow through a computation: the condition number of a problem, the backward stability of an algorithm, and the standard rewrites, such as QR for least squares and the log-sum-exp shift, that keep machine learning code accurate.
