A soft-margin SVM on 40 points is trained by an iterative solver. After 100 iterations its objective is $13.478$, after 1,000 iterations $13.231$, after 5,000 iterations $13.2226$. Is that the minimum? The values keep decreasing, and the gradient tests of the [unconstrained optimization note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization) do not apply, because the hinge loss has kinks. The optimal value $p^*$ is exactly what is unknown.

An upper bound on $p^*$ is easy: any feasible point gives one. What is missing is a **lower bound**, a number that $p^*$ provably cannot go below. With both, the optimum is bracketed and the solver can stop with a guarantee. The KKT conditions of the [previous note](/notes/math/lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt) already contained one: on the feasible set, adding the constraints with non-negative multipliers can only lower the objective. Every choice of multipliers gives such a bound, and the best of them is often the optimum itself.

## The Lagrange dual function

The **primal problem** is the one of the previous note: minimize $f(x)$ subject to $g_i(x) \le 0$ for $i = 1, \dots, m$ and $h_j(x) = 0$ for $j = 1, \dots, p$. Its optimal value is $p^*$, which is $+\infty$ if no point is feasible and $-\infty$ if the objective is unbounded below. The problem is **convex** if $f$ and the $g_i$ are convex and the $h_j$ affine. With multipliers $\lambda \in \mathbb R^m$ and $\nu \in \mathbb R^p$ the Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^p \nu_j h_j(x) .
$$

The **dual function** is its minimum over $x$, for fixed multipliers:

$$
g(\lambda, \nu) = \inf_x \mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu) .
$$

The infimum is over all $x$, feasible or not, so computing $g$ is an unconstrained problem, and the result may be $-\infty$. For each fixed $x$ the Lagrangian is an affine function of $(\lambda, \nu)$, and a pointwise infimum of affine functions is concave, by the [pointwise maximum rule](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#operations-that-preserve-convexity) applied to $-g$. So **the dual function is concave whatever $f$, $g_i$ and $h_j$ are**, convex or not.

## Weak duality

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Every choice of multipliers with $\lambda \ge 0$ gives a lower bound $g(\lambda, \nu) \le p^*$, because on the feasible set the added constraint terms are never positive. The best such bound is the dual problem.</aside>

Take any feasible $\tilde x$ and any $\lambda \ge 0$, $\nu$. Inequalities between vectors, such as $\lambda \ge 0$, hold entry by entry. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(\lambda, \nu) &\le \mathcal L(\tilde x, \lambda, \nu) \\
&= f(\tilde x) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i g_i(\tilde x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(\tilde x) \\
&\le f(\tilde x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line holds because an infimum is at most any of its values, the last because $\lambda_i \ge 0$, $g_i(\tilde x) \le 0$ and $h_j(\tilde x) = 0$. The bound holds for every feasible $\tilde x$, hence for the infimum of $f$ over them, which is $p^*$. Nothing in the argument uses convexity.

The **dual problem** asks for the best lower bound:

$$
d^* = \max_{\lambda \ge 0,\ \nu}\ g(\lambda, \nu) \le p^* .
$$

It maximizes a concave function subject to $\lambda \ge 0$, so it is a convex problem even when the primal is not. The difference $p^* - d^* \ge 0$ is the **duality gap**.

Take the problem: minimize $x^2$ subject to $x \ge 1$, written as $g_1(x) = 1 - x \le 0$; clearly $p^* = 1$ at $x^* = 1$. The Lagrangian $x^2 + \lambda(1 - x)$ is a parabola in $x$ with its minimum at $x = \lambda/2$, so

$$
g(\lambda) = \frac{\lambda^2}{4} + \lambda\Big(1 - \frac\lambda2\Big) = \lambda - \frac{\lambda^2}{4} .
$$

At $\lambda = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$ it takes the values $0$, $0.75$, $1$, $0.75$ and $0$. Every one is a lower bound on $p^* = 1$, and the best one, at $\lambda^* = 2$, equals $p^*$. The minimizer of the Lagrangian at $\lambda^* = 2$ is $x = 1 = x^*$, and $2x^* - \lambda^* = 0$ is the stationarity condition of KKT.

<WeakDuality />

The two problems are the same function optimized in opposite orders ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), §5.4.1; [Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, 2020](#references), Section 7.2). For a fixed $x$, the supremum of $\mathcal L$ over $\lambda \ge 0$ and $\nu$ is $f(x)$ if $x$ is feasible and $+\infty$ if some constraint is violated, since its multiplier can then grow without limit. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
p^* &= \inf_x\ \sup_{\lambda \ge 0,\, \nu} \mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu) , \\
d^* &= \sup_{\lambda \ge 0,\, \nu}\ \inf_x \mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Weak duality is the max–min inequality, which holds for any function of two arguments: the player who moves second does at least as well. Strong duality says that the order does not matter, and then a primal and a dual solution form a saddle point of $\mathcal L$.

## Strong duality and Slater's condition

When $d^* = p^*$, **strong duality** holds. It fails in general, and holds for most convex problems. The standard sufficient condition is **Slater's condition**: the problem is convex and some point $\bar x$ is **strictly feasible**, with $g_i(\bar x) \lt 0$ for every $i$ and $h_j(\bar x) = 0$ for every $j$. Under it, $d^* = p^*$, and the dual optimum is attained whenever $p^*$ is finite.

Inequalities that are affine need not hold strictly: a feasible point is enough for them. In particular every feasible problem with a convex objective and only affine constraints, such as least squares with linear constraints or the SVM, has strong duality ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), §5.2.3).

<details>
<summary>Proof sketch, for one inequality constraint</summary>

Consider the set of pairs that the problem can achieve, $\mathcal A = \{(u, t) : g_1(x) \le u \text{ and } f(x) \le t \text{ for some } x\}$. It is convex, because $f$ and $g_1$ are, and it contains everything above and to the right of each of its points. The point $(0, p^*)$ lies on its boundary: $(0, p^* - \varepsilon)$ is not in $\mathcal A$ for any $\varepsilon \gt 0$.

A convex set has a supporting line at each boundary point: there are $(\lambda, \mu) \ne (0, 0)$ with $\lambda u + \mu t \ge \mu p^*$ for every $(u, t) \in \mathcal A$. Since $\mathcal A$ extends to the right and upward, $\lambda \ge 0$ and $\mu \ge 0$. If $\mu = 0$, then $\lambda \gt 0$ and $\lambda u \ge 0$ on $\mathcal A$; but the Slater point gives a pair with $u = g_1(\bar x) \lt 0$, a contradiction.

So $\mu \gt 0$, and dividing by it, $f(x) + (\lambda/\mu) g_1(x) \ge p^*$ for every $x$, that is $g(\lambda/\mu) \ge p^*$. With weak duality, $d^* = p^*$, attained at $\lambda/\mu$. The full proof is in [Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004)](#references), §5.3.2.

</details>

Without a Slater point, even a convex problem can have a gap. Minimize $e^{-x}$ subject to $x^2/y \le 0$, over $y \gt 0$. Only $x = 0$ is feasible, so $p^* = 1$. No point satisfies the constraint strictly, since $x^2/y$ is never negative. The dual function is

$$
g(\lambda) = \inf_{x,\, y \gt 0}\Big(e^{-x} + \frac{\lambda x^2}{y}\Big) = 0
$$

for every $\lambda \ge 0$. Along $y = x^3$ the Lagrangian is $e^{-x} + \lambda/x$, which for $\lambda = 1$ takes the values $1.368$, $0.207$, $0.050$ and $0.010$ at $x = 1, 5, 20, 100$ and tends to 0. So $d^* = 0 \lt 1 = p^*$ ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), Exercise 5.21).

Strong duality forces the KKT conditions. If $x^*$ is primal optimal, $(\lambda^*, \nu^*)$ dual optimal and $d^* = p^*$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x^*) &= g(\lambda^*, \nu^*) \\
&\le \mathcal L(x^*, \lambda^*, \nu^*) \\
&= f(x^*) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i^* g_i(x^*) + \sum_j \nu_j^* h_j(x^*) \\
&\le f(x^*) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

so both inequalities are equalities. The first says that $x^*$ minimizes $\mathcal L(\cdot, \lambda^*, \nu^*)$, which gives stationarity. The second says that $\sum_i \lambda_i^* g_i(x^*) = 0$, a sum of non-positive terms, so every term is zero: complementary slackness. This is how the [SVM note](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines#the-kkt-conditions) derives its KKT conditions.

## The dual of a linear program

For a linear objective the Lagrangian is linear in $x$, and its infimum is finite only when the coefficient of $x$ vanishes. That condition becomes the dual's constraint. Take the **linear program**

$$
\min_x\ c^\top x \quad \text{subject to} \quad Ax \ge b,\ x \ge 0 .
$$

Give the constraints $b - Ax \le 0$ the multipliers $y \ge 0$ and the constraints $-x \le 0$ the multipliers $s \ge 0$, and collect the terms in $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x, y, s) &= c^\top x + y^\top(b - Ax) - s^\top x \\
&= b^\top y + (c - A^\top y - s)^\top x .
\end{aligned}
$$

A linear function of $x$ is bounded below only if it is constant, so $g(y, s) = b^\top y$ when $c - A^\top y - s = 0$ and $-\infty$ otherwise. The condition $s = c - A^\top y \ge 0$ is the dual constraint, and the dual is again a linear program:

$$
\max_y\ b^\top y \quad \text{subject to} \quad A^\top y \le c,\ y \ge 0 .
$$

The constraints of an LP are affine, so strong duality holds whenever the primal is feasible and bounded.

For example, minimize $3x_1 + 2x_2$ subject to $x_1 + x_2 \ge 2$, $x_1 + 3x_2 \ge 3$ and $x \ge 0$. The feasible set is a polyhedron with vertices $(0, 2)$, $(1.5, 0.5)$ and $(3, 0)$, with costs 4, 5.5 and 9, so $p^* = 4$ at $x^* = (0, 2)$. The dual is: maximize $2y_1 + 3y_2$ subject to $y_1 + y_2 \le 3$, $y_1 + 3y_2 \le 2$ and $y \ge 0$. The point $y^* = (2, 0)$ is feasible with value 4. Every dual value is at most $p^*$ and every primal value at least $d^*$, so the two points certify each other: both are optimal, with no search. Complementary slackness holds pair by pair:

| Constraint | Slack at the optimum | Paired variable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| primal $x_1 + x_2 \ge 2$ | $0$ (tight) | $y_1^* = 2$ |
| primal $x_1 + 3x_2 \ge 3$ | $3$ | $y_2^* = 0$ |
| dual $y_1 + y_2 \le 3$ | $1$ | $x_1^* = 0$ |
| dual $y_1 + 3y_2 \le 2$ | $0$ (tight) | $x_2^* = 2$ |

Every row has a zero in it. Read $y$ as prices of the requirements $b$: raising the first requirement from 2 to $2 + u$ raises the optimal cost by $2u$, the multiplier.

## Least squares under a norm constraint

The constraint $\lVert x\rVert^2 \le t$ enters the Lagrangian as a penalty $\lambda\lVert x\rVert^2$, so minimizing the Lagrangian is ridge regression. Take

$$
\min_x\ \lVert Ax - b\rVert^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \lVert x\rVert^2 \le t ,
$$

with $t \gt 0$ and one multiplier $\lambda \ge 0$. The Lagrangian is convex in $x$, and setting its gradient to zero gives the ridge solution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x, \lambda) &= \lVert Ax - b\rVert^2 + \lambda\big(\lVert x\rVert^2 - t\big) , \\
\nabla_x\mathcal L &= 2A^\top(Ax - b) + 2\lambda x = 0 , \\
x_\lambda &= (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1}A^\top b .
\end{aligned}
$$

The dual function is $g(\lambda) = \mathcal L(x_\lambda, \lambda)$, and its derivative is simple:

$$
g'(\lambda) = \lVert x_\lambda\rVert^2 - t .
$$

This is the envelope theorem: the derivative of $\mathcal L(x_\lambda, \lambda)$ through $x_\lambda$ vanishes because $\nabla_x\mathcal L = 0$ there, so only the explicit $\lambda$ counts. The point $x = 0$ is strictly feasible, so Slater holds and $d^* = p^*$. If the least-squares solution already has $\lVert x_0\rVert^2 \le t$, then $\lambda^* = 0$. Otherwise $g'(\lambda^*) = 0$ says $\lVert x_{\lambda^*}\rVert^2 = t$, which is complementary slackness: the dual picks the penalty whose ridge solution exactly meets the constraint.

This is the duality that the [regularization note](/notes/ml/regularization#the-constrained-form-why-l1-gives-zeros) invokes: the constrained and the penalized forms of ridge regression trace the same family of solutions, with $\lambda$ the multiplier of the radius $t$.

Take $A$ with rows $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 1)$, $b = (1, 2, 2)$ and $t = 1$. The least-squares solution $(0.667, 1.667)$ has squared norm $3.22 \gt 1$, so the constraint binds. The dual function gives a lower bound for every $\lambda$:

| $\lambda$ | 0 | 0.5 | 1 | 2 | 2.085 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $g(\lambda)$ | 0.333 | 1.167 | 1.625 | 1.933 | 1.9348 | 1.400 |

The first value is the unconstrained minimum. The maximum is at $\lambda^* = 2.085$, where $x_{\lambda^*} = (0.526, 0.850)$ has squared norm exactly 1. A constrained solver applied to the primal finds the same point and the same value, $1.9348$.

## The SVM dual: nearest points of two convex hulls

The [SVM note](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines#the-dual-problem) derives the dual of the soft-margin SVM step by step, by the scheme above, and reads off the support vectors from it. Duality adds a geometric reading of the hard-margin dual ([Bennett and Bredensteiner, 2000](#references)). Start from

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{maximize}\quad & \sum_i \alpha_i - \frac12\Big\lVert\sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i\Big\rVert^2 \\
\text{subject to}\quad & \alpha \ge 0, \quad \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The equality constraint gives the two classes the same total weight $s = \sum_{y_i = +1}\alpha_i = \sum_{y_i = -1}\alpha_i$. Write $\alpha_i = s\,\beta_i$, so that $\beta$ sums to 1 on each class. Then $\sum_i \alpha_i = 2s$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i &= s\,(c_+ - c_-) , \\
c_\pm &= \sum_{y_i = \pm1}\beta_i x_i ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $c_+$ and $c_-$ are convex combinations of the points of each class: points of the two **convex hulls**. The dual objective becomes a parabola in $s$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D(s) &= 2s - \tfrac12 s^2\lVert c_+ - c_-\rVert^2 , \\
\max_{s \ge 0} D &= \frac{2}{\lVert c_+ - c_-\rVert^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The maximum is at $s = 2/\lVert c_+ - c_-\rVert^2$. Maximizing over the remaining weights $\beta$ is minimizing $\lVert c_+ - c_-\rVert$ over the two hulls. With $\delta$ the distance between the hulls,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d^* &= 2/\delta^2 , \\
w^* &= s\,(c_+ - c_-) , \\
\gamma &= 1/\lVert w^*\rVert = \delta/2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The maximum-margin hyperplane is the perpendicular bisector of the shortest segment between the two convex hulls, and the margin is half its length. For the soft margin, the bound $\alpha_i \le C$ caps each $\beta_i$ and the hulls shrink to "reduced" hulls, which overlap less.

The four points of the SVM note make this concrete: negatives $A = (0, 0)$ and $B = (2, 0)$, positives $C = (1, 2)$ and $D = (3, 3)$. The hulls are the segments $AB$ and $CD$. Their nearest points are $c_- = (1, 0)$, the midpoint of $AB$, and $c_+ = C = (1, 2)$, at distance $\delta = 2$. Then $s = \frac12$, so $\alpha_C = \frac12$, $\alpha_A = \alpha_B = \frac14$ and $\alpha_D = 0$; $w^* = \frac12(0, 2) = (0, 1)$, $\gamma = 1$ and $d^* = \frac12$. These are the numbers the SVM note finds from the KKT conditions.

## The duality gap as a stopping criterion

A primal feasible point gives an upper bound on $p^*$, a dual feasible point a lower bound, and the optimum lies between them. For any feasible $x$ and any $\lambda \ge 0$, $\nu$,

$$
g(\lambda, \nu) \le d^* \le p^* \le f(x) .
$$

So the computable difference $f(x) - g(\lambda, \nu)$ bounds both $f(x) - p^*$ and $d^* - g(\lambda, \nu)$: it certifies how far the current point is from optimal without knowing $p^*$ ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), §5.5.1). A solver that stops when the gap is below $\varepsilon$ returns a point within $\varepsilon$ of the optimum. Scikit-learn's coordinate-descent `Lasso`, for example, checks a duality gap against its tolerance ([scikit-learn documentation](#references)). Interior-point methods produce such a pair at every step: the point on their central path with barrier weight $t$ comes with dual multipliers whose gap is exactly $m/t$, for $m$ inequalities ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), §11.2.2).

Take the SVM of the opening. Absorb the bias into the weights by appending a constant feature 1 to every point, as many linear SVM solvers do ([Hsieh et al., 2008](#references)). The primal is then unconstrained,

$$
P(w) = \tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \max(0, 1 - y_i w^\top z_i) ,
$$

with $z_i = (x_i, 1)$ and $C = 1$. The derivation of the SVM note without $b$ gives the dual

$$
D(\alpha) = \sum_i \alpha_i - \tfrac12\Big\lVert\sum_i \alpha_i y_i z_i\Big\rVert^2
$$

over the box $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$, with no equality constraint. Projected gradient ascent alternates a gradient step on $D$ with [clipping to the box](/notes/math/lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt#projection-onto-a-box-and-onto-the-simplex). Every iterate $\alpha$ is dual feasible, $w(\alpha) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i z_i$ is a primal point, and their gap bounds the error:

| Iteration | $P(w(\alpha))$ | $D(\alpha)$ | gap $P - D$ | true error $P - p^*$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 19.682 | 0.291 | 19 | 6.5 |
| 10 | 16.260 | 1.744 | 15 | 3.0 |
| 100 | 13.478 | 12.393 | 1.1 | 0.26 |
| 1,000 | 13.231 | 13.222 | $8.3 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $8.2 \cdot 10^{-3}$ |
| 5,000 | 13.2226 | 13.2226 | $2 \cdot 10^{-11}$ | $2 \cdot 10^{-11}$ |

Stopping at a gap below $10^{-3}$ ends the run at iteration 1,422, where the true error is $9.9 \cdot 10^{-4}$, inside the guarantee. The gap is pessimistic early, when the dual iterate is poor, and tight near the end. At the solution 15 of the 40 points have $\alpha_i \gt 0$, the support vectors, and 12 of them sit at the bound $C$, inside the margin or misclassified.

## Where it breaks: a non-convex problem

Weak duality holds for any problem, so the dual still gives a bound when the primal is hard; strong duality is what fails. Split $n$ items into two groups to minimize $x^\top W x$ over sign vectors $x \in \{-1, 1\}^n$, so that $x^\top W x$ adds $W_{ij}$ when items $i$ and $j$ share a group and subtracts it when they do not. Written as $x_i^2 = 1$, the constraints are equalities. The Lagrangian is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(x, \nu) &= x^\top W x + \sum_i \nu_i (x_i^2 - 1) \\
&= x^\top \big(W + \operatorname{diag}(\nu)\big)x - \mathbf 1^\top\nu ,
\end{aligned}
$$

a quadratic form whose infimum over $x$ is 0 if the matrix is positive semidefinite and $-\infty$ otherwise. So $g(\nu) = -\mathbf 1^\top\nu$ when $W + \operatorname{diag}(\nu) \succeq 0$. The choice $\nu_i = -\lambda_{\min}(W)$ for every $i$ is feasible and gives the bound $p^* \ge n\,\lambda_{\min}(W)$ ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), §5.1.5; [Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 7).

For a random symmetric $8 \times 8$ matrix $W$, checking all 256 sign vectors gives $p^* = -22.235$. The eigenvalue bound is $-28.855$. The best multipliers, found by maximizing the concave dual function numerically, raise it to about $-22.83$. Numerical ascent only certifies $d^* \ge -22.83$, so the gap is at most about $0.6$; for problems of this kind it is typically not zero. The problem is combinatorial, and no convex dual can close the gap in general.

Two smaller failures appear in practice. Recovering a primal solution from a dual one needs the minimizer of the Lagrangian to be unique, which fails for linear programs, where the Lagrangian is linear in $x$. And the dual can be larger than the primal: the SVM dual has one variable per training point and a dense $n \times n$ matrix, so for millions of points the primal is solved instead, by stochastic gradients.

## What you should remember

- The dual function $g = \inf_x \mathcal L$ is concave, and $g(\lambda, \nu) \le p^*$ whenever $\lambda \ge 0$.
- $p^* = \inf_x \sup_\lambda \mathcal L$ and $d^* = \sup_\lambda \inf_x \mathcal L$; weak duality is the max–min inequality.
- Convex problem plus a strictly feasible point (Slater) gives $d^* = p^*$, and with it the KKT conditions.
- LP: the dual of $\min\{c^\top x : Ax \ge b,\ x \ge 0\}$ is $\max\{b^\top y : A^\top y \le c,\ y \ge 0\}$.
- The hard-margin SVM dual finds the nearest points of the two hulls; the margin is half their distance.
- $f(x) - g(\lambda, \nu)$ bounds the suboptimality of both points: a safe stopping rule.

## Code

The bound of the figure, a convex problem with a duality gap, an LP and its dual, norm-constrained least squares, the SVM dual as nearest points, an SVM solver stopped by its duality gap, and the two-way partitioning bound, with NumPy and SciPy. It runs in a few seconds. Exact digits can differ slightly across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
import itertools
from scipy.optimize import brentq, linprog, minimize

# min x^2 s.t. 1 - x <= 0: the dual function g(lam) = lam - lam^2/4 lies below p* = 1 and touches it at lam = 2
lam = np.linspace(0, 5, 501); g = lam - lam**2 / 4
print(g.max(), lam[g.argmax()], (g <= 1 + 1e-12).all())
# 1.0 2.0 True

# a convex problem without a Slater point: min e^{-x} s.t. x^2 / y <= 0 (y > 0); p* = 1 but g(lam) = 0
for x in (1., 5., 20., 100.):                              # along y = x^3 the Lagrangian tends to 0 for every lam
    print(x, f'{np.exp(-x) + 1.0 * x**2 / x**3:.4f}', end=' | ')
print()
# 1.0 1.3679 | 5.0 0.2067 | 20.0 0.0500 | 100.0 0.0100

# an LP and its dual: min 3x1 + 2x2 s.t. x1 + x2 >= 2, x1 + 3x2 >= 3, x >= 0;  max 2y1 + 3y2 s.t. y1 + y2 <= 3, y1 + 3y2 <= 2, y >= 0
A = np.array([[1., 1], [1, 3]]); b = np.array([2., 3]); c = np.array([3., 2])
pr = linprog(c, A_ub=-A, b_ub=-b, bounds=[(0, None)] * 2)
du = linprog(-b, A_ub=A.T, b_ub=c, bounds=[(0, None)] * 2)
print(pr.x, pr.fun, du.x, -du.fun, A @ pr.x - b, c - A.T @ du.x)   # slacks: primal constraints, dual constraints
# [0. 2.] 4.0 [2. 0.] 4.0 [0. 3.] [1. 0.]

# least squares under a norm constraint: min |Ax - b|^2 s.t. |x|^2 <= t; the dual is a function of one multiplier
A = np.array([[1., 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]]); b = np.array([1., 2, 2]); t = 1.0
x_lam = lambda l: np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + l * np.eye(2), A.T @ b)          # the minimizer of the Lagrangian: ridge
gd = lambda l: np.sum((A @ x_lam(l) - b) ** 2) + l * (x_lam(l) @ x_lam(l) - t)
ls = x_lam(0); print(ls.round(4), round(ls @ ls, 4), round(gd(0), 4))      # unconstrained solution is infeasible
lam_s = brentq(lambda l: x_lam(l) @ x_lam(l) - t, 0, 100); xs = x_lam(lam_s)
r = minimize(lambda x: np.sum((A @ x - b) ** 2), np.zeros(2), method='SLSQP',
             constraints=[{'type': 'ineq', 'fun': lambda x: t - x @ x}], options={'ftol': 1e-14})
print(round(lam_s, 4), xs.round(4), round(gd(lam_s), 6), round(r.fun, 6), [round(float(gd(l)), 4) for l in (0.5, 1, 2, 4)])
# [0.6667 1.6667] 3.2222 0.3333
# 2.0851 [0.5262 0.8504] 1.934833 1.934833 [1.1667, 1.625, 1.9333, 1.4]

# the hard-margin SVM of the ML note: the dual optimum is the nearest pair of points of the two convex hulls
P = np.array([[1., 2], [3, 3]]); Nn = np.array([[0., 0], [2, 0]])         # positive points C, D; negative points A, B
best = min(((np.linalg.norm(u @ P - v @ Nn), u, v) for s in np.linspace(0, 1, 401) for q in np.linspace(0, 1, 401)
            for u, v in [(np.array([s, 1 - s]), np.array([q, 1 - q]))]), key=lambda z: z[0])
D, u, v = best

# a soft-margin SVM (bias absorbed into w) solved by projected gradient ascent on its box-constrained dual;
# the duality gap P(w(alpha)) - D(alpha) bounds the suboptimality of every iterate
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, C = 40, 1.0
y = np.repeat([1., -1.], n // 2)
X = rng.normal(size=(n, 2)) + y[:, None]                     # two overlapping clouds around (1, 1) and (-1, -1)
Z = np.column_stack([X, np.ones(n)]) * y[:, None]            # rows y_i (x_i, 1)
Q = Z @ Z.T
Pobj = lambda w: 0.5 * w @ w + C * np.maximum(0, 1 - Z @ w).sum()
Dobj = lambda a: a.sum() - 0.5 * a @ Q @ a
eta = 1 / np.linalg.eigvalsh(Q)[-1]
a, hist = np.zeros(n), []
for k in range(1, 20_001):
    a = np.clip(a + eta * (1 - Q @ a), 0, C)                 # gradient step, then projection onto the box [0, C]
    hist.append((Pobj(Z.T @ a), Dobj(a)))
p_star = hist[-1][0]
print(round(p_star, 4), f'{hist[-1][0] - hist[-1][1]:.0e}', (a > 1e-8).sum(), (a > C - 1e-8).sum())
for k in (1, 10, 100, 1000, 5000):
    pk, dk = hist[k - 1]
    print(k, round(pk, 4), round(dk, 4), f'{pk - dk:.1e}', f'{pk - p_star:.1e}', end=' | ')
print()
stop = next(k for k, (pk, dk) in enumerate(hist, 1) if pk - dk < 1e-3)
print(stop, f'{hist[stop - 1][0] - p_star:.1e}', all(pk - p_star <= pk - dk + 1e-12 for pk, dk in hist))

# a non-convex problem: min x'Wx over x in {-1, 1}^8; the dual is g(nu) = -1'nu where W + diag(nu) is PSD
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 8
W = rng.normal(size=(n, n)); W = (W + W.T) / 2
p_star = min(np.array(x) @ W @ np.array(x) for x in itertools.product([-1, 1], repeat=n))
def dual(nu):                                                # shift nu to feasibility; value and a supergradient
    lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(W + np.diag(nu))
    return -nu.sum() + n * lam[0], -1 + n * V[:, 0] ** 2
nu, best = np.zeros(n), -np.inf
for k in range(1, 100_001):                                  # supergradient ascent on the concave dual
    val, g = dual(nu)
    best = max(best, val)
```

## Next

Every number in this section was computed in floating point and treated as exact. Mostly that is harmless, but not always. The Newton step solves a linear system whose matrix can be nearly singular, the ridge solution $x_\lambda$ can be computed through $A^\top A$ or through a factorization of $A$ with very different accuracy, and the softmax of the maximum-entropy solution overflows for large scores. The [next note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) measures how errors in the data and in the arithmetic grow through a computation, and which rewrites keep machine learning code accurate.
