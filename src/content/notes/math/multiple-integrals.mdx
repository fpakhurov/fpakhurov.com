Throw a dart whose two coordinates $X_1$ and $X_2$ are independent standard normal numbers. How often does it land within distance 1 of the centre? The answer is the integral of the joint density over the unit disc:

$$
P\big(X_1^2 + X_2^2 \le 1\big) = \iint_{x_1^2 + x_2^2 \le 1} \frac{1}{2\pi}\, e^{-(x_1^2 + x_2^2)/2}\, dx_1\, dx_2 .
$$

Integrating over $x_2$ first leaves the integral of $e^{-x_2^2/2}$ between $\pm\sqrt{1 - x_1^2}$, which has no elementary antiderivative ([Conrad](#references), Example 4.6; the fold in the Gaussian section says why). Yet the answer is exactly $1 - e^{-1/2} = 0.3935$, and it takes two lines once the disc is described by a radius and an angle.

Probability is written in integrals of this kind. A density must integrate to 1, an expectation is an integral, a marginal integrates a variable out, and a generative model that transforms random noise needs to know what the transform does to the density. Two tools compute them: reducing an integral over several variables to integrals over one, and changing the coordinates to ones that fit the region or the integrand.

## Fubini: one variable at a time

In one variable, $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$ is the limit of Riemann sums over finer and finer partitions. In two, cut a rectangle $R = [a, b] \times [c, d]$ into small cells of area $\Delta x\, \Delta y$, take a point $(x_i, y_j)$ in each, and let the cells shrink:

$$
\iint_R f\, dA = \lim \sum_{i}\sum_{j} f(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta x\, \Delta y .
$$

For $f \ge 0$ this is the volume under the surface $z = f(x, y)$; for a density $p(x, y)$ it is the probability of the rectangle. The definition says what the integral is, not how to compute it.

To compute it, add the cells column by column. The sum over a column $x = x_i$ is a Riemann sum in $y$, and the sum over the columns is then a Riemann sum in $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_R f\, dA &\approx \sum_i \Big(\sum_j f(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta y\Big)\, \Delta x \\
&\approx \sum_i \Big(\int_c^d f(x_i, y)\, dy\Big)\, \Delta x \\
&\approx \int_a^b \Big(\int_c^d f(x, y)\, dy\Big)\, dx .
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding the rows first gives the other order. For a continuous $f$ on a rectangle the approximations become equalities in the limit, which is **Fubini's theorem**: a double integral equals the **iterated integral** in either order. The proof squeezes the iterated integral between the lower and upper sums of the double one ([HSE, 2018](#references), Theorem 1), and the fold below gives it.

<details>
<summary>Proof: Fubini for a continuous function on a rectangle</summary>

Let $F(x) = \int_c^d f(x, y)\, dy$. A continuous function on a closed rectangle is uniformly continuous, so $\lvert F(x) - F(x')\rvert \le (d - c)\max_y \lvert f(x, y) - f(x', y)\rvert$ is small when $x'$ is close to $x$: $F$ is continuous and has an integral. Cut $[a, b]$ into intervals $I_k$ and $[c, d]$ into $J_l$, and let $m_{kl}$ and $M_{kl}$ be the minimum and maximum of $f$ on the cell $I_k \times J_l$. For $x \in I_k$, integrating $m_{kl} \le f(x, y) \le M_{kl}$ over each $J_l$ and adding:

$$
\sum_l m_{kl}\, \lvert J_l\rvert \le F(x) \le \sum_l M_{kl}\, \lvert J_l\rvert .
$$

Integrating over $I_k$ and adding over $k$ puts $\int_a^b F\, dx$ between the lower sum $\sum_{k,l} m_{kl}\lvert I_k\rvert\lvert J_l\rvert$ and the upper sum $\sum_{k,l} M_{kl}\lvert I_k\rvert\lvert J_l\rvert$ of the double integral. The double integral lies between the same two sums. By uniform continuity, $M_{kl} - m_{kl} \lt \varepsilon$ on fine enough cells, so the two sums differ by less than $\varepsilon\,(b - a)(d - c)$, and the two integrals are equal.

</details>

The theorem holds for every Riemann-integrable $f$ on a rectangle ([HSE, 2018](#references), Theorem 2). Over unbounded regions it becomes the Fubini–Tonelli theorem of measure theory: the iterated integrals in either order equal the double integral for every $f \ge 0$, where the value $+\infty$ is allowed, and for every $f$ with $\iint \lvert f\rvert < \infty$ ([Tao, 2011](#references), Theorem 1.7.18 and Corollary 1.7.23). For $f \ge 0$ the reason is the one above: a sum of non-negative terms does not depend on the order of summation. A signed $f$ is split as $f = f^+ - f^-$, and both parts need finite integrals.

For $f(x, y) = x^2 + xy$ on $[0, 1] \times [0, 2]$ the two orders agree:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 \!\! \int_0^2 (x^2 + xy)\, dy\, dx &= \int_0^1 (2x^2 + 2x)\, dx = \tfrac53 , \\
\int_0^2 \!\! \int_0^1 (x^2 + xy)\, dx\, dy &= \int_0^2 \big(\tfrac13 + \tfrac y2\big)\, dy = \tfrac53 .
\end{aligned}
$$

### Regions that are not rectangles

On other regions the inner limits depend on the outer variable. To justify it, extend $f$ by zero to a rectangle around the region. The extension is bounded and discontinuous only on the boundary curve, a set of zero area, so it is Riemann integrable ([Lebl](#references), Theorem 10.4.3) and Fubini applies to it. On the triangle $0 \le y \le x \le 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1 \!\! \int_0^x xy\, dy\, dx &= \int_0^1 \frac{x^3}{2}\, dx = \frac18 , \\
\int_0^1 \!\! \int_y^1 xy\, dx\, dy &= \int_0^1 \frac{y(1 - y^2)}{2}\, dy = \frac18 .
\end{aligned}
$$

In probability this is how a marginal is computed, $p_X(x) = \int p(x, y)\, dy$, as in the [joint distributions note](/notes/probability/joint-distributions#joint-and-marginal-distributions), and how $P(Y \le X)$ becomes an integral over a triangle.

### When the order matters

The condition $\iint \lvert f\rvert < \infty$ is not a technicality. On the unit square, $f(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)^2$ is the derivative of $y/(x^2 + y^2)$ in $y$. So $\int_0^1 f\, dy = 1/(1 + x^2)$, and the iterated integral in this order is $\int_0^1 dx/(1 + x^2) = \pi/4$. Exchanging $x$ and $y$ flips the sign of $f$, so the other order gives $-\pi/4$. The two orders disagree because $\lvert f\rvert$ is not integrable near the corner $(0, 0)$. In the polar coordinates of the next section, $x^2 - y^2 = r^2\cos 2\theta$ and $(x^2 + y^2)^2 = r^4$, so $\lvert f\rvert = \lvert\cos 2\theta\rvert/r^2$. Over the quarter disc $r \le 1$ inside the square, with the area element $r\, dr\, d\theta$:

$$
\iint \lvert f\rvert \ge \int_0^{\pi/2} \lvert\cos 2\theta\rvert\, d\theta \int_0^1 \frac{dr}{r} .
$$

The angular integral is 1, and the radial one diverges at $r = 0$.

## Polar coordinates

In polar coordinates a point of the plane is $(x, y) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ with $r \ge 0$ and $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$. A disc or an annulus becomes a rectangle in $(r, \theta)$, and a function of the distance alone, such as $e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}$, becomes a function of $r$ alone. The price is the area element. A small polar cell between the radii $r \pm \tfrac12\Delta r$ and the angles $\theta$ and $\theta + \Delta\theta$ is nearly a rectangle with sides $\Delta r$ and $r\,\Delta\theta$, the length of an arc of radius $r$:

$$
dA = dx\, dy = r\, dr\, d\theta .
$$

For such a cell this is exact: by the sector formula $\tfrac12\rho^2\Delta\theta$, its area is $\tfrac12\big((r + \tfrac12\Delta r)^2 - (r - \tfrac12\Delta r)^2\big)\Delta\theta = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta$. The factor $r$ is the determinant of the Jacobian of the map $(r, \theta) \mapsto (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
J &= \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix} , \\
\det J &= r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r .
\end{aligned}
$$

## The Gaussian integral

The integral $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\, dx$ has no elementary antiderivative, yet its value is exact. Its square is easier. Read $I^2$ as a double integral over the plane and switch to polar coordinates, where the extra factor $r$ makes the integrand an exact derivative, $\tfrac{d}{dr}\big(-\tfrac12 e^{-r^2}\big) = r e^{-r^2}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I^2 &= \int_{\mathbb R} e^{-x^2}\, dx \int_{\mathbb R} e^{-y^2}\, dy \\
&= \iint_{\mathbb R^2} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\, dx\, dy && \text{Fubini} \\
&= \int_0^{2\pi} \!\! \int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2}\, r\, dr\, d\theta && \text{polar coordinates} \\
&= 2\pi \cdot \Big[-\tfrac12 e^{-r^2}\Big]_0^\infty = \pi .
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $I > 0$, we get $I = \sqrt\pi$. The polar map is a bijection from $(0, \infty) \times (0, 2\pi)$ onto the plane without the ray $\{y = 0,\ x \ge 0\}$, and a ray has zero area, so leaving it out changes no integral.

<details>
<summary>Proof outline: no elementary antiderivative for the Gaussian</summary>

An elementary function is built from rational functions, exponentials, logarithms and roots by finitely many operations and compositions. Liouville's theorem, in the form of [Conrad](#references), Theorem 4.4, reduces the question to algebra: $e^{-x^2}$ has an elementary antiderivative only if it has one of the form $r(x)e^{-x^2}$ with $r$ rational, that is, only if $r' - 2xr = 1$. No rational $r$ solves this. A pole of order $k$ of $r$ is a pole of order $k + 1$ of $r'$ and at most of order $k$ of $2xr$, so the left side would have a pole; hence $r$ is a polynomial. A polynomial $r \ne 0$ of degree $m$ makes the left side a polynomial of degree $m + 1 \ge 1$, and $r = 0$ makes it 0. Neither is the constant 1 (Example 4.6 there).

</details>

The plane is unbounded, so the second and third lines are limits: over squares $[-R, R]^2$ in Cartesian coordinates and over discs of radius $R$ in polar ones. The integrand is positive, so the integral over the square lies between the integrals over the disc of radius $R$ inside it and the disc of radius $R\sqrt 2$ around it, and both discs tend to $\pi$:

| $R$ | disc of radius $R$ | square $[-R, R]^2$ | disc of radius $R\sqrt 2$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | $3.0841$ | $3.1123$ | $3.1405$ |
| 3 | $3.1412$ | $3.1415$ | $3.1416$ |

The substitution $x = u/\sqrt 2$ gives $\int e^{-u^2/2}\, du = \sqrt{2\pi}$, which is why the [standard normal density](/notes/probability/continuous-distributions#normal) carries the factor $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

## The change-of-variables formula

Polar coordinates are one case of a general rule. Let $T: U \to V$ be a bijection between open sets of $\mathbb R^n$, continuously differentiable with a continuously differentiable inverse, and let $J_T(x)$ be its Jacobian. For $f \ge 0$ on $V$, or $f$ with $\int_V \lvert f\rvert \lt \infty$:

$$
\boxed{\; \int_V f(y)\, dy = \int_U f\big(T(x)\big)\, \big\lvert\det J_T(x)\big\rvert\, dx \;}
$$

To see why, cut $U$ into small cubes $C_k$ with centres $x_k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_V f(y)\, dy &= \sum_k \int_{T(C_k)} f(y)\, dy \\
&\approx \sum_k f\big(T(x_k)\big)\, \operatorname{vol}\big(T(C_k)\big) \\
&\approx \sum_k f\big(T(x_k)\big)\, \big\lvert\det J_T(x_k)\big\rvert\, \operatorname{vol}(C_k) .
\end{aligned}
$$

1. The images $T(C_k)$ tile $V$.
2. On a small region $f$ is nearly constant.
3. On a small cube $T$ is close to the affine map $x_k + J_T(x_k)(x - x_k)$, and an affine map multiplies volumes by the absolute value of its determinant, as in the [determinants note](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#the-determinant-how-a-map-scales-volume) and the [Jacobian of a small square](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians#the-jacobian-of-a-vector-function).

The last sum is a Riemann sum for the right side of the formula. The precise theorem ([Lebl](#references), Theorem 10.7.2) takes a compact set $S \subset U$ whose boundary has zero volume (a box, a ball, a polygon) and a Riemann-integrable $f$ on $T(S)$, and states $\int_{T(S)} f = \int_S (f \circ T)\, \lvert\det J_T\rvert$. For $f \ge 0$ the boxed form follows by letting $S$ grow to $U$, balls of radius $R \to \infty$ for example, and passing to the limit on both sides; a signed $f$ is split into $f^+ - f^-$. The proof turns the second approximation into two inequalities.

<details>
<summary>Proof outline: why the approximations become exact</summary>

1. On a cube $C$ of side $s$ around $x_C$, write $T = A \circ \tilde T$ with $A = J_T(x_C)$. Then $\tilde T$ has Jacobian $A^{-1}J_T$, equal to $I$ at $x_C$. Because $J_T$ is uniformly continuous on $S$ and $\lVert A^{-1}\rVert$ is bounded there, small enough cubes keep $\lVert A^{-1}J_T(y) - I\rVert \le \varepsilon$ on all of $C$.
2. The fundamental theorem of calculus along the segment from $x_C$ to $y$ gives $\lVert \tilde T(y) - \tilde T(x_C) - (y - x_C)\rVert \le \varepsilon\lVert y - x_C\rVert \le \varepsilon\sqrt n\, s/2$. So $\tilde T(C)$ lies in the cube of side $s(1 + \sqrt n\,\varepsilon)$ around $\tilde T(x_C)$.
3. The linear map $A$ multiplies volumes by $\lvert\det A\rvert$, so $\operatorname{vol} T(C) \le (1 + \sqrt n\,\varepsilon)^n \lvert\det J_T(x_C)\rvert \operatorname{vol}(C)$.
4. For $f \ge 0$, with $x_C$ chosen where $\lvert\det J_T\rvert$ is smallest on $C$, summing $\int_{T(C)} f \le \sup_{T(C)} f \cdot \operatorname{vol} T(C)$ over the cubes bounds $\int_{T(S)} f$ by $(1 + \sqrt n\,\varepsilon)^n$ times an upper Riemann sum of $(f \circ T)\lvert\det J_T\rvert$.
5. The same argument for $T^{-1}$ gives the reverse inequality, and $\varepsilon \to 0$ closes the gap ([Lebl](#references) carries out these steps).

</details>

In one variable the formula is the substitution rule. If $F' = f$, the chain rule gives $(F \circ g)' = f(g)\,g'$, and the fundamental theorem of calculus gives $\int_a^b f(g(x))\,g'(x)\, dx = F(g(b)) - F(g(a)) = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(y)\, dy$. For a decreasing $g$ the limits on the right come in the wrong order; putting them in order flips the sign, and $g' \lt 0$ absorbs it, so $\int_{g([a, b])} f(y)\, dy = \int_a^b f(g(x))\,\lvert g'(x)\rvert\, dx$. The absolute value records that an interval run backwards still has a positive length.

The ellipse $x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 \le 1$ is the image of the unit disc under $T(u, v) = (au, bv)$, whose Jacobian $\operatorname{diag}(a, b)$ has determinant $ab$. Its area is therefore $ab$ times the area $\pi$ of the disc, $\pi ab$; for $a = 3$ and $b = 2$ that is $6\pi = 18.850$.

The same move normalizes every Gaussian. For a symmetric positive definite $\Sigma$ with [Cholesky factorization](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-cholesky-factorization) $\Sigma = LL^\top$, substitute $x = Lz$, a linear map with Jacobian $L$. Then $\Sigma^{-1} = L^{-\top}L^{-1}$ gives $x^\top \Sigma^{-1}x = (L^{-1}x)^\top(L^{-1}x) = z^\top z$, and $\det\Sigma = \det L \det L^\top = (\det L)^2$ gives $\lvert\det L\rvert = \sqrt{\det\Sigma}$. The integrand $e^{-z^\top z/2}$ is the product of the $e^{-z_i^2/2}$, so Tonelli splits the integral into $n$ one-variable Gaussians:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R^n} e^{-\frac12 x^\top \Sigma^{-1}x}\, dx &= \sqrt{\det\Sigma} \int_{\mathbb R^n} e^{-\frac12 z^\top z}\, dz \\
&= \sqrt{\det\Sigma}\, \prod_{i=1}^n \int_{\mathbb R} e^{-z_i^2/2}\, dz_i \\
&= (2\pi)^{n/2} \sqrt{\det\Sigma} .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $\Sigma$ with rows $(2, 1.2)$ and $(1.2, 1)$, $\det\Sigma = 0.56$ and the integral is $2\pi\sqrt{0.56} = 4.702$, which numerical integration confirms. This is the normalizing constant of the [multivariate Gaussian density](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#the-density).

## Densities under a transform

If a random vector $X$ has density $p_X$ and $T$ is a change of variables as above, what is the density of $Y = T(X)$? The probability of a set $B$ of values of $Y$ is the probability of its preimage, an integral over $x$. Changing variables with the map $T^{-1}$ turns it into an integral over $B$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Y \in B) &= P\big(X \in T^{-1}(B)\big) \\
&= \int_{T^{-1}(B)} p_X(x)\, dx \\
&= \int_B p_X\big(T^{-1}(y)\big)\, \big\lvert\det J_{T^{-1}}(y)\big\rvert\, dy .
\end{aligned}
$$

This holds for every set $B$, so the last integrand is the density of $Y$. The chain rule applied to $T^{-1}(T(x)) = x$ gives $J_{T^{-1}}(y) = J_T(x)^{-1}$, and $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A$, so

$$
p_Y(y) = \frac{p_X(x)}{\big\lvert\det J_T(x)\big\rvert} ,
$$

where $x = T^{-1}(y)$. Where $T$ stretches volumes, $\lvert\det J_T\rvert > 1$, the same probability is spread over more volume and the density falls; where it compresses, the density rises. An integral picks up $\lvert\det J\rvert$ and a density divides by it, because their product, probability, is what is conserved ([Deisenroth, 2025](#references), Appendix B).

Now the opening question. Let $(R, \Theta)$ be the polar coordinates of the two independent standard normals $(X_1, X_2)$. Apply the formula to the map $T$ from $(x_1, x_2)$ to $(r, \theta)$. Its inverse, the polar map, has $\lvert\det J_{T^{-1}}\rvert = r$, so by the last line of the derivation above the density of $(R, \Theta)$ is the joint density of $(X_1, X_2)$ times $r$:

$$
p_{R,\Theta}(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot r e^{-r^2/2} .
$$

The density factorizes, so the angle and the radius are independent: the angle is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$, and the radius has density $r e^{-r^2/2}$. The dart lands within distance 1 with probability

$$
P(R \le 1) = \int_0^1 r e^{-r^2/2}\, dr = 1 - e^{-1/2} = 0.3935 ,
$$

the share of a two-dimensional Gaussian inside its "one sigma" circle quoted in the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#covariance-as-geometry). Read backwards, the same computation is the Box–Muller method that [produces normal samples](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#producing-samples) from uniform ones. In one dimension the formula is the [change-of-variables formula of the probability course](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables#the-change-of-variables-formula). Stacked over a chain of learned invertible maps, it gives the exact likelihood of a [normalizing flow](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows#change-of-variables), whose architectures are designed so that $\det J$ is cheap.

## The volume of a ball in n dimensions

How large is the unit ball in ten dimensions? Two uses of the change of variables answer it. The scaling $x \mapsto rx$ has Jacobian $rI$ and determinant $r^n$, so the ball of radius $r$ has volume $V_n r^n$, where $V_n$ is the volume of the unit ball. A function of the distance alone can then be integrated over thin shells: the shell between the radii $r$ and $r + dr$ has volume $d(V_n r^n) = nV_n r^{n-1}\, dr$, and summing over shells is a Riemann sum in $r$. For a continuous $g$, the fold turns this into $\int_{\lVert x\rVert \le R} g(\lVert x\rVert)\, dx = \int_0^R g(r)\, nV_n r^{n-1}\, dr$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: integrating over shells</summary>

Let $\Phi(r) = \int_{\lVert x\rVert \le r} g(\lVert x\rVert)\, dx$. For $h > 0$, $\Phi(r + h) - \Phi(r)$ is the integral over the shell $r \lt \lVert x\rVert \le r + h$, of volume $V_n\big((r + h)^n - r^n\big)$. On the shell $g(\lVert x\rVert)$ lies between the minimum and the maximum of $g$ on $[r, r + h]$, so

$$
\min_{[r, r + h]} g \le \frac{\Phi(r + h) - \Phi(r)}{V_n\big((r + h)^n - r^n\big)} \le \max_{[r, r + h]} g .
$$

As $h \to 0$ both bounds tend to $g(r)$ by continuity, and $V_n\big((r + h)^n - r^n\big)/h \to nV_n r^{n-1}$. So $\Phi'(r) = g(r)\, nV_n r^{n-1}$, and the fundamental theorem of calculus with $\Phi(0) = 0$ gives the formula. For $g(r) = e^{-r^2}$, let $R \to \infty$.

</details>

Compute the $n$-dimensional Gaussian integral both ways. By Fubini it is a product of $n$ one-dimensional integrals, each equal to $\sqrt\pi$; by shells it is an integral over $r$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi^{n/2} &= \int_{\mathbb R^n} e^{-\lVert x\rVert^2}\, dx \\
&= \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\, nV_n r^{n-1}\, dr \\
&= \frac n2\, V_n\, \Gamma\big(\tfrac n2\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last step substitutes $u = r^2$, which turns $\int_0^\infty r^{n-1}e^{-r^2}\, dr$ into $\tfrac12\Gamma(n/2)$, where $\Gamma(s) = \int_0^\infty u^{s-1}e^{-u}\, du$ is the gamma function. Integrating by parts, $\Gamma(s + 1) = \big[-u^s e^{-u}\big]_0^\infty + s\int_0^\infty u^{s-1}e^{-u}\, du = s\,\Gamma(s)$ for $s > 0$. With $\tfrac n2\Gamma(\tfrac n2) = \Gamma(\tfrac n2 + 1)$,

$$
V_n = \frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\big(\tfrac n2 + 1\big)} .
$$

From $\Gamma(1) = 1$ and the recursion, $\Gamma(k + 1) = k!$; the substitution $u = x^2$ turns $\Gamma(\tfrac12)$ into $2\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\, dx = \sqrt\pi$. For $n = 2$ this gives $\pi$, and for $n = 3$, with $\Gamma(\tfrac52) = \tfrac32 \cdot \tfrac12 \sqrt\pi$, it gives $4\pi/3$. For $n = 10$, $\Gamma(6) = 5! = 120$ and $V_{10} = \pi^5/120 = 2.550$. The ball sits inside the cube $[-1, 1]^n$ of volume $2^n$:

| $n$ | $V_n$ | share of the cube $[-1, 1]^n$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | $3.142$ | $78.5\%$ |
| 3 | $4.189$ | $52.4\%$ |
| 5 | $5.264$ | $16.4\%$ |
| 10 | $2.550$ | $0.25\%$ |
| 20 | $0.0258$ | $2.5 \cdot 10^{-8}$ |

In ten dimensions almost all of the cube's volume lies in its corners, outside the inscribed ball.

## High dimensions: why grids fail

Fubini reduces an $n$-dimensional integral to $n$ nested one-dimensional ones, and a numerical rule with $k$ points per axis then needs $k^n$ evaluations of the integrand. A million evaluations buy $10^{6/10} = 3.98$ points per axis in ten dimensions. The midpoint rule has error proportional to the squared spacing (the fold derives it), and the spacing shrinks like $N^{-1/n}$ for $N$ points, so the error falls like $N^{-2/n}$: in ten dimensions a hundred times more points cut it by a factor of only $100^{0.2} = 2.5$. [Deisenroth (2025)](#references), Table 3, gives a rule of thumb: Gaussian quadrature up to about three dimensions, Bayesian quadrature up to about ten, and Monte Carlo beyond.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: the error of the midpoint rule</summary>

Take a cell of side $h$ with centre $m$, and let $M$ bound the norm of the Hessian of $f$. The [second-order Taylor expansion](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians#the-hessian-and-the-second-order-taylor-expansion) with its remainder gives $f(x) = f(m) + \nabla f(m)^\top(x - m) + \rho(x)$ with $\lvert\rho(x)\rvert \le \tfrac M2\lVert x - m\rVert^2$. The linear term integrates to zero over the cell, which is symmetric about $m$. Each coordinate contributes $h^{n-1}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2} t^2\, dt = h^{n+2}/12$ to the integral of $\lVert x - m\rVert^2$, so

$$
\Big\lvert \int_{\text{cell}} f\, dx - h^n f(m)\Big\rvert \le \frac M2 \cdot \frac{n\,h^{n+2}}{12} .
$$

Adding over the $\operatorname{vol}(D)/h^n$ cells, the total error is at most $\tfrac{nM}{24}\operatorname{vol}(D)\, h^2$.

</details>

**Monte Carlo integration** writes the integral as an expectation, $\int_D f(x)\, dx = \operatorname{vol}(D)\, \mathbb E[f(U)]$ with $U$ uniform on $D$, and averages $f$ over $N$ random points. By the central limit theorem its error is about $\sigma/\sqrt N$, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of $\operatorname{vol}(D) f(U)$, and the rate $1/\sqrt N$ does not depend on the dimension. The estimator and its error bar belong to the [Monte Carlo note](/notes/probability/monte-carlo) of the probability course.

The table above shows the catch. A hit-or-miss estimate of $V_{10}$ from points in the cube sees the ball once in 400 draws, so $\sigma$ is large next to the answer. The estimate is $2^{10}$ times the share $\hat q$ of hits, and the share is a mean of $N$ indicators with success probability $q = 0.00249$. Its standard deviation is $\sqrt{q(1 - q)/N}$, so the relative error is $\sqrt{(1 - q)/(qN)} = 0.020$ for $N = 10^6$: the 2% that the [law of large numbers note](/notes/probability/law-of-large-numbers#monte-carlo-estimation) finds.

## Where it breaks

The change of variables needs a bijection. Let $X$ be standard normal and $Y = X^2$. Applied to the branch $x = \sqrt y$ alone, the formula gives $p_X(\sqrt y)/(2\sqrt y)$, the Jacobian of $x \mapsto x^2$ being $2x$. That function integrates to $\tfrac12$ over $y > 0$, since it is the density of $X$ restricted to $x > 0$. Every $y > 0$ has two preimages, $\pm\sqrt y$, and the density of $Y$ adds their contributions:

$$
p_Y(y) = \frac{p_X(\sqrt y) + p_X(-\sqrt y)}{2\sqrt y} = \frac{e^{-y/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi y}} ,
$$

the chi-squared density with one degree of freedom. For a map between spaces of different dimensions the Jacobian is not square and the formula has no determinant at all. The generator of a GAN maps a noise vector of dimension $k$ into an image space of dimension $n > k$, and its image has zero volume there. Cut a noise cube $[0, 1]^k$ into $N^k$ small cubes of diameter $\sqrt k/N$. A generator built from linear maps and Lipschitz activations is Lipschitz with some constant $\ell$, so it sends each small cube into a ball of radius $\ell\sqrt k/N$. The total volume is at most $N^k V_n(\ell\sqrt k/N)^n$, which tends to 0 as $N \to \infty$ because $k \lt n$. A density $p$ would give the image probability $\int_{\text{image}} p = 0$, yet every generated image lies in it, so the images have no density in the ordinary sense.

The determinant itself costs $O(n^3)$ operations for a general map of $n$ variables, the cost of [elimination](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#what-elimination-costs). That is why normalizing flows restrict themselves to maps with triangular or otherwise structured Jacobians, whose determinant is the [product of the diagonal](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#properties-used-everywhere). And closed forms are the exception. The Gaussian integral works because its integrand depends on the radius alone, so the polar factor $r$ makes it an exact derivative; the evidence of a Bayesian model or the normalizer of an energy-based model has no closed form and is approximated by sampling or by variational bounds.

## What you should remember

- Fubini: a double integral is two single ones, in either order, if $\iint\lvert f\rvert < \infty$.
- Polar coordinates: $dx\, dy = r\, dr\, d\theta$, and $r = \det J$ of the polar map.
- $\int e^{-x^2} dx = \sqrt\pi$; hence $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ and $(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}$ in Gaussian densities.
- Integrals pick up $\lvert\det J_T\rvert$; densities divide by it: $p_Y(y) = p_X(x)/\lvert\det J_T(x)\rvert$.
- A many-to-one map needs a sum over all preimages.
- Grids need $k^n$ points; the unit ball fills 0.25% of the cube in ten dimensions.

## Code

Fubini in both orders and its counterexample, the polar Jacobian and one polar cell, the Gaussian integral squeezed between discs and squares, the Gaussian normalizer and the ellipse by numerical integration, the polar coordinates of two normals, the volume of the unit ball, and the density of $X^2$. Random numbers come from a fixed seed; exact digits may vary across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import gamma, pi
from scipy import integrate, special

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Fubini: the same integral in both orders, on a rectangle and on a triangle
f = lambda x, y: x ** 2 + x * y
print(round(integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: f(x, y), 0, 1, 0, 2)[0], 6),    # dy inside, x in [0, 1]
      round(integrate.dblquad(lambda x, y: f(x, y), 0, 2, 0, 1)[0], 6))    # dx inside, y in [0, 2]
print(round(integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: x * y, 0, 1, 0, lambda x: x)[0], 6),
      round(integrate.dblquad(lambda x, y: x * y, 0, 1, lambda y: y, 1)[0], 6))
# 1.666667 1.666667 | 0.125 0.125

# where Fubini fails: (x^2 - y^2) / (x^2 + y^2)^2 on the unit square, not absolutely integrable
g = lambda x, y: (x * x - y * y) / (x * x + y * y) ** 2
dy_first = integrate.quad(lambda x: integrate.quad(lambda y: g(x, y), 0, 1)[0], 0, 1, limit=200)[0]
dx_first = integrate.quad(lambda y: integrate.quad(lambda x: g(x, y), 0, 1)[0], 0, 1, limit=200)[0]
print(round(dy_first, 6), round(dx_first, 6), round(np.pi / 4, 6))   # 0.785398 -0.785398 0.785398

# the polar Jacobian, and the area of one polar cell
r, th = 1.5, np.radians(30)
J = np.array([[np.cos(th), -r * np.sin(th)], [np.sin(th), r * np.cos(th)]])
dr, dth = 0.25, np.radians(20)
print(round(np.linalg.det(J), 6), round(0.5 * ((r + dr / 2) ** 2 - (r - dr / 2) ** 2) * dth, 4), round(r * dr * dth, 4))
# 1.5 0.1309 0.1309

# the Gaussian integral: discs and squares squeeze the limit to pi
for R in (1, 2, 3):
    print(R, round(np.pi * (1 - np.exp(-R * R)), 4), round(np.pi * special.erf(R) ** 2, 4), round(np.pi * (1 - np.exp(-2 * R * R)), 4))
# 1 1.9859 2.231 2.7164 | 2 3.0841 3.1123 3.1405 | 3 3.1412 3.1415 3.1416
print(round(integrate.quad(lambda x: np.exp(-x * x), -np.inf, np.inf)[0] ** 2, 10), round(np.pi, 10))   # 3.1415926536 3.1415926536

# the Gaussian normalizer in two dimensions: (2 pi) sqrt(det Sigma), by the substitution x = L z
Sig = np.array([[2, 1.2], [1.2, 1]]); P = np.linalg.inv(Sig)
num = integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: np.exp(-0.5 * np.array([x, y]) @ P @ np.array([x, y])), -12, 12, -9, 9)[0]
print(round(num, 4), round(2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(np.linalg.det(Sig)), 4))   # 4.7019 4.7019

# an ellipse: area pi a b through the map (u, v) -> (a u, b v)
a_, b_ = 3, 2
ell = integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: 1.0, -a_, a_, lambda x: -b_ * np.sqrt(1 - (x / a_) ** 2), lambda x: b_ * np.sqrt(1 - (x / a_) ** 2))[0]
print(round(ell, 4), round(np.pi * a_ * b_, 4))   # 18.8496 18.8496

# densities under a transform: two standard normals in polar coordinates
Z = rng.normal(size=(1_000_000, 2)); Rr = np.hypot(Z[:, 0], Z[:, 1]); Th = np.arctan2(Z[:, 1], Z[:, 0])
print(round((Rr <= 1).mean(), 4), round(1 - np.exp(-0.5), 4), round((Rr ** 2).mean(), 3), round(np.corrcoef(Rr, Th)[0, 1], 4))
# 0.3934 0.3935 1.999 -0.0011: P(R <= 1) and its formula, E[R^2] = 2, no correlation between radius and angle

# the volume of the unit ball, pi^(n/2) / Gamma(n/2 + 1), and its share of the cube [-1, 1]^n
for n in (2, 3, 5, 10, 20):
    V = pi ** (n / 2) / gamma(n / 2 + 1)
    print(n, round(V, 4), f'{V / 2 ** n:.3g}', end=' | ')
print()
# 2 3.1416 0.785 | 3 4.1888 0.524 | 5 5.2638 0.164 | 10 2.5502 0.00249 | 20 0.0258 2.46e-08
print(round(1e6 ** 0.1, 2), round(100 ** 0.2, 2))   # 3.98 2.51: points per axis, error reduction for 100 times more points

# a many-to-one map: Y = X^2; one branch integrates to 1/2, both branches give the chi-squared density
pX = lambda x: np.exp(-x * x / 2) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi)
one = integrate.quad(lambda y: pX(np.sqrt(y)) / (2 * np.sqrt(y)), 0, np.inf)[0]
both = integrate.quad(lambda y: (pX(np.sqrt(y)) + pX(-np.sqrt(y))) / (2 * np.sqrt(y)), 0, np.inf)[0]
print(round(one, 6), round(both, 6))   # 0.5 1.0
```
