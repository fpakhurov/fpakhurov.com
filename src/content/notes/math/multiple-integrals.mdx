The [previous note](/notes/math/matrix-calculus) finished the derivatives: it differentiated whole expressions in vectors and matrices. The other half of calculus enters machine learning through probability. A density must integrate to 1, an expectation is an integral, a marginal integrates a variable out, and a generative model that transforms random noise needs to know what the transform does to the density. All of these are integrals over several variables. This note defines them and computes them one variable at a time with Fubini's theorem, changes coordinates with the factor $\lvert\det J\rvert$ that the [Jacobian section](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians#the-jacobian-of-a-vector-function) showed to be a local volume change, derives the Gaussian integral $\sqrt\pi$ that normalizes every Gaussian density, and ends with Monte Carlo integration for the dimensions where nothing else works.

**Notation.** Vectors are lowercase letters, written as columns, and matrices are uppercase. $D \subset \mathbb R^2$ or $\mathbb R^n$ is a region, $\iint_D f\, dA$ or $\int_D f(x)\, dx$ the integral of $f$ over it, and $\operatorname{vol}(D)$ its area or volume. $T: U \to V$ is a change of variables between open sets of $\mathbb R^n$, $J_T(x)$ its Jacobian and $\lvert\det J_T(x)\rvert$ the absolute value of its determinant. $(r, \theta)$ are polar coordinates. $p_X$ is the density of a random vector $X$.

## Baseline: the integral as a limit of sums

In one variable, $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$ is the limit of Riemann sums $\sum_i f(x_i)\, \Delta x$ over finer and finer partitions. In two variables, cut a rectangle $R = [a, b] \times [c, d]$ into small cells of area $\Delta x\, \Delta y$, take a point $(x_i, y_j)$ in each, and let the cells shrink:

$$
\iint_R f\, dA = \lim \sum_{i}\sum_{j} f(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta x\, \Delta y .
$$

For $f \ge 0$ it is the volume under the surface $z = f(x, y)$; for a density $p(x, y)$ it is the probability of the rectangle. The definition says what the integral is, not how to compute it. With 100 cells per axis, a grid has $10^4$ cells in two dimensions and $10^{20}$ in ten.

## Fubini: one variable at a time

The idea: a finite double sum can be added row by row or column by column, and each inner sum is a one-variable Riemann sum. In the limit, a double integral becomes two single integrals, in either order. For $f$ continuous on the rectangle $R$:

$$
\boxed{\; \iint_R f\, dA = \int_a^b \Big(\int_c^d f(x, y)\, dy\Big) dx = \int_c^d \Big(\int_a^b f(x, y)\, dx\Big) dy \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint_R f\, dA &\approx \sum_i \sum_j f(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta y\, \Delta x && \text{a Riemann sum on a grid} \\
&= \sum_i \Big(\sum_j f(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta y\Big)\, \Delta x && \text{add each column } x = x_i \text{ first} \\
&\approx \sum_i \Big(\int_c^d f(x_i, y)\, dy\Big)\, \Delta x && \text{the inner sum is a Riemann sum in } y \\
&\approx \int_a^b \Big(\int_c^d f(x, y)\, dy\Big)\, dx && \text{the outer sum is a Riemann sum in } x .
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding the rows first gives the other order. For a continuous $f$ on a rectangle the approximations become equalities in the limit; the general theorem (Fubini and Tonelli) allows any $f$ with $\iint \lvert f\rvert < \infty$, or any $f \ge 0$, and unbounded regions.

**Worked example.** $f(x, y) = x^2 + xy$ on $[0, 1] \times [0, 2]$. Integrating $y$ first, $\int_0^2 (x^2 + xy)\, dy = 2x^2 + 2x$, and $\int_0^1 (2x^2 + 2x)\, dx = \tfrac23 + 1 = \tfrac53$. Integrating $x$ first, $\int_0^1 (x^2 + xy)\, dx = \tfrac13 + \tfrac y2$, and $\int_0^2 \big(\tfrac13 + \tfrac y2\big)\, dy = \tfrac23 + 1 = \tfrac53$.

**Regions that are not rectangles.** The inner limits then depend on the outer variable. On the triangle $0 \le y \le x \le 1$, $\iint xy\, dA = \int_0^1 \int_0^x xy\, dy\, dx = \int_0^1 \tfrac{x^3}{2}\, dx = \tfrac18$; in the other order, $\int_0^1 \int_y^1 xy\, dx\, dy = \int_0^1 \tfrac{y(1 - y^2)}{2}\, dy = \tfrac14 - \tfrac18 = \tfrac18$. In probability this is how a marginal is computed, $p_X(x) = \int p(x, y)\, dy$, as in the [joint distributions note](/notes/probability/joint-distributions#joint-and-marginal-distributions), and how $P(Y \le X)$ is an integral over a triangle.

**When the order matters.** The condition $\iint \lvert f\rvert < \infty$ is not a technicality. On the unit square, $f(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)^2$ is the derivative of $y/(x^2 + y^2)$ in $y$, so $\int_0^1 f\, dy = 1/(1 + x^2)$ and the iterated integral in this order is $\int_0^1 dx/(1 + x^2) = \pi/4$. Exchanging $x$ and $y$ flips the sign of $f$, so the other order gives $-\pi/4$. The two orders disagree because $\lvert f\rvert$ is not integrable near the corner $(0, 0)$.

## Polar coordinates

In polar coordinates a point of the plane is $(x, y) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ with $r \ge 0$ and $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$. A region that is round in $(x, y)$, a disc or an annulus, is a rectangle in $(r, \theta)$, and a function of the distance alone, such as $e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}$, becomes a function of $r$ alone. The price is the area element. A small polar cell between the radii $r - \tfrac{\Delta r}{2}$ and $r + \tfrac{\Delta r}{2}$ and between the angles $\theta$ and $\theta + \Delta\theta$ is nearly a rectangle with sides $\Delta r$ and $r\,\Delta\theta$, the length of an arc of radius $r$:

$$
dA = dx\, dy = r\, dr\, d\theta .
$$

For a cell centred at the radius $r$ this is even exact: the cell's area is $\tfrac12\big((r + \tfrac{\Delta r}{2})^2 - (r - \tfrac{\Delta r}{2})^2\big)\Delta\theta = r\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta$. With $r = 1.5$, $\Delta r = 0.25$ and $\Delta\theta = 20° = 0.349$, a cell of area $\Delta r\,\Delta\theta = 0.0873$ in the $(r, \theta)$ plane becomes a cell of area $1.5 \cdot 0.0873 = 0.1309$ in the $(x, y)$ plane. The factor $r$ is the determinant of the Jacobian of the map $(r, \theta) \mapsto (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$:

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det J = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r .
$$

## The Gaussian integral

The integral $I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\, dx$ has no elementary antiderivative to evaluate, yet its value is exact. The idea: square it, read the square as a double integral over the plane, and switch to polar coordinates, where the extra factor $r$ makes the integrand an exact derivative.

$$
\boxed{\; \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\, dx = \sqrt\pi \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
I^2 &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\, dx \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2}\, dy && \text{the name of the variable does not matter} \\
&= \iint_{\mathbb R^2} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\, dx\, dy && \text{Fubini for a nonnegative function} \\
&= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2}\, r\, dr\, d\theta && \text{polar coordinates, } dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta \\
&= 2\pi \cdot \Big[-\tfrac12 e^{-r^2}\Big]_0^\infty && \text{the integrand does not depend on } \theta;\ \tfrac{d}{dr}\big(-\tfrac12 e^{-r^2}\big) = r e^{-r^2} \\
&= 2\pi \cdot \tfrac12 = \pi && \text{and } I > 0, \text{ so } I = \sqrt\pi .
\end{aligned}
$$

The plane is unbounded, so the second and third lines are limits: over squares $[-R, R]^2$ in Cartesian coordinates and over discs of radius $R$ in polar ones. The integrand is positive, so the integral over the disc of radius $R$ is at most the one over the square $[-R, R]^2$, which is at most the one over the disc of radius $R\sqrt 2$ that contains the square, and both discs tend to the same limit. For $R = 2$ the three values are $\pi(1 - e^{-4}) = 3.0841$, $\big(\int_{-2}^{2} e^{-x^2} dx\big)^2 = 3.1123$ and $\pi(1 - e^{-8}) = 3.1405$; for $R = 3$ they are $3.1412$, $3.1415$ and $3.1416$.

**The normal density.** The substitution $x = u/\sqrt 2$, $dx = du/\sqrt 2$, gives $\int e^{-u^2/2}\, du = \sqrt{2\pi}$, which is why the [standard normal density](/notes/probability/continuous-distributions#normal) carries the factor $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$. In $n$ dimensions the next section gives $\int e^{-\frac12 x^\top \Sigma^{-1}x}\, dx = (2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}$.

## The change-of-variables formula

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Near each point a differentiable map is its Jacobian, a linear map, and a linear map multiplies every volume by the absolute value of its determinant. Changing variables in an integral therefore multiplies the integrand by $\lvert\det J\rvert$, the local volume factor; polar coordinates are the case $\lvert\det J\rvert = r$. A density transforms the opposite way, because probability, not volume, is conserved.</aside>

Let $T: U \to V$ be a bijection between open sets of $\mathbb R^n$ that is continuously differentiable with a continuously differentiable inverse. The idea: cut $U$ into small cubes; $T$ maps each one to a small curved region whose volume is $\lvert\det J_T\rvert$ times the cube's, as the [Jacobian of a small square](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians#the-jacobian-of-a-vector-function) showed, and the images tile $V$. For $f$ integrable on $V$:

$$
\boxed{\; \int_V f(y)\, dy = \int_U f\big(T(x)\big)\, \big\lvert\det J_T(x)\big\rvert\, dx \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_V f(y)\, dy &= \sum_k \int_{T(C_k)} f(y)\, dy && \text{the images } T(C_k) \text{ of cubes } C_k \text{ tile } V \\
&\approx \sum_k f\big(T(x_k)\big)\, \operatorname{vol}\big(T(C_k)\big) && f \text{ is nearly constant on a small region} \\
&\approx \sum_k f\big(T(x_k)\big)\, \big\lvert\det J_T(x_k)\big\rvert\, \operatorname{vol}(C_k) && T \approx \text{its Jacobian on } C_k;\ \text{determinant as volume} \\
&\to \int_U f\big(T(x)\big)\, \big\lvert\det J_T(x)\big\rvert\, dx && \text{a Riemann sum over the cubes } C_k .
\end{aligned}
$$

The third line combines the two facts behind the formula: on a small cube, $T$ is close to the affine map $x_k + J_T(x_k)(x - x_k)$, and an affine map multiplies volumes by $\lvert\det J_T(x_k)\rvert$, as in the [determinants note](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#the-determinant-how-a-map-scales-volume). A rigorous proof bounds the errors of both approximations uniformly over the cubes; the argument is longer but adds no new idea. In one variable the formula is the substitution rule $\int f(y)\, dy = \int f(g(x))\, \lvert g'(x)\rvert\, dx$, where the absolute value appears because an interval run backwards still has a positive length.

**Worked example: an ellipse.** The ellipse $x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 \le 1$ is the image of the unit disc under $T(u, v) = (au, bv)$, whose Jacobian is $\operatorname{diag}(a, b)$ with determinant $ab$. Its area is $\int_{\text{disc}} ab\; du\, dv = \pi ab$; for $a = 3$ and $b = 2$, $6\pi = 18.850$.

**Worked example: the Gaussian normalizer.** For a symmetric positive definite $\Sigma = LL^\top$ (its Cholesky factorization), substitute $x = Lz$. Then $x^\top \Sigma^{-1}x = z^\top L^\top (LL^\top)^{-1} Lz = z^\top z$ and $\lvert\det L\rvert = \sqrt{\det\Sigma}$, so the integral becomes a standard one:

$$
\int_{\mathbb R^n} e^{-\frac12 x^\top \Sigma^{-1}x}\, dx = \sqrt{\det\Sigma} \int_{\mathbb R^n} e^{-\frac12 z^\top z}\, dz = \sqrt{\det\Sigma}\, \big(\sqrt{2\pi}\big)^n .
$$

The last step is Fubini: the integrand is a product of $n$ one-variable Gaussians, each contributing $\sqrt{2\pi}$. For $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\det\Sigma = 0.56$ and the integral is $2\pi\sqrt{0.56} = 4.702$, which numerical integration confirms. This is the normalizing constant of the [multivariate Gaussian density](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#the-density).

## Densities under a transform

If a random vector $X$ has density $p_X$ and $T$ is a change of variables as above, the density of $Y = T(X)$ follows from the formula applied to the inverse map. The idea: the probability of a set $B$ of values of $Y$ is the probability of its preimage, an integral over $x$, and changing variables to $y$ brings in the volume factor of $T^{-1}$:

$$
\boxed{\; p_Y(y) = p_X\big(T^{-1}(y)\big)\, \big\lvert\det J_{T^{-1}}(y)\big\rvert = \frac{p_X(x)}{\lvert\det J_T(x)\rvert}, \qquad x = T^{-1}(y) \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(Y \in B) &= P\big(X \in T^{-1}(B)\big) && Y = T(X) \text{ and } T \text{ is a bijection} \\
&= \int_{T^{-1}(B)} p_X(x)\, dx && \text{the definition of a density} \\
&= \int_B p_X\big(T^{-1}(y)\big)\, \big\lvert\det J_{T^{-1}}(y)\big\rvert\, dy && \text{change of variables with the map } T^{-1} \\
J_{T^{-1}}(y) &= J_T(x)^{-1} && \text{the chain rule applied to } T^{-1}(T(x)) = x .
\end{aligned}
$$

The third line holds for every set $B$, so its integrand is the density of $Y$; the last line, with $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A$, gives the second form. Where $T$ stretches volumes, $\lvert\det J_T\rvert > 1$, the same probability is spread over more volume and the density falls; where it compresses, the density rises. The integral transforms with $\lvert\det J\rvert$ and the density with its inverse because probability, the product of the two, is what is conserved.

**Worked example: two normals in polar coordinates.** Let $X_1, X_2$ be independent standard normals, with joint density $\frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(x_1^2 + x_2^2)/2}$, and let $(R, \Theta)$ be the polar coordinates of $(X_1, X_2)$. Here the map from $(r, \theta)$ to $(x_1, x_2)$ has $\lvert\det J\rvert = r$, so the density of $(R, \Theta)$ is the joint density times $r$:

$$
p_{R,\Theta}(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-r^2/2}\, r = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\pi}}_{\Theta \text{ uniform on } [0, 2\pi)} \cdot \underbrace{r e^{-r^2/2}}_{\text{density of } R} .
$$

The density factorizes, so the angle and the radius are independent; the angle is uniform; and $P(R \le 1) = \int_0^1 r e^{-r^2/2}\, dr = 1 - e^{-1/2} = 0.3935$, the share of a two-dimensional Gaussian inside its "one sigma" circle quoted in the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#covariance-as-geometry). Read backwards, the same computation is the Box–Muller method that [produces normal samples](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#producing-samples) from uniform ones. In one dimension the formula is the [change-of-variables formula of the probability course](/notes/probability/functions-of-random-variables#the-change-of-variables-formula); stacked over a chain of learned invertible maps, it is the exact likelihood of a [normalizing flow](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows#change-of-variables), whose architectures are designed so that $\det J$ is cheap to compute.

## Monte Carlo integration

Fubini reduces an $n$-dimensional integral to $n$ one-dimensional ones, but a numerical rule with $k$ points per axis still needs $k^n$ evaluations, and its error with $N = k^n$ points shrinks only like $N^{-2/n}$ for the midpoint rule. **Monte Carlo integration** writes the integral as an expectation, $\int_D f(x)\, dx = \operatorname{vol}(D)\, E[f(U)]$ with $U$ uniform on $D$, and averages $f$ over $N$ random points. By the central limit theorem its error is about $\sigma/\sqrt N$, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of $\operatorname{vol}(D) f(U)$, whatever the dimension ([plain Monte Carlo and its error](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#plain-monte-carlo-and-its-error)).

**Worked example.** The disc has area $\pi$, and the share of the square $[-1, 1]^2$ inside it is $\pi/4$. With one fixed random generator, 4 times the share of hits is $3.36 \pm 0.15$ for $N = 100$ points, $3.111 \pm 0.017$ for $N = 10^4$ and $3.1403 \pm 0.0016$ for $N = 10^6$, each $\pm$ one standard error: a hundred times more points, ten times smaller error. In ten dimensions the unit ball has volume $\pi^5/120 = 2.550$, but it fills only $0.25\%$ of the cube $[-1, 1]^{10}$. A million points give $2.547 \pm 0.051$, a relative error of $2\%$ against $0.05\%$ for the disc with the same $N$: the rate is still $1/\sqrt N$, but $\sigma$ is large relative to the answer because hits are rare. Rare regions are what [importance sampling](/notes/probability/monte-carlo#importance-sampling) addresses.

## What changed

| | Riemann sums (baseline) | Fubini | Change of variables | Monte Carlo |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it does | defines the integral | reduces it to one-variable integrals | moves it to coordinates that fit the region or the integrand | estimates it from random points |
| Key formula | $\lim \sum f\, \Delta x\, \Delta y$ | $\iint f\, dA = \int\!\big(\int f\, dy\big) dx$ | $\int_V f = \int_U (f \circ T)\, \lvert\det J_T\rvert$ | $\operatorname{vol}(D) \cdot \frac1N \sum f(U_i)$ |
| Condition | $f$ bounded and nice enough | $\iint \lvert f\rvert < \infty$ or $f \ge 0$ | $T$ a differentiable bijection with differentiable inverse | finite variance |
| Cost in $n$ dimensions | $k^n$ cells | $n$ nested one-variable integrals | one Jacobian determinant per point | $N$ points, error $\propto 1/\sqrt N$ |
| In machine learning | — | marginals, expectations | Gaussian normalizers, densities of transformed variables, flows | expectations in high dimension, sampling-based training |

## Limitations

- **Closed forms are the exception.** The Gaussian integral works because of a symmetry; most integrals of machine learning, such as the evidence of a Bayesian model or the normalizer of an energy-based model, have no closed form and are approximated by sampling or by variational bounds.
- **Fubini needs absolute integrability.** Without it the two orders can disagree, as the counterexample shows.
- **The change of variables needs a bijection.** For a map that is many-to-one, such as $x \mapsto x^2$, the contributions of all preimages must be added; for a map into a lower dimension, such as the generator of a GAN, the image has zero volume and there is no density in the ordinary sense.
- **Determinants cost $O(n^3)$.** Computing $\det J_T$ for a general map of $n$ variables is expensive, which is why normalizing flows restrict themselves to maps with triangular or otherwise structured Jacobians.
- **Monte Carlo is slow.** Its $1/\sqrt N$ rate needs a hundred times more samples for each extra digit, and its constant can be huge when the integrand is concentrated in a small region.

## What you should remember

- A double integral can be computed as two single integrals, in either order, when $\iint \lvert f\rvert < \infty$ (Fubini); for nonrectangular regions the inner limits depend on the outer variable.
- In polar coordinates $dx\, dy = r\, dr\, d\theta$; the factor $r$ is $\det J$ of the polar map.
- $\int e^{-x^2} dx = \sqrt\pi$, by squaring and switching to polar coordinates; hence the $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ of the normal density and $(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}$ in $n$ dimensions.
- Change of variables: $\int_V f = \int_U (f \circ T)\, \lvert\det J_T\rvert$, because $\lvert\det J_T\rvert$ is the local volume factor.
- Densities transform with the inverse factor: $p_Y(y) = p_X(x)/\lvert\det J_T(x)\rvert$, the formula behind normalizing flows.
- Monte Carlo error is $\sigma/\sqrt N$ in any dimension; rare regions make $\sigma$ large.

## Code

Fubini in both orders and its counterexample, the polar Jacobian and one polar cell, the Gaussian integral squeezed between discs and squares, the Gaussian normalizer and the ellipse by numerical integration, the polar coordinates of two normals, and Monte Carlo estimates of $\pi$ and of the volume of the ten-dimensional ball. Random numbers come from fixed seeds; exact digits may vary across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate, special

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Fubini: the same integral in both orders, on a rectangle and on a triangle
f = lambda x, y: x ** 2 + x * y
print(round(integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: f(x, y), 0, 1, 0, 2)[0], 6),    # dy inside, x in [0, 1]
      round(integrate.dblquad(lambda x, y: f(x, y), 0, 2, 0, 1)[0], 6))    # dx inside, y in [0, 2]
print(round(integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: x * y, 0, 1, 0, lambda x: x)[0], 6),
      round(integrate.dblquad(lambda x, y: x * y, 0, 1, lambda y: y, 1)[0], 6))
# 1.666667 1.666667 | 0.125 0.125

# where Fubini fails: (x^2 - y^2) / (x^2 + y^2)^2 on the unit square, not absolutely integrable
g = lambda x, y: (x * x - y * y) / (x * x + y * y) ** 2
dy_first = integrate.quad(lambda x: integrate.quad(lambda y: g(x, y), 0, 1)[0], 0, 1, limit=200)[0]
dx_first = integrate.quad(lambda y: integrate.quad(lambda x: g(x, y), 0, 1)[0], 0, 1, limit=200)[0]
print(round(dy_first, 6), round(dx_first, 6), round(np.pi / 4, 6))   # 0.785398 -0.785398 0.785398

# the polar Jacobian, and the area of one polar cell
r, th = 1.5, np.radians(30)
J = np.array([[np.cos(th), -r * np.sin(th)], [np.sin(th), r * np.cos(th)]])
dr, dth = 0.25, np.radians(20)
print(round(np.linalg.det(J), 6), round(0.5 * ((r + dr / 2) ** 2 - (r - dr / 2) ** 2) * dth, 4), round(r * dr * dth, 4))
# 1.5 0.1309 0.1309

# the Gaussian integral: discs and squares squeeze the limit to pi
for R in (1, 2, 3):
    print(R, round(np.pi * (1 - np.exp(-R * R)), 4), round(np.pi * special.erf(R) ** 2, 4), round(np.pi * (1 - np.exp(-2 * R * R)), 4))
# 1 1.9859 2.231 2.7164 | 2 3.0841 3.1123 3.1405 | 3 3.1412 3.1415 3.1416
print(round(integrate.quad(lambda x: np.exp(-x * x), -np.inf, np.inf)[0] ** 2, 10), round(np.pi, 10))   # 3.1415926536 3.1415926536

# the Gaussian normalizer in two dimensions: (2 pi) sqrt(det Sigma), by the substitution x = L z
Sig = np.array([[2, 1.2], [1.2, 1]]); P = np.linalg.inv(Sig)
num = integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: np.exp(-0.5 * np.array([x, y]) @ P @ np.array([x, y])), -12, 12, -9, 9)[0]
print(round(num, 4), round(2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(np.linalg.det(Sig)), 4))   # 4.7019 4.7019

# an ellipse: area pi a b through the map (u, v) -> (a u, b v)
a_, b_ = 3, 2
ell = integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: 1.0, -a_, a_, lambda x: -b_ * np.sqrt(1 - (x / a_) ** 2), lambda x: b_ * np.sqrt(1 - (x / a_) ** 2))[0]
print(round(ell, 4), round(np.pi * a_ * b_, 4))   # 18.8496 18.8496

# densities under a transform: two standard normals in polar coordinates
Z = rng.normal(size=(1_000_000, 2)); Rr = np.hypot(Z[:, 0], Z[:, 1]); Th = np.arctan2(Z[:, 1], Z[:, 0])
print(round((Rr <= 1).mean(), 4), round(1 - np.exp(-0.5), 4), round((Rr ** 2).mean(), 3), round(np.corrcoef(Rr, Th)[0, 1], 4))
# 0.3934 0.3935 1.999 -0.0011: P(R <= 1) and its formula, E[R^2] = 2, no correlation between radius and angle

# Monte Carlo: pi as 4 times the share of the square [-1, 1]^2 inside the unit disc (its own generator)
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for n in (100, 10_000, 1_000_000):
    hit = ((rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=(n, 2)) ** 2).sum(1) <= 1)
    print(n, round(4 * hit.mean(), 4), round(4 * hit.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n), 4))
# 100 3.36 0.1474 | 10000 3.1108 0.0166 | 1000000 3.1403 0.0016
# the volume of the unit ball in ten dimensions, pi^5 / 120, from the cube [-1, 1]^10
hit = ((rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=(1_000_000, 10)) ** 2).sum(1) <= 1)
print(round(np.pi ** 5 / 120, 4), hit.sum(), round(1024 * hit.mean(), 3), round(1024 * hit.std(ddof=1) / 1000, 3))
# 2.5502 2487 2.547 0.051: 2487 hits in a million points
```

A zero gradient is only a candidate for a minimum and the Hessian test is only local; the next note identifies the class of functions, the convex ones, for which every local minimum is global, and gives the tests for convexity that the gradient and the Hessian make possible.
