The [previous note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) closed the linear-algebra section: every matrix, read through its SVD, rotates, stretches along orthogonal axes and rotates again. Linear maps are the functions we understand completely. The functions of machine learning are not linear: a loss is built from exponentials, logarithms and products of weights. Calculus is the way to use linear algebra on them anyway, because near any point a smooth function is almost linear, and the derivative is that linear map. This note builds the idea in one variable: the derivative and its rules, the Taylor polynomials that add curvature and higher terms with a controlled error, what the first two terms tell an optimizer, and how a computer approximates a derivative when no formula is at hand. The [next note](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians) does the same for functions of many variables.

**Notation.** $f: \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is a function of one variable, $f'$, $f''$ and $f^{(k)}$ are its first, second and $k$-th derivatives, and $h$ is a small increment of the input. A quantity is $o(h)$ if it divided by $h$ tends to 0 as $h \to 0$, and $O(h^k)$ if it is at most a constant times $\lvert h\rvert^k$ for small $h$. $u = 2^{-53} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-16}$ is the **unit roundoff** of double precision: every correctly rounded operation has a relative error of at most $u$ ([floating point](/notes/acos/floating-point#rounding-ulp-and-machine-epsilon)).

## Baseline: the slope of a secant

The slope of the line through the points $(x, f(x))$ and $(x + h, f(x + h))$ is the **difference quotient**:

$$
\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} .
$$

For $f(x) = x^2$ at $x = 1$ it is $\big((1 + h)^2 - 1\big)/h = 2 + h$: $2.1$ for $h = 0.1$ and $2.01$ for $h = 0.01$. The **derivative** is its limit as $h \to 0$, here $f'(1) = 2$. The quotient is what a computer can evaluate, and its error, $h$ in this example, is the subject of the last section. As a definition, though, the limit hides what the derivative is for. It is for replacing a curve by a line.

## The derivative as the best linear approximation

The idea: $f$ is differentiable at $x$ when, near $x$, it equals a linear function of the increment plus an error that is small *compared with the increment*. The slope of that linear function is $f'(x)$:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) &= f(x) + f'(x)\, h + r(h), \\
\frac{r(h)}{h} &\to 0 \text{ as } h \to 0
\end{aligned}
}
$$

This is the limit definition rewritten. Solve the boxed equation for the error and divide by $h$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{r(h)}{h} &= \frac{f(x + h) - f(x) - f'(x)\, h}{h} && \text{the definition of } r \\
&= \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} - f'(x) && \text{split the fraction} \\
&\to 0 && \text{exactly when the difference quotient tends to } f'(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The tangent is the *best* line through $(x, f(x))$. Any other slope $m$ leaves the error $f(x + h) - f(x) - mh = (f'(x) - m)\, h + r(h)$, which divided by $h$ tends to $f'(x) - m \ne 0$. Another line misses by an amount proportional to $h$; the tangent misses by less than any multiple of $h$.

**Worked example.** $f(x) = \sqrt x$ at $x = 4$ has $f'(4) = 1/(2\sqrt 4) = 0.25$. The tangent predicts $\sqrt{4.1} \approx 2 + 0.25 \cdot 0.1 = 2.025$, while $\sqrt{4.1} = 2.024846$. The error is $1.54 \cdot 10^{-4}$, about a thousandth of the step. Halving the step to $h = 0.05$ gives the prediction $2.0125$ against $2.012461$, an error of $3.88 \cdot 10^{-5}$: a quarter of the previous one. The error falls like $h^2$, and the next sections explain why: it is $\tfrac12 f''(4)\, h^2 = -h^2/64$ to leading order, $-1.56 \cdot 10^{-4}$ and $-3.91 \cdot 10^{-5}$.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> A differentiable function is locally a linear map plus an error of smaller order. In one variable the linear map is multiplication by the number $f'(x)$; in many variables it becomes a vector, the gradient, or a matrix, the Jacobian. Every rule of differential calculus, and every gradient-based method of machine learning, works by replacing a function with this linear map and controlling what is left over.</aside>

## The rules, and the chain rule derived

The derivatives of $x^n$, $e^x$, $\log x$, $\sin x$ are assumed known. Three rules build everything else from them:

- **Linearity:** $(af + bg)' = af' + bg'$ for constants $a$, $b$, because the linear parts of $f$ and $g$ add.
- **Product:** $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$. Multiply the two approximations: $(f + f'h + r_f)(g + g'h + r_g) = fg + (f'g + fg')\, h + \dots$, where every remaining term is $o(h)$.
- **Chain rule**, for a composition $g(f(x))$, derived below. The quotient rule follows from the product rule and the chain rule applied to $1/g$.

The idea of the chain rule: compose the two linear approximations. A step $h$ in $x$ moves $f(x)$ by about $f'(x)\, h$, and that step moves $g$ by about $g'(f(x))$ times it:

$$
\boxed{\; \big(g \circ f\big)'(x) = g'\big(f(x)\big)\, f'(x) \;}
$$

To prove it, write $y = f(x)$ and $k = f(x + h) - f(x)$, the step that $h$ causes in the input of $g$. Differentiability of $g$ at $y$ is written as $g(y + k) = g(y) + g'(y)\, k + \varepsilon(k)\, k$ with $\varepsilon(k) \to 0$ as $k \to 0$ and $\varepsilon(0) = 0$; this form allows $k = 0$, which can happen for $h \ne 0$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{g(f(x + h)) - g(f(x))}{h} &= \frac{g(y + k) - g(y)}{h} && \text{the definitions of } y \text{ and } k \\
&= \big(g'(y) + \varepsilon(k)\big)\, \frac{k}{h} && \text{differentiability of } g \text{ at } y \\
&= \big(g'(y) + \varepsilon(k)\big)\Big(f'(x) + \frac{r_f(h)}{h}\Big) && \text{differentiability of } f \text{ at } x \\
&\to g'(y)\, f'(x) && \text{as } h \to 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line uses that $k \to 0$ as $h \to 0$, because a differentiable $f$ is continuous, so $\varepsilon(k) \to 0$; and that $r_f(h)/h \to 0$.

**Worked example: the sigmoid.** $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ is $g(f(x))$ with the inner function $f(x) = 1 + e^{-x}$ and the outer $g(v) = 1/v$. Their derivatives are $f'(x) = -e^{-x}$ and $g'(v) = -1/v^2$, so

$$
\sigma'(x) = -\frac{1}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} \cdot \big(-e^{-x}\big) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = \sigma(x)\big(1 - \sigma(x)\big) .
$$

At $x = 1$, $\sigma(1) = 0.7311$ and $\sigma'(1) = 0.7311 \cdot 0.2689 = 0.1966$; a central difference with $h = 10^{-5}$ gives the same six digits (Code section). Backpropagation is this rule applied once per node of a computation, from the loss backwards, and the [one-unit example of the backpropagation note](/notes/ml/backpropagation#the-computational-graph) multiplies exactly this local derivative.

## Taylor polynomials and the remainder

The tangent matches the value and the slope of $f$ at a point $a$. The idea of Taylor's theorem: match also the second, third, …, $n$-th derivatives, and the polynomial hugs $f$ more closely, with an error controlled by the next derivative. The **Taylor polynomial** of order $n$ at $a$ is

$$
T_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\, (x - a)^k = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2}(x - a)^2 + \dots + \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n .
$$

It is the only polynomial of degree at most $n$ whose first $n$ derivatives at $a$ equal those of $f$. The $k$-th derivative of $(x - a)^j/j!$ at $x = a$ is 1 when $j = k$ and 0 otherwise, so each coefficient is forced. $T_1$ is the tangent line.

**Taylor's theorem.** If $f$ has $n + 1$ continuous derivatives on an interval that contains $a$ and $x$, then

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= T_n(x) + R_n(x), \\
R_n(x) &= \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n + 1)!}\, (x - a)^{n+1} \ \text{ for some } \xi \text{ between } a \text{ and } x
\end{aligned}
}
$$

The proof goes by induction on $n$, through an exact integral formula for the remainder. Assume that $f(x) = T_{n-1}(x) + \int_a^x f^{(n)}(t)\, \frac{(x - t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\, dt$ holds for $n - 1$; for $n = 1$ this is the fundamental theorem of calculus, $f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^x f'(t)\, dt$. Integrate by parts with $f^{(n)}(t)$ as the factor to differentiate and $\frac{(x - t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}$ as the factor to integrate, whose antiderivative in $t$ is $-\frac{(x - t)^n}{n!}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= T_{n-1}(x) + \int_a^x f^{(n)}(t)\, \frac{(x - t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\, dt && \text{the claim for } n - 1 \\
&= T_{n-1}(x) + \Big[-f^{(n)}(t)\, \frac{(x - t)^n}{n!}\Big]_{t=a}^{t=x} + \int_a^x f^{(n+1)}(t)\, \frac{(x - t)^n}{n!}\, dt && \text{integration by parts} \\
&= T_{n-1}(x) + \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n + \int_a^x f^{(n+1)}(t)\, \frac{(x - t)^n}{n!}\, dt && \text{the bracket vanishes at } t = x \\
&= T_n(x) + \int_a^x f^{(n+1)}(t)\, \frac{(x - t)^n}{n!}\, dt && \text{the definition of } T_n \\
&= T_n(x) + f^{(n+1)}(\xi) \int_a^x \frac{(x - t)^n}{n!}\, dt && \text{mean value theorem for integrals} \\
&= T_n(x) + f^{(n+1)}(\xi)\, \frac{(x - a)^{n+1}}{(n + 1)!} && \text{integrate the power.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The fourth line is the integral formula for $n$, which completes the induction. The fifth uses that $(x - t)^n$ keeps one sign for $t$ between $a$ and $x$, so the integral of $f^{(n+1)}$ against it equals the integral of the weight alone times a value of the continuous $f^{(n+1)}$ somewhere on the interval. As $x \to a$ the remainder is $O\big((x - a)^{n+1}\big)$: each extra order buys one more power of the distance.

**Worked example: $e^x$.** All derivatives of $e^x$ are $e^x$, so at $a = 0$ the polynomial is $T_n(x) = 1 + x + x^2/2 + \dots + x^n/n!$ and the remainder at $x = 1$ is $e^\xi/(n + 1)!$ with $0 < \xi < 1$. The theorem therefore traps the error between $1/(n + 1)!$ and $e/(n + 1)!$:

| $n$ | $T_n(1)$ | error $e - T_n(1)$ | $1/(n+1)!$ | $e/(n+1)!$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $2$ | $0.718$ | $0.500$ | $1.359$ |
| 2 | $2.5$ | $0.218$ | $0.167$ | $0.453$ |
| 3 | $2.6667$ | $0.0516$ | $0.0417$ | $0.113$ |
| 4 | $2.70833$ | $0.00995$ | $0.00833$ | $0.0227$ |

The factorial grows faster than any power, so for $e^x$ the remainder goes to 0 at every $x$ and the **Taylor series** $\sum_k x^k/k!$ converges to $e^x$ everywhere.

**Worked example: $\log(1 + x)$.** The $k$-th derivative is $(-1)^{k-1}(k - 1)!/(1 + x)^k$, so at $a = 0$ the coefficients are $(-1)^{k-1}/k$ and $T_n(x) = x - x^2/2 + x^3/3 - \dots$. For $x \ge 0$ the remainder is at most $x^{n+1}/(n + 1)$ in absolute value, because $(1 + \xi)^{n+1} \ge 1$. At $x = 0.1$, where $\log 1.1 = 0.0953102$, the errors of $T_1, \dots, T_4$ are $-4.69 \cdot 10^{-3}$, $3.10 \cdot 10^{-4}$, $-2.32 \cdot 10^{-5}$ and $1.85 \cdot 10^{-6}$, against bounds $5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}$, $3.3 \cdot 10^{-4}$, $2.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ and $2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}$: each order gains about one digit, a factor $x = 0.1$. At $x = 1.8$ the same polynomials give $1.8$, $0.18$, $2.124$ and $-0.500$, swinging further from $\log 2.8 = 1.030$ at every order. The terms $x^k/k$ grow when $x > 1$, the series diverges there, and the bound $x^{n+1}/(n + 1)$ grows with them. A Taylor polynomial is a local statement.

Taylor polynomials are how libraries evaluate functions near a point where the direct formula loses digits. For $x = 10^{-10}$, computing $1 + x$ first keeps only about seven significant digits of $x$, because the spacing of doubles near 1 is $2.2 \cdot 10^{-16}$, and $\log(1 + x)$ comes out with a relative error of $8 \cdot 10^{-8}$. Near 0 the series says $\log(1 + x) = x - x^2/2 + \dots$, and the library function `log1p` computes it to full precision; `expm1` does the same for $e^x - 1$. In machine learning, the second-order Taylor expansion of a log-density around its maximum is the [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation#the-laplace-approximation), and the expansion of a loss around the current weights is the starting point of every optimizer.

## Why first and second order matter for optimization

The first-order term says in which direction $f$ decreases and how fast; the second-order term says for how long the decrease lasts. Take a gradient step $x \to x - \eta f'(x)$ with step size $\eta > 0$ on a function whose second derivative is bounded, $\lvert f'' \rvert \le L$. The idea: Taylor's theorem with $n = 1$ gives the new value exactly, up to one unknown point $\xi$, and the bound on $f''$ turns it into a guaranteed decrease:

$$
\boxed{\; f\big(x - \eta f'(x)\big) \le f(x) - \eta\Big(1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}\Big) f'(x)^2 \;}
$$

Expand the new value with the step $h = -\eta f'(x)$ and bound the one unknown factor:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f\big(x - \eta f'(x)\big) &= f(x) + f'(x)\big(-\eta f'(x)\big) + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\big(\eta f'(x)\big)^2 && \text{Taylor with } n = 1,\ h = -\eta f'(x) \\
&= f(x) - \eta f'(x)^2 + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\, \eta^2 f'(x)^2 && \text{collect the terms} \\
&\le f(x) - \eta f'(x)^2 + \tfrac12 L \eta^2 f'(x)^2 && f''(\xi) \le L \\
&= f(x) - \eta\Big(1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}\Big) f'(x)^2 && \text{factor out } \eta f'(x)^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $0 < \eta < 2/L$ the bracket is positive and every step with $f'(x) \ne 0$ lowers $f$. The same bound, with $L$ the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian, gives the step-size limit of [gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case).

The second-order term also gives a better step. Minimizing the quadratic model $f(x) + f'(x)h + \tfrac12 f''(x) h^2$ over $h$, when $f''(x) > 0$, gives $f'(x) + f''(x) h = 0$ and **Newton's step** $h = -f'(x)/f''(x)$: the gradient step with the step size set by the curvature. At a **critical point**, where $f'(x^*) = 0$, Taylor with $n = 1$ reads $f(x^* + h) - f(x^*) = \tfrac12 f''(\xi) h^2$. If $f''(x^*) > 0$, then $f'' > 0$ near $x^*$ by continuity and $x^*$ is a strict local minimum; if $f''(x^*) < 0$, a strict local maximum. If $f''(x^*) = 0$ the test says nothing: $x^3$, $x^4$ and $-x^4$ all have $f' = f'' = 0$ at 0, and the first has no extremum there, the second a minimum and the third a maximum.

**Worked example.** $f(x) = e^x - 2x$ has $f'(x) = e^x - 2$ and $f''(x) = e^x$, so its minimum is at $x^* = \log 2 = 0.6931$ with $f''(x^*) = 2 > 0$. From $x_0 = 0$:

| step | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| gradient descent, $\eta = 0.2$: error $x_k - x^*$ | $-0.693$ | $-0.493$ | $-0.337$ | $-0.223$ | $-0.143$ |
| Newton: error $x_k - x^*$ | $-0.693$ | $0.307$ | $0.0426$ | $9.0 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $4.0 \cdot 10^{-7}$ |

Near the minimum, gradient descent multiplies the error by about $1 - \eta f''(x^*) = 0.6$ per step, as the first-order term predicts. Newton's method roughly squares it, doubling the number of correct digits per step, because it uses the curvature that gradient descent ignores. The [unconstrained optimization note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization) proves both rates; [logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs) is fitted by the many-variable Newton step.

## Finite differences and their error

When $f$ is available only as a program, its derivative can be approximated by a difference quotient with a small fixed $h$. Taylor's theorem gives the error of the formula, and floating point adds a second error that *grows* as $h$ shrinks. The best $h$ balances the two.

**Truncation error.** The **forward difference** $D_+ f(x) = \big(f(x + h) - f(x)\big)/h$ follows from Taylor with $n = 1$, and the **central difference** $D_0 f(x) = \big(f(x + h) - f(x - h)\big)/(2h)$ from Taylor with $n = 2$ at $x + h$ and $x - h$. In the central difference the even terms cancel:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x \pm h) &= f(x) \pm f'(x)\, h + \tfrac12 f''(x)\, h^2 \pm \tfrac16 f'''(\xi_\pm)\, h^3 && \text{Taylor with } n = 2 \\
f(x + h) - f(x - h) &= 2 f'(x)\, h + \tfrac16 \big(f'''(\xi_+) + f'''(\xi_-)\big)\, h^3 && \text{subtract: } f(x) \text{ and } h^2 \text{ terms cancel} \\
&= 2 f'(x)\, h + \tfrac13 f'''(\xi)\, h^3 && \text{intermediate value theorem} \\
D_0 f(x) &= f'(x) + \tfrac16 f'''(\xi)\, h^2 && \text{divide by } 2h .
\end{aligned}
$$

The third line uses that the average of $f'''$ at two points is a value of the continuous $f'''$ somewhere between them. The same steps with $n = 1$ give $D_+ f(x) = f'(x) + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\, h$. The forward difference is first-order accurate, the central one second-order.

**Rounding error.** The computed values of $f$ carry a relative error of about $u$ each, so the numerator is wrong by up to about $2u\lvert f(x)\rvert$, and dividing by a small $h$ magnifies it. Subtracting two nearly equal numbers keeps their absolute error but loses most of their significant digits; it is the standard example of an ill-conditioned computation in the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability). With $M_k$ a bound on $\lvert f^{(k)} \rvert$ near $x$, the total errors are at most

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_+(h) &= \frac{M_2}{2}\, h + \frac{2u\lvert f(x)\rvert}{h}, \\
E_0(h) &= \frac{M_3}{6}\, h^2 + \frac{u\lvert f(x)\rvert}{h} .
\end{aligned}
$$

**The optimal step.** The idea: one term grows with $h$ and the other falls, so the sum has a minimum where the derivative in $h$ vanishes:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
h_+^* &= 2\sqrt{\frac{u\lvert f\rvert}{M_2}}, \quad E_+^* = 2\sqrt{u\lvert f\rvert M_2}; \\
h_0^* &= \Big(\frac{3u\lvert f\rvert}{M_3}\Big)^{1/3}, \quad E_0^* = \frac{M_3}{2}\, \big(h_0^*\big)^2
\end{aligned}
}
$$

For the forward difference write $E_+ = \alpha h + \beta/h$ with $\alpha = M_2/2$ and $\beta = 2u\lvert f\rvert$; for the central one $E_0 = \gamma h^2 + \beta'/h$ with $\gamma = M_3/6$ and $\beta' = u\lvert f\rvert$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_+'(h) &= \alpha - \frac{\beta}{h^2} = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ h_+^* = \sqrt{\beta/\alpha} = 2\sqrt{u\lvert f\rvert/M_2} && \text{set the derivative to zero} \\
E_+^* &= \alpha\sqrt{\beta/\alpha} + \beta\sqrt{\alpha/\beta} = 2\sqrt{\alpha\beta} = 2\sqrt{u\lvert f\rvert M_2} && \text{substitute } h_+^* \\
E_0'(h) &= 2\gamma h - \frac{\beta'}{h^2} = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \big(h_0^*\big)^3 = \frac{\beta'}{2\gamma} = \frac{3u\lvert f\rvert}{M_3} && \text{set the derivative to zero} \\
E_0^* &= \gamma\big(h_0^*\big)^2 + \frac{\beta'}{h_0^*} = \gamma\big(h_0^*\big)^2 + 2\gamma\big(h_0^*\big)^2 = \frac{M_3}{2}\big(h_0^*\big)^2 && \beta' = 2\gamma \big(h_0^*\big)^3 .
\end{aligned}
$$

When $f$ and its derivatives are of order 1, the forward difference is best at $h \approx \sqrt u \approx 10^{-8}$ and keeps about half of the sixteen digits; the central difference is best at $h \approx u^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$ and keeps about two thirds ([Nocedal and Wright, 2006](#references), Section 8.1).

**Worked example.** $f(x) = e^x$ at $x = 1$, where $f = M_2 = M_3 = e$. The forward difference has $h_+^* = 2\sqrt u = 2.1 \cdot 10^{-8}$ and a best error bound of $2e\sqrt u = 5.7 \cdot 10^{-8}$. The central difference has $h_0^* = (3u)^{1/3} = 6.9 \cdot 10^{-6}$ and a bound of $\tfrac e2 (3u)^{2/3} = 6.5 \cdot 10^{-11}$. Measured in double precision, the forward error is $1.4 \cdot 10^{-6}$ at $h = 10^{-6}$ and $6.6 \cdot 10^{-9}$ at $h = 10^{-8}$, and it grows again to $4.3 \cdot 10^{-4}$ at $h = 10^{-12}$. The central error is $4.5 \cdot 10^{-7}$ at $h = 10^{-3}$, $5.9 \cdot 10^{-11}$ at $h = 10^{-5}$, and $2.1 \cdot 10^{-4}$ at $h = 10^{-12}$.

The bounds are worst cases: rounding errors partly cancel, so the measured points scatter below the lines on the rounding side. Two practical details matter. The step should be exactly representable relative to $x$, which a power of two such as $2^{-17} \approx 7.6 \cdot 10^{-6}$ guarantees for $x = 1$. And a finite-difference gradient of a function of $n$ variables costs $n + 1$ or $2n$ evaluations, which is why networks are differentiated by backpropagation and checked by finite differences only on a few coordinates, as in [gradient checking](/notes/ml/backpropagation#checking-gradients-by-finite-differences).

## What changed

| | Approximates | Error for a step $h$ | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Secant slope (baseline) | the average slope over $[x, x + h]$ | $O(h)$ in the slope | the definition |
| Tangent, $T_1$ | $f$ by the best linear function | $o(h)$; $\tfrac12 f''(\xi) h^2$ if $f''$ exists | gradient steps, the descent bound |
| $T_2$ | $f$ with its curvature | $\tfrac16 f'''(\xi) h^3$ | Newton's step, the second-order test, the Laplace approximation |
| $T_n$ | $f$ matching $n$ derivatives | $f^{(n+1)}(\xi)\, h^{n+1}/(n + 1)!$ | evaluating functions (`log1p`, `expm1`) |
| Forward difference | $f'$ from two values | $\tfrac12 M_2 h + 2u\lvert f\rvert/h$, best about $\sqrt u$ | quick checks |
| Central difference | $f'$ from two values | $\tfrac16 M_3 h^2 + u\lvert f\rvert/h$, best about $u^{2/3}$ | gradient checking |

## Limitations

- **One variable.** Losses depend on millions of weights; the [next note](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians) turns the derivative into a gradient and a Jacobian.
- **Smoothness is assumed.** $\lvert x\rvert$, the ReLU and the hinge loss have no derivative at their kinks, and a finite difference across a kink returns an average of the two slopes. [Convex analysis](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions) replaces the derivative there by a set of slopes.
- **A Taylor series need not converge, or need not converge to $f$.** The series of $\log(1 + x)$ diverges for $x > 1$. The function $e^{-1/x^2}$, extended by 0 at 0, has all derivatives 0 at 0, so every $T_n$ is identically 0 while the function is not.
- **The remainder needs a bound on $f^{(n+1)}$ over the whole interval,** which is rarely known in practice; the bound tells how the error scales, not always its size.
- **Finite-difference bounds are models.** The real error depends on how $f$ is implemented, on whether $x + h$ is exact, and on cancellation between rounding errors.

## What you should remember

- $f$ is differentiable at $x$ when $f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x)h + o(h)$: the derivative is the slope of the best linear approximation.
- The chain rule $(g \circ f)' = g'(f(x))\, f'(x)$ composes linear approximations; backpropagation applies it node by node.
- Taylor: $f(x) = T_n(x) + f^{(n+1)}(\xi)(x - a)^{n+1}/(n + 1)!$. Each order adds a power of $x - a$ to the accuracy near $a$, and a series can still diverge far from $a$.
- First order gives the descent direction and, with $\lvert f''\rvert \le L$, a guaranteed decrease for steps $\eta < 2/L$; second order gives Newton's step and the test $f'' > 0$ for a minimum.
- Forward differences have error $O(h)$, central $O(h^2)$, and rounding adds $O(u/h)$. The best steps are about $\sqrt u \approx 10^{-8}$ and $u^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$ in double precision.

## Code

The worked examples: the tangent of $\sqrt x$, the chain rule for the sigmoid, the Taylor errors of $e^x$ and $\log(1 + x)$, `log1p`, gradient descent against Newton, and the finite-difference errors. Exact digits in the last places may vary across platforms and library versions.

```python
import math

# the tangent of sqrt at 4: the error falls like h^2 and matches f''(4) h^2 / 2 = -h^2 / 64
for h in (0.1, 0.05):
    print(h, f'{math.sqrt(4 + h) - (2 + h / 4):.3e}', f'{-h * h / 64:.3e}')
# 0.1 -1.543e-04 -1.563e-04 | 0.05 -3.882e-05 -3.906e-05

# the chain rule: sigma'(1) = sigma(1)(1 - sigma(1)) against a central difference
sig = lambda x: 1 / (1 + math.exp(-x))
print(round(sig(1) * (1 - sig(1)), 6), round((sig(1 + 1e-5) - sig(1 - 1e-5)) / 2e-5, 6))   # 0.196612 0.196612

# Taylor polynomials of e^x at 0, evaluated at 1: the error lies between 1/(n+1)! and e/(n+1)!
T = 1.0
for n in range(1, 5):
    T += 1 / math.factorial(n)
    print(n, round(T, 5), round(math.e - T, 5), round(1 / math.factorial(n + 1), 5), round(math.e / math.factorial(n + 1), 5))
# 1 2.0 0.71828 0.5 1.35914 | 2 2.5 0.21828 0.16667 0.45305 | 3 2.66667 0.05162 0.04167 0.11326 | 4 2.70833 0.00995 0.00833 0.02265

# log(1 + x): one digit per order at x = 0.1, divergence at x = 1.8
for x in (0.1, 1.8):
    T = 0.0
    for n in range(1, 5):
        T += (-1) ** (n + 1) * x ** n / n
        print(x, n, f'{T:.6f}', f'{math.log1p(x) - T:.2e}', f'{x ** (n + 1) / (n + 1):.1e}')
# 0.1: errors -4.69e-03 3.10e-04 -2.32e-05 1.85e-06, bounds 5.0e-03 3.3e-04 2.5e-05 2.0e-06
# 1.8: T_n = 1.800000 0.180000 2.124000 -0.500400, log(2.8) = 1.029619

# log(1 + x) for tiny x: forming 1 + x first loses digits, log1p does not
x = 1e-10
print(f'{(math.log(1 + x) - math.log1p(x)) / math.log1p(x):.1e}')   # 8.3e-08

# gradient descent (step 0.2) against Newton on f(x) = e^x - 2x, minimum at log 2
xg = xn = 0.0
for k in range(5):
    print(k, f'{xg - math.log(2):.3f}', f'{xn - math.log(2):.1e}')
    xg -= 0.2 * (math.exp(xg) - 2)
    xn -= (math.exp(xn) - 2) / math.exp(xn)
# errors: GD -0.693 -0.493 -0.337 -0.223 -0.143; Newton -6.9e-01 3.1e-01 4.3e-02 9.0e-04 4.0e-07

# finite differences of e^x at 1: predicted optimal steps and errors, then measured errors
u = 2.0 ** -53
print(f'{2 * math.sqrt(u):.1e} {2 * math.e * math.sqrt(u):.1e} {(3 * u) ** (1 / 3):.1e} {math.e / 2 * (3 * u) ** (2 / 3):.1e}')
# 2.1e-08 5.7e-08 6.9e-06 6.5e-11
for k in (3, 5, 6, 8, 12):
    h = 10.0 ** -k
    fwd = (math.exp(1 + h) - math.exp(1)) / h
    cen = (math.exp(1 + h) - math.exp(1 - h)) / (2 * h)
    print(k, f'{abs(fwd - math.e):.1e} {abs(cen - math.e):.1e}')
# 3 1.4e-03 4.5e-07 | 5 1.4e-05 5.9e-11 | 6 1.4e-06 1.6e-10 | 8 6.6e-09 6.6e-09 | 12 4.3e-04 2.1e-04
```
