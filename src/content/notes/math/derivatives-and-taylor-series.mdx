What is $\sqrt{4.1}$, without a calculator? The square root is curved, yet near 4 it is almost a line: it rises by about a quarter of each small step, so $\sqrt{4.1} \approx 2 + 0.25 \cdot 0.1 = 2.025$. The true value is $2.024846$. The guess is off by $1.5 \cdot 10^{-4}$, and halving the step makes the error four times smaller.

Every loss in machine learning is built like this function: from exponentials, logarithms and products of weights, none of them linear. Yet near any point a smooth function is almost linear, and the linear algebra of the [previous notes](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) applies to that linear part. How good is the linear guess, how do we add curvature to it, and how does a computer find the slope when it has only the function's values?

## The derivative as the best linear approximation

The slope of the line through $(x, f(x))$ and $(x + h, f(x + h))$ is the **difference quotient** $\big(f(x + h) - f(x)\big)/h$. For $f(x) = x^2$ at $x = 1$ it is $\big((1 + h)^2 - 1\big)/h = 2 + h$: $2.1$ for $h = 0.1$ and $2.01$ for $h = 0.01$. The **derivative** $f'(x)$ is its limit as $h \to 0$, here $f'(1) = 2$.

The limit hides what the derivative is for, which is replacing a curve by a line. Rewritten, the definition says that $f$ is **differentiable** at $x$ when, near $x$, it equals a linear function of the increment plus an error that is small compared with the increment:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) &= f(x) + f'(x)\, h + r(h), \\
\frac{r(h)}{h} &\to 0 \quad \text{as } h \to 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The two definitions agree, because dividing the error by $h$ gives

$$
\frac{r(h)}{h} = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} - f'(x) ,
$$

which tends to 0 exactly when the difference quotient tends to $f'(x)$. A quantity is written $o(h)$ when, divided by $h$, it tends to 0, and $O(h^k)$ when it is at most a constant times $\lvert h\rvert^k$ for small $h$. So $f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x)h + o(h)$.

The tangent is the *best* line through $(x, f(x))$. Any other slope $m$ leaves the error $(f'(x) - m)\, h + r(h)$, which divided by $h$ tends to $f'(x) - m \ne 0$. Another line misses by an amount proportional to $h$; the tangent misses by less than any multiple of $h$.

**Example.** For $f(x) = \sqrt x$ at 4, $f'(4) = 1/(2\sqrt4) = 0.25$:

| step $h$ | tangent $2 + h/4$ | $\sqrt{4 + h}$ | error | $-h^2/64$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0.1$ | $2.025$ | $2.024846$ | $-1.54 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $-1.56 \cdot 10^{-4}$ |
| $0.05$ | $2.0125$ | $2.012461$ | $-3.88 \cdot 10^{-5}$ | $-3.91 \cdot 10^{-5}$ |

The error falls like $h^2$, and to leading order it is $\tfrac12 f''(4)\,h^2 = -h^2/64$, the last column. Taylor's theorem below explains why.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> A differentiable function is locally a linear map plus an error of smaller order. In one variable the map is multiplication by $f'(x)$; in many variables it becomes the gradient or the Jacobian.</aside>

## The rules, and the chain rule derived

Four derivatives come straight from the definition. For $x^n$ the binomial expansion gives $\big((x + h)^n - x^n\big)/h = nx^{n-1} + O(h)$. The number $e$ is defined as the base for which $(e^h - 1)/h \to 1$, so $(e^{x+h} - e^x)/h = e^x(e^h - 1)/h \to e^x$. For $\sin$, the addition formula and the limits $\sin h/h \to 1$, $(\cos h - 1)/h \to 0$ give $\cos$. And $(\log x)' = 1/x$ follows by differentiating $e^{\log x} = x$ with the chain rule below ([Strang and Herman, 2016a](#references), theorems 3.3, 3.8, 3.14 and 3.15).

Three rules build the rest from them. Linearity, $(af + bg)' = af' + bg'$, holds because the linear parts of $f$ and $g$ add. The product rule, $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$, comes from multiplying the two approximations: $(f + f'h + r_f)(g + g'h + r_g) = fg + (f'g + fg')\,h + o(h)$, since every other product contains $h^2$, $r_f$ or $r_g$ and is $o(h)$. The third, for a composition, is the chain rule:

$$
\big(g \circ f\big)'(x) = g'\big(f(x)\big)\, f'(x) .
$$

It composes the two linear approximations. A step $h$ in $x$ moves $f(x)$ by about $f'(x)\,h$, and that step moves $g$ by about $g'(f(x))$ times it. To make this exact, write $y = f(x)$ and $k = f(x + h) - f(x)$, the step that $h$ causes in the input of $g$. Differentiability of $g$ at $y$ can be written as $g(y + k) - g(y) = \big(g'(y) + \varepsilon(k)\big)\,k$ with $\varepsilon(k) \to 0$ as $k \to 0$ and $\varepsilon(0) = 0$; this form allows $k = 0$, which can happen for $h \ne 0$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{g(f(x + h)) - g(f(x))}{h} &= \frac{g(y + k) - g(y)}{h} \\
&= \big(g'(y) + \varepsilon(k)\big)\, \frac{k}{h} \\
&\to g'(y)\, f'(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

In the limit $k/h \to f'(x)$ by differentiability of $f$, and $k = f'(x)h + r(h) \to 0$, so $\varepsilon(k) \to 0$. The quotient rule follows from the product rule and the chain rule applied to $1/g$.

**Example: the sigmoid.** $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ is $g(f(x))$ with the inner function $f(x) = 1 + e^{-x}$ and the outer $g(v) = 1/v$, whose derivatives are $f'(x) = -e^{-x}$ and $g'(v) = -1/v^2$. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma'(x) &= -\frac{1}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} \cdot \big(-e^{-x}\big) \\
&= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} \\
&= \sigma(x)\big(1 - \sigma(x)\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

At $x = 1$, $\sigma(1) = 0.7311$ and $\sigma'(1) = 0.7311 \cdot 0.2689 = 0.1966$; a central difference with $h = 10^{-5}$ gives the same six digits (Code section). Backpropagation applies this rule once per node of a computation, from the loss backwards, and the [one-unit example of the backpropagation note](/notes/ml/backpropagation#the-computational-graph) multiplies exactly this local derivative.

## Taylor polynomials and the remainder

The tangent matches the value and the slope of $f$ at a point $a$. Matching the second, third, up to the $n$-th derivative as well gives a polynomial that hugs $f$ more closely. The **Taylor polynomial** of order $n$ at $a$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_n(x) &= \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}\, (x - a)^k \\
&= f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) + \dots + \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x - a)^n ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where $f^{(k)}$ is the $k$-th derivative. It is the only polynomial of degree at most $n$ whose first $n$ derivatives at $a$ equal those of $f$: the $k$-th derivative of $(x - a)^j/j!$ at $x = a$ is 1 when $j = k$ and 0 otherwise, so each coefficient is forced. $T_1$ is the tangent.

How far is $T_n$ from $f$? **Taylor's theorem** answers with the next derivative. If $f$ has $n + 1$ continuous derivatives on an interval containing $a$ and $x$, then for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= T_n(x) + R_n(x), \\
R_n(x) &= \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n + 1)!}\, (x - a)^{n+1} .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the remainder in **Lagrange form**. The theorem holds under weaker assumptions too: a proof by Rolle's theorem needs only that $f^{(n+1)}$ exist ([Strang and Herman, 2016b](#references), theorem 6.7). With $n$ derivatives at $a$ alone, one still gets the **Peano form** $R_n(x) = o\big((x - a)^n\big)$, which says how fast the error vanishes but not how large it is.

<details>
<summary>Proof: the Lagrange form by Rolle's theorem, and the Peano form</summary>

Rolle's theorem: a function continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable inside, with equal values at the ends, has a zero derivative somewhere inside. Unless it is constant, its maximum or its minimum lies inside, and there the derivative vanishes ([Strang and Herman, 2016a](#references), theorems 4.1, 4.2 and 4.4). The mean value theorem, $f(b) - f(a) = f'(c)(b - a)$, is Rolle applied to $f$ minus its chord (theorem 4.5).

*Lagrange.* Fix $x$ and choose $K$ with $g(x) = 0$ for $g(t) = f(t) - T_n(t) - K(t - a)^{n+1}$. Since $T_n$ matches the first $n$ derivatives, $g(a) = g'(a) = \dots = g^{(n)}(a) = 0$. Rolle on $[a, x]$ gives $g'(\xi_1) = 0$, Rolle on $[a, \xi_1]$ gives $g''(\xi_2) = 0$, and so on up to $g^{(n+1)}(\xi) = f^{(n+1)}(\xi) - (n + 1)!\,K = 0$. So $K = f^{(n+1)}(\xi)/(n + 1)!$, and $R_n(x) = K(x - a)^{n+1}$ is the Lagrange form.

*Peano.* Here $R = f - T_n$ has $R(a) = \dots = R^{(n)}(a) = 0$. By induction on $n$, such an $R$ is $o\big((x - a)^n\big)$; for $n = 1$ this is the definition of $R'(a) = 0$. For the step, $R'$ satisfies the claim for $n - 1$. Given $\epsilon > 0$, the mean value theorem gives, for $x$ close enough to $a$ and some $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$, $\lvert R(x)\rvert = \lvert R'(\xi)\rvert\,\lvert x - a\rvert \le \epsilon\lvert\xi - a\rvert^{n-1}\lvert x - a\rvert \le \epsilon\lvert x - a\rvert^n$.

</details>

We prove the theorem through an exact formula for the remainder,

$$
R_n(x) = \int_a^x f^{(n+1)}(t)\, \frac{(x - t)^n}{n!}\, dt ,
$$

by induction on $n$. For $n = 0$ it is the fundamental theorem of calculus, $f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^x f'(t)\,dt$. Assume it for $n - 1$ and integrate $R_{n-1}$ by parts, differentiating $f^{(n)}(t)$ and integrating $(x - t)^{n-1}/(n-1)!$, whose antiderivative in $t$ is $-(x - t)^n/n!$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{n-1}(x) &= \int_a^x f^{(n)}(t)\, \frac{(x - t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\, dt \\
&= \Big[-f^{(n)}(t)\, \frac{(x - t)^n}{n!}\Big]_{t=a}^{t=x} + R_n(x) \\
&= \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}\,(x - a)^n + R_n(x) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The bracket vanishes at $t = x$, and its value at $t = a$ is the next term of the Taylor polynomial. So $f = T_{n-1} + R_{n-1} = T_n + R_n$, which completes the induction.

The Lagrange form follows from the integral. The weight $(x - t)^n$ keeps one sign for $t$ between $a$ and $x$. So the integral lies between $m$ and $M$ times $\int_a^x (x - t)^n/n!\,dt$, where $m$ and $M$ are the minimum and maximum of the continuous $f^{(n+1)}$ on the interval. By the intermediate value theorem, $f^{(n+1)}$ takes the value in between at some point $\xi$ ([Strang and Herman, 2016a](#references), theorems 4.1 and 2.11):

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_n(x) &= f^{(n+1)}(\xi) \int_a^x \frac{(x - t)^n}{n!}\, dt \\
&= f^{(n+1)}(\xi)\, \frac{(x - a)^{n+1}}{(n + 1)!} .
\end{aligned}
$$

As $x \to a$ the remainder is $O\big((x - a)^{n+1}\big)$: each extra order buys one more power of the distance.

**Example: $e^x$.** All derivatives of $e^x$ are $e^x$, so at $a = 0$ the polynomial is $T_n(x) = 1 + x + x^2/2 + \dots + x^n/n!$, and the remainder at $x = 1$ is $e^\xi/(n + 1)!$ with $0 < \xi < 1$. The theorem traps the error between $1/(n + 1)!$ and $e/(n + 1)!$:

| $n$ | $T_n(1)$ | error $e - T_n(1)$ | $1/(n+1)!$ | $e/(n+1)!$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $2$ | $0.718$ | $0.500$ | $1.359$ |
| 2 | $2.5$ | $0.218$ | $0.167$ | $0.453$ |
| 3 | $2.6667$ | $0.0516$ | $0.0417$ | $0.113$ |
| 4 | $2.70833$ | $0.00995$ | $0.00833$ | $0.0227$ |

The factorial grows faster than any power, so for $e^x$ the remainder goes to 0 at every $x$. Indeed $\lvert R_n(x)\rvert \le e^{\lvert x\rvert}\lvert x\rvert^{n+1}/(n + 1)!$, and once $k > 2\lvert x\rvert$, each further factor $\lvert x\rvert/k$ of $\lvert x\rvert^{n+1}/(n + 1)!$ is below $\tfrac12$. So the **Taylor series** $\sum_k x^k/k!$ converges to $e^x$ everywhere.

**Example: $\log(1 + x)$.** The $k$-th derivative is $(-1)^{k-1}(k - 1)!/(1 + x)^k$, so at $a = 0$ the coefficients are $(-1)^{k-1}/k$ and $T_n(x) = x - x^2/2 + x^3/3 - \dots$. For $x \ge 0$ the Lagrange remainder is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lvert R_n(x)\rvert &= \frac{n!}{(1 + \xi)^{n+1}} \cdot \frac{x^{n+1}}{(n + 1)!} \\
&= \frac{x^{n+1}}{(n + 1)(1 + \xi)^{n+1}} \le \frac{x^{n+1}}{n + 1} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

because $(1 + \xi)^{n+1} \ge 1$. At $x = 0.1$, where $\log 1.1 = 0.0953102$:

| $n$ | error $\log 1.1 - T_n(0.1)$ | bound $0.1^{n+1}/(n + 1)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $-4.69 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $5.0 \cdot 10^{-3}$ |
| 2 | $3.10 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $3.3 \cdot 10^{-4}$ |
| 3 | $-2.32 \cdot 10^{-5}$ | $2.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ |
| 4 | $1.85 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | $2.0 \cdot 10^{-6}$ |

Each order gains about one digit, a factor $x = 0.1$. At $x = 1.8$ the same polynomials give $1.8$, $0.18$, $2.124$ and $-0.500$, swinging further from $\log 2.8 = 1.030$ at every order. The terms $x^k/k$ grow when $x > 1$. A series whose terms do not tend to 0 diverges, and the bound grows with the terms. A Taylor polynomial is a local statement.

Libraries use Taylor polynomials to evaluate functions near a point where the direct formula loses digits. For $x = 10^{-10}$, rounding $1 + x$ to a double moves it by up to $u = 1.1 \cdot 10^{-16}$, half the spacing of doubles near 1. Since $\log(1 + x) \approx x$ and the slope of $\log$ there is 1, the result inherits that error, up to $u/x = 1.1 \cdot 10^{-6}$ relative; here it comes out as $8 \cdot 10^{-8}$. Near 0 the series says $\log(1 + x) = x - x^2/2 + \dots$, and the library function `log1p` computes it to full precision; `expm1` does the same for $e^x - 1$.

In machine learning the second-order expansion of a log-density around its maximum is the [Laplace approximation](/notes/bayesian-methods/laplace-approximation#the-laplace-approximation), and the expansion of a loss around the current weights is the starting point of every optimizer.

## Why first and second order matter for optimization

The first-order term says in which direction $f$ decreases and how fast; the second-order term says for how long the decrease lasts. Take a gradient step $x \to x - \eta f'(x)$ with step size $\eta > 0$ on a function whose second derivative is bounded, $\lvert f''\rvert \le L$. Write $g = f'(x)$ and expand with Taylor's theorem for $n = 1$ and $h = -\eta g$; the bound on $f''$ then turns the one unknown factor into a guarantee:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x - \eta g) &= f(x) - \eta g^2 + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\, \eta^2 g^2 \\
&\le f(x) - \eta g^2 + \tfrac12 L\eta^2 g^2 && f''(\xi) \le L \\
&= f(x) - \eta\Big(1 - \frac{L\eta}{2}\Big)\, g^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $0 < \eta < 2/L$ the bracket is positive, and every step with $f'(x) \ne 0$ lowers $f$. A line search turns this into a test: it accepts a step when the actual decrease is at least a fixed fraction of the first-order prediction $\eta g^2$ ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 2). The same bound, with $L$ the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian, gives the step-size limit of [gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case).

The second-order term also gives a better step. Minimizing the quadratic model $f(x) + f'(x)h + \tfrac12 f''(x) h^2$ over $h$, when $f''(x) > 0$, gives $f'(x) + f''(x)\,h = 0$, and **Newton's step** $h = -f'(x)/f''(x)$: a gradient step whose size is set by the curvature.

At a **critical point**, where $f'(x^*) = 0$, Taylor with $n = 1$ reads $f(x^* + h) - f(x^*) = \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\, h^2$. If $f''(x^*) > 0$, then $f'' > 0$ near $x^*$ by continuity, and $x^*$ is a strict local minimum; if $f''(x^*) < 0$, a strict local maximum. If $f''(x^*) = 0$ the test says nothing: $x^3$, $x^4$ and $-x^4$ all have $f' = f'' = 0$ at 0, and the first has no extremum there, the second a minimum and the third a maximum.

**Example.** $f(x) = e^x - 2x$ has $f'(x) = e^x - 2$ and $f''(x) = e^x$, so its minimum is at $x^* = \log 2 = 0.6931$, with $f''(x^*) = 2 > 0$. From $x_0 = 0$ the errors $x_k - x^*$ are:

| step $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| gradient descent, $\eta = 0.2$ | $-0.693$ | $-0.493$ | $-0.337$ | $-0.223$ | $-0.143$ |
| Newton | $-0.693$ | $0.307$ | $0.0426$ | $9.0 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $4.0 \cdot 10^{-7}$ |

Near the minimum, gradient descent multiplies the error by about $1 - \eta f''(x^*) = 0.6$ per step. Subtracting $x^*$ from the update and applying the mean value theorem to $f'$, with $f'(x^*) = 0$, gives $x_{k+1} - x^* = \big(1 - \eta f''(\xi_k)\big)(x_k - x^*)$ with $\xi_k$ between $x_k$ and $x^*$. Newton's method roughly squares it, doubling the number of correct digits per step, because it uses the curvature that gradient descent ignores. The [unconstrained optimization note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization#quadratic-convergence) proves Newton's rate, and [logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs) is fitted by the many-variable Newton step.

## Finite differences and their error

When $f$ is available only as a program, its derivative can be approximated by a difference quotient with a small fixed $h$. Taylor's theorem gives the error of the formula, and floating point adds a second error that *grows* as $h$ shrinks. The best $h$ balances the two.

### Truncation error

The **forward difference** $D_+f(x) = \big(f(x + h) - f(x)\big)/h$ comes from Taylor with $n = 1$, and the **central difference** $D_0f(x) = \big(f(x + h) - f(x - h)\big)/(2h)$ from Taylor with $n = 2$ at both $x + h$ and $x - h$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x \pm h) = f(x) &\pm f'(x)\, h + \tfrac12 f''(x)\, h^2 \\
&\pm \tfrac16 f'''(\xi_\pm)\, h^3 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtracting the two expansions cancels $f(x)$ and the $h^2$ terms. The average of $f'''(\xi_+)$ and $f'''(\xi_-)$ is a value $f'''(\xi)$ of the continuous $f'''$ between them, by the intermediate value theorem:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + h) - f(x - h) &= 2f'(x)\, h + \tfrac13 f'''(\xi)\, h^3 , \\
D_0f(x) &= f'(x) + \tfrac16 f'''(\xi)\, h^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The same steps with $n = 1$ give $D_+f(x) = f'(x) + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\,h$. The forward difference is first-order accurate, the central one second-order.

### Rounding error

In double precision every correctly rounded operation has a relative error of at most the **unit roundoff** $u = 2^{-53} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-16}$ ([floating point](/notes/acos/floating-point#rounding-ulp-and-machine-epsilon); [Higham, 2002](#references)). Assume each computed value of $f$ is correctly rounded, as `math.exp` nearly is. Then $f(x + h)$ and $f(x)$ each carry an error of at most about $u\lvert f(x)\rvert$, so the numerator of a difference quotient is wrong by up to about $2u\lvert f(x)\rvert$, and dividing by a small $h$ magnifies it. Subtracting two nearly equal numbers keeps their absolute error but loses most of their significant digits, the standard ill-conditioned computation of the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability).

With $M_k$ a bound on $\lvert f^{(k)}\rvert$ near $x$, the total errors are at most

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_+(h) &= \frac{M_2}{2}\, h + \frac{2u\lvert f(x)\rvert}{h}, \\
E_0(h) &= \frac{M_3}{6}\, h^2 + \frac{u\lvert f(x)\rvert}{h} .
\end{aligned}
$$

### The best step

In each bound one term grows with $h$ and the other falls. The second derivative of each bound in $h$ is positive for $h > 0$, so the bound is convex there, and it is smallest where its derivative in $h$ vanishes. For the forward difference write $E_+ = \alpha h + \beta/h$, with $\alpha = M_2/2$ and $\beta = 2u\lvert f\rvert$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_+'(h) &= \alpha - \beta/h^2 = 0, \\
h_+^* &= \sqrt{\beta/\alpha} = 2\sqrt{u\lvert f\rvert/M_2}, \\
E_+(h_+^*) &= 2\sqrt{\alpha\beta} = 2\sqrt{u\lvert f\rvert M_2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

For the central one write $E_0 = \gamma h^2 + \beta'/h$, with $\gamma = M_3/6$ and $\beta' = u\lvert f\rvert$. At the optimum $\beta'/h = 2\gamma h^2$, which gives the last line:

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_0'(h) &= 2\gamma h - \beta'/h^2 = 0, \\
\big(h_0^*\big)^3 &= \beta'/(2\gamma) = 3u\lvert f\rvert/M_3, \\
E_0(h_0^*) &= 3\gamma\big(h_0^*\big)^2 = \tfrac12 M_3\big(h_0^*\big)^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

When $f$ and its derivatives are of order 1, the forward difference is best at $h \approx \sqrt u \approx 10^{-8}$ and keeps about half of the sixteen digits. The central difference is best at $h \approx u^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}$ and keeps about two thirds ([Nocedal and Wright, 2006](#references)).

**Example.** $f(x) = e^x$ at $x = 1$, where $f = M_2 = M_3 = e$. The forward difference has $h_+^* = 2\sqrt u = 2.1 \cdot 10^{-8}$ and a best error bound of $2e\sqrt u = 5.7 \cdot 10^{-8}$. The central difference has $h_0^* = (3u)^{1/3} = 6.9 \cdot 10^{-6}$ and a bound of $\tfrac e2 (3u)^{2/3} = 6.5 \cdot 10^{-11}$. Measured in double precision:

| step $h$ | forward error | central error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $10^{-3}$ | $1.4 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $4.5 \cdot 10^{-7}$ |
| $10^{-5}$ | $1.4 \cdot 10^{-5}$ | $5.9 \cdot 10^{-11}$ |
| $10^{-6}$ | $1.4 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | $1.6 \cdot 10^{-10}$ |
| $10^{-8}$ | $6.6 \cdot 10^{-9}$ | $6.6 \cdot 10^{-9}$ |
| $10^{-12}$ | $4.3 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $2.1 \cdot 10^{-4}$ |

Both errors fall until rounding takes over and then rise again, the central one from a lower floor and a larger step.

The bounds are worst cases: rounding errors partly cancel, so the measured points scatter below the lines on the rounding side. Two practical details matter. The step should be exactly representable relative to $x$, so that the computed $(x + h) - x$ equals the $h$ we divide by; a power of two such as $2^{-17} \approx 7.6 \cdot 10^{-6}$ guarantees it for $x = 1$, since $1 + 2^{-17}$ is a double. And a finite-difference gradient of a function of $n$ variables costs $n + 1$ or $2n$ evaluations, which is why networks are differentiated by backpropagation and checked by finite differences on a few coordinates only, as in [gradient checking](/notes/ml/backpropagation#checking-gradients-by-finite-differences).

## Where it breaks

Everything above assumes smoothness, and the activations of neural networks are not smooth everywhere. Take $f(x) = \lvert x\rvert$, the shape of a ReLU's kink, at $x = 10^{-6}$, where $f'(x) = 1$ exactly. A central difference with $h = 10^{-5}$ straddles the kink:

$$
\frac{\lvert 1.1 \cdot 10^{-5}\rvert - \lvert -0.9 \cdot 10^{-5}\rvert}{2 \cdot 10^{-5}} = 0.1 .
$$

The answer is wrong by 90%, and no rounding is involved: the Taylor expansion behind the formula does not exist across the kink. With $h = 10^{-7}$, which stays on one side, the difference returns 1. A gradient check that fails near a kink says nothing about the code being checked. Points within $h$ of a kink are excluded from such checks, and [convex analysis](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions) replaces the derivative at a kink by a set of slopes.

Smoothness is not enough for a Taylor series either. The function $e^{-1/x^2}$, extended by 0 at 0, has every derivative equal to 0 at 0, so every $T_n$ is identically 0.

<details>
<summary>Proof: every derivative of e^(−1/x²) vanishes at 0</summary>

For $x \ne 0$, induction with the product and chain rules gives $f^{(n)}(x) = P_n(1/x)\,e^{-1/x^2}$ for a polynomial $P_n$. At 0, by the definition of the derivative and $f^{(n-1)}(0) = 0$,

$$
f^{(n)}(0) = \lim_{h \to 0}\tfrac1h P_{n-1}(1/h)\,e^{-1/h^2} = 0 ,
$$

because with $s = 1/h^2$ every term is a power $\lvert h\rvert^{-j} = s^{j/2}$ times $e^{-s}$, and $s^{j/2}e^{-s} \to 0$: the series of $e^s$ gives $e^s \ge s^m/m!$ for any integer $m > j/2$.

</details>

Its series converges everywhere, to the wrong function: at $x = 0.5$ the function is $e^{-4} = 0.0183$. The remainder formula is still correct; its bound needs $\max\lvert f^{(n+1)}\rvert$ over the whole interval, which grows too fast here, and is rarely known in practice. The bound tells how the error scales with the distance, not always its size.

## What you should remember

- $f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x)h + o(h)$: the derivative is the slope of the best linear approximation.
- The chain rule $(g \circ f)' = g'(f(x))\, f'(x)$ composes linear approximations; backpropagation applies it node by node.
- Taylor: $f(x) = T_n(x) + f^{(n+1)}(\xi)(x - a)^{n+1}/(n + 1)!$; each order adds a power of $x - a$, and a series can still diverge far from $a$.
- With $\lvert f''\rvert \le L$, a gradient step with $\eta < 2/L$ always decreases $f$; Newton's step $-f'/f''$ uses the curvature.
- Forward differences have error $O(h)$, central $O(h^2)$, rounding adds $O(u/h)$; the best steps are about $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-5}$.
- A difference across a kink is wrong however small the rounding error.

## Code

The worked examples: the tangent of $\sqrt x$, the chain rule for the sigmoid, the Taylor errors of $e^x$ and $\log(1 + x)$, `log1p`, gradient descent against Newton, the finite-difference errors, and the two failures. Digits in the last places may vary across platforms and library versions.

```python
import math

# the tangent of sqrt at 4: the error falls like h^2 and matches f''(4) h^2 / 2 = -h^2 / 64
for h in (0.1, 0.05):
    print(h, f'{math.sqrt(4 + h) - (2 + h / 4):.3e}', f'{-h * h / 64:.3e}')
# 0.1 -1.543e-04 -1.563e-04 | 0.05 -3.882e-05 -3.906e-05

# the chain rule: sigma'(1) = sigma(1)(1 - sigma(1)) against a central difference
sig = lambda x: 1 / (1 + math.exp(-x))
print(round(sig(1) * (1 - sig(1)), 6), round((sig(1 + 1e-5) - sig(1 - 1e-5)) / 2e-5, 6))   # 0.196612 0.196612

# Taylor polynomials of e^x at 0, evaluated at 1: the error lies between 1/(n+1)! and e/(n+1)!
T = 1.0
for n in range(1, 5):
    T += 1 / math.factorial(n)
    print(n, round(T, 5), round(math.e - T, 5), round(1 / math.factorial(n + 1), 5), round(math.e / math.factorial(n + 1), 5))
# 1 2.0 0.71828 0.5 1.35914 | 2 2.5 0.21828 0.16667 0.45305 | 3 2.66667 0.05162 0.04167 0.11326 | 4 2.70833 0.00995 0.00833 0.02265

# log(1 + x): one digit per order at x = 0.1, divergence at x = 1.8
for x in (0.1, 1.8):
    T = 0.0
    for n in range(1, 5):
        T += (-1) ** (n + 1) * x ** n / n
        print(x, n, f'{T:.6f}', f'{math.log1p(x) - T:.2e}', f'{x ** (n + 1) / (n + 1):.1e}')
# 0.1: errors -4.69e-03 3.10e-04 -2.32e-05 1.85e-06, bounds 5.0e-03 3.3e-04 2.5e-05 2.0e-06
# 1.8: T_n = 1.800000 0.180000 2.124000 -0.500400, log(2.8) = 1.029619

# log(1 + x) for tiny x: forming 1 + x first loses digits, log1p does not
x = 1e-10
print(f'{(math.log(1 + x) - math.log1p(x)) / math.log1p(x):.1e}')   # 8.3e-08

# gradient descent (step 0.2) against Newton on f(x) = e^x - 2x, minimum at log 2
xg = xn = 0.0
for k in range(5):
    print(k, f'{xg - math.log(2):.3f}', f'{xn - math.log(2):.1e}')
    xg -= 0.2 * (math.exp(xg) - 2)
    xn -= (math.exp(xn) - 2) / math.exp(xn)
# errors: GD -0.693 -0.493 -0.337 -0.223 -0.143; Newton -6.9e-01 3.1e-01 4.3e-02 9.0e-04 4.0e-07

# finite differences of e^x at 1: predicted optimal steps and errors, then measured errors
u = 2.0 ** -53
print(f'{2 * math.sqrt(u):.1e} {2 * math.e * math.sqrt(u):.1e} {(3 * u) ** (1 / 3):.1e} {math.e / 2 * (3 * u) ** (2 / 3):.1e}')
# 2.1e-08 5.7e-08 6.9e-06 6.5e-11
for k in (3, 5, 6, 8, 12):
    h = 10.0 ** -k
    fwd = (math.exp(1 + h) - math.exp(1)) / h
    cen = (math.exp(1 + h) - math.exp(1 - h)) / (2 * h)
    print(k, f'{abs(fwd - math.e):.1e} {abs(cen - math.e):.1e}')
# 3 1.4e-03 4.5e-07 | 5 1.4e-05 5.9e-11 | 6 1.4e-06 1.6e-10 | 8 6.6e-09 6.6e-09 | 12 4.3e-04 2.1e-04

# where it breaks: a central difference across the kink of |x|, and a function whose Taylor series is 0
for h in (1e-5, 1e-7):
    print(h, round((abs(1e-6 + h) - abs(1e-6 - h)) / (2 * h), 6))
print(round(math.exp(-1 / 0.5 ** 2), 4))
# 1e-05 0.1 | 1e-07 1.0
# 0.0183
```
