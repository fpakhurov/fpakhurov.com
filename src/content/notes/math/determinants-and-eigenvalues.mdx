Apply the matrix

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}
$$

to $x = (1, 0)$ again and again: $Ax = (4, 2)$, $A^2x = (18, 14)$, $A^3x = (86, 78)$. Does $A^k x$ grow, shrink or settle? In which direction does it point for large $k$, and how fast does it get there? The entries of $A$ do not say. A Markov chain asks the same questions about its distribution after $k$ steps, a recurrent network about its gradient after $k$ time steps, and gradient descent about its error after $k$ iterations.

Unlike the [orthogonal matrices](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality#orthogonal-matrices) of the previous note, a general square matrix stretches some directions, squeezes others and turns most of them. Two kinds of numbers attached to it answer the questions. The **determinant** says how the map scales volume, and it is zero exactly when the map cannot be undone. The **eigenvalues** are the stretch factors along the directions that the map does not turn, and in those directions repeated application is the power of a number.

## The determinant: how a map scales volume

A linear map sends the unit square, spanned by $e_1$ and $e_2$, to the parallelogram spanned by the columns $Ae_1 = a_1$ and $Ae_2 = a_2$. Every other square of the grid goes to a shifted copy of the same parallelogram, because $A(x + e_1) = Ax + a_1$. So the map multiplies the area of every grid square, and therefore of every region that the grid approximates, by the same factor. That factor, with a sign that records whether the map keeps or reverses orientation, is the determinant.

<DeterminantArea />

Three properties of signed area pin the determinant down, and they are its definition in any dimension:

1. $\det I = 1$: the unit cube has volume 1.
2. $\det$ is linear in each column when the other columns are fixed. Scaling one edge of a parallelogram by $c$ scales its area by $c$, and two parallelograms that share all edges but one, placed side by side, have areas that add.
3. $\det$ is zero when two columns are equal: the parallelogram is flat.

Adding a multiple of one column to another does not change the determinant, by linearity in that column and property 3; geometrically it is a shear, which slides the parallelogram along one edge without changing its area. Swapping two columns flips the sign: expanding $\det(a + b, a + b) = 0$ by linearity leaves $\det(a, b) + \det(b, a) = 0$. The sign is the orientation. A positive determinant keeps the turn from $e_1$ to $e_2$ counterclockwise; a negative one makes it clockwise, as a mirror does.

### The formula for 2-by-2 matrices

Expand each column in the standard basis and use linearity. Every term then has either a repeated basis vector, which gives 0, or the two basis vectors in some order, which gives $\pm 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(ae_1 + ce_2,\; be_1 + de_2)
&= ad\det(e_1, e_2) + cb\det(e_2, e_1) \\
&= ad\det(e_1, e_2) - cb\det(e_1, e_2) && \text{swap} \\
&= ad - bc && \det I = 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The terms $ab\det(e_1, e_1)$ and $cd\det(e_2, e_2)$ of the expansion vanish by property 3. The three matrices of the figure are

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \\
C &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \\
D &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

$\det A = 4 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 2 = 10$: the unit square becomes a parallelogram of area 10, and the turn from $Ae_1 = (4, 2)$ to $Ae_2 = (1, 3)$ is still counterclockwise. $\det C = 1 - 4 = -3$: the area is 3, and the turn from $(1, 2)$ to $(2, 1)$ is clockwise, so $C$ mirrors the plane. $\det D = 4 - 4 = 0$: the columns are parallel, the square is flattened onto a segment, and two different points, such as $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$, land on the same image $(2, 4)$. $D$ has no inverse.

For $n \times n$ matrices, expanding all $n$ columns in the same way gives the **Leibniz formula**, a sum of $n!$ signed products, one per permutation of the columns. It proves that the three properties define exactly one function, and it is useless for computation: for $n = 20$ it has $2.4 \cdot 10^{18}$ terms.

### Properties used everywhere

Each follows from the definition in a line ([Strang, 2011](#references), lecture 18):

- $\det(AB) = \det A \det B$. The map $AB$ applies $B$ and then $A$, so volumes are scaled by $\det B$ and then by $\det A$, and two orientation reversals cancel.
- $A$ is invertible exactly when $\det A \ne 0$, and then $\det A^{-1} = 1/\det A$, since $\det A \det A^{-1} = \det I$. A zero determinant means a flattened image, so some nonzero $x$ has $Ax = 0$.
- $\det A^\top = \det A$. The Leibniz formula treats rows and columns alike, so everything said about columns holds for rows.
- A triangular matrix has the product of its diagonal as determinant. Subtracting multiples of earlier columns clears everything above the diagonal without changing the determinant, and a diagonal matrix scales each axis separately.
- $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$, because each of the $n$ columns is scaled. Doubling a $3 \times 3$ matrix multiplies its determinant by 8.
- An orthogonal matrix has $\det Q = \pm 1$, from $(\det Q)^2 = \det(Q^\top Q) = 1$: $+1$ for rotations and $-1$ for reflections.

The determinant of a sum is not the sum of the determinants; there is no rule for it.

## Computing a determinant by elimination

Gaussian elimination turns $A$ into an upper triangular matrix with two kinds of row operations. Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged, and a row swap flips its sign. With $s$ swaps, the [factorization](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#lu-factorization) $PA = LU$ has $\det P = (-1)^s$, $\det L = 1$ (ones on the diagonal) and $\det U = u_{11} \cdots u_{nn}$, the product of the pivots. Taking determinants of both sides,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det P \det A &= \det L \det U \\
(-1)^s \det A &= u_{11} u_{22} \cdots u_{nn} \\
\det A &= (-1)^s\, u_{11} u_{22} \cdots u_{nn} .
\end{aligned}
$$

For

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix},
$$

subtracting twice row 1 from row 2 and four times row 1 from row 3 gives the rows $(2, 1, 1)$, $(0, 1, 1)$ and $(0, 3, 5)$. Subtracting three times the new row 2 from row 3 gives $(0, 0, 2)$. No swaps were needed, the pivots are 2, 1 and 2, and $\det M = 4$. Elimination costs about $n^3/3$ multiply–adds against the $n!$ terms of the Leibniz formula: for $n = 20$, about 2,500 instead of $2.4 \cdot 10^{18}$.

### The log-determinant

In machine learning the determinant rarely appears alone; its logarithm does. The density of a [multivariate Gaussian](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#the-density) contains $\lvert \Sigma \rvert^{-1/2}$, so its log-likelihood contains minus half of $\log \det \Sigma$. A normalizing flow pays $\log \lvert \det J \rvert$ for every layer, the [change-of-variables](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows#change-of-variables) correction that the [multiple integrals note](/notes/math/multiple-integrals) derives. Flows use layers with [triangular Jacobians](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows#the-trick-a-triangular-jacobian) because then the determinant is the product of the diagonal.

The log-determinant is computed as a sum of logarithms of the pivots, never as the logarithm of their product. The determinant of $0.1\,I$ in 1,000 dimensions is $10^{-1000}$, which underflows to 0 in floating point, while its logarithm, $-2302.6$, is an ordinary number. NumPy's `slogdet` returns the sign and the logarithm for that reason, and Gaussian processes take it from a [Cholesky factor](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#regression-the-posterior-in-closed-form), the subject of the [next note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices).

## Eigenvectors: directions the map only stretches

A nonzero vector $v$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$ with **eigenvalue** $\lambda$ if

$$
Av = \lambda v .
$$

Any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is again one, so it is the direction that matters, the line through $v$. On that line the map is multiplication by $\lambda$: a stretch for $\lambda \gt 1$, a squeeze for $0 \lt \lambda \lt 1$, a flip for negative $\lambda$, and a collapse to $0$ for $\lambda = 0$. Most vectors are turned by the map; the eigenvectors are the exceptions.

### The characteristic polynomial

How do we find the eigenvalues? $\lambda$ is one exactly when $A - \lambda I$ sends some nonzero vector to zero, that is, when it is singular, and singularity is what the determinant detects:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& Av = \lambda v \text{ for some } v \ne 0 \\
\iff\ & (A - \lambda I)v = 0 \text{ for some } v \ne 0 \\
\iff\ & A - \lambda I \text{ is not invertible} \\
\iff\ & \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second step uses that a square matrix is invertible exactly when its kernel is $\{0\}$ ([matrices note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#existence-and-uniqueness)). $\det(A - \lambda I)$ is a polynomial of degree $n$ in $\lambda$, the **characteristic polynomial**, and the eigenvalues are its roots. Once $\lambda$ is known, its eigenvectors are the nonzero vectors of the kernel of $A - \lambda I$, found by elimination. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det\begin{pmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{pmatrix}
&= (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc \\
&= \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

that is, $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} A)\lambda + \det A$, where the **trace** $\operatorname{tr} A$ is the sum of the diagonal entries.

For the opening matrix the trace is 7 and the determinant 10, so the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2)$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 5$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$. The eigenvectors come from

$$
\begin{aligned}
A - 5I &= \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \\
A - 2I &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Both rows of $A - 5I$ say $x_1 = x_2$, so $v_1 = (1, 1)$, and indeed $Av_1 = (5, 5) = 5v_1$. Both rows of $A - 2I$ say $2x_1 + x_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = (1, -2)$, and $Av_2 = (2, -4) = 2v_2$. The two eigenvectors are not orthogonal, $v_1^\top v_2 = -1$; for a non-symmetric matrix that is the normal case.

An eigenvector of $A$ is also an eigenvector of the matrices built from $A$: if $Av = \lambda v$, then $A^2 v = \lambda^2 v$, $A^{-1}v = \lambda^{-1} v$ and $(A + cI)v = (\lambda + c)v$ ([Strang, 2016](#references), section 6.1). For the opening matrix, $A + 3I$ has eigenvalues 8 and 5, and $A^{-1}$ has $0.2$ and $0.5$. The shift is what [ridge regression](/notes/ml/regularization#ridge-a-penalty-on-the-size-of-the-weights) does to $X^\top X$: adding $\lambda I$ raises every eigenvalue by $\lambda$ and keeps the eigenvectors. Eigenvalues of a sum or a product of two different matrices follow no such rule.

Not every real matrix has a real eigenvector. The rotation by $90°$,

$$
R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
$$

turns every direction. Its characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 + 1$, with roots $\pm i$. By the fundamental theorem of algebra, every polynomial of degree $n$ has $n$ complex roots counted with multiplicity, so every $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues in $\mathbb C$. A real matrix has its complex eigenvalues in conjugate pairs, and each pair describes a rotation within a plane. The [next note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices) shows that symmetric matrices never have them.

### Trace and determinant from the eigenvalues

Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ be the $n$ eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, possibly complex. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{tr} A &= \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_n, \\
\det A &= \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n .
\end{aligned}
$$

To see why, compare two expansions of $p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$, which has leading coefficient 1 and the same roots as $\det(A - \lambda I)$. Factored over its roots and multiplied out, it is

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\lambda) &= (\lambda - \lambda_1)\cdots(\lambda - \lambda_n) \\
&= \lambda^n - (\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_n)\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + (-1)^n \lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n .
\end{aligned}
$$

Expanded by the Leibniz formula, the product of the diagonal entries is one term, and every other term leaves out at least two diagonal entries, so it has at most $n - 2$ factors of $\lambda$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\lambda) &= (\lambda - a_{11})\cdots(\lambda - a_{nn}) + (\text{degree} \le n - 2) \\
&= \lambda^n - (a_{11} + \dots + a_{nn})\lambda^{n-1} + \dots
\end{aligned}
$$

The coefficients of $\lambda^{n-1}$ give the trace. For the determinant, set $\lambda = 0$: $p(0) = \det(-A) = (-1)^n \det A$, and the first expansion gives $p(0) = (-1)^n\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n$.

For the opening matrix, $5 + 2 = 7 = 4 + 3$ and $5 \cdot 2 = 10 = \det A$. For the rotation $R$, $i + (-i) = 0 = \operatorname{tr} R$ and $i \cdot (-i) = 1 = \det R$. Two consequences follow at once: a matrix is singular exactly when 0 is one of its eigenvalues, and the total variance $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$ of a covariance matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues, the identity behind [explained variance](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#explained-variance-and-choosing-k) in PCA.

## Diagonalization and matrix powers

Write the $n$ eigenvector equations $Av_i = \lambda_i v_i$ side by side as the columns of one matrix equation. With $V = [v_1 \ \cdots \ v_n]$ and the diagonal $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$, multiplying $V$ by $\Lambda$ on the right scales its columns, so

$$
AV = V\Lambda .
$$

If the $n$ eigenvectors are independent, $V$ is invertible, and multiplying by $V^{-1}$ on the right gives $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$: in the basis of its eigenvectors, $A$ is diagonal. Every power then reduces to a power of $\Lambda$, because each inner $V^{-1}V$ is $I$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^k &= (V\Lambda V^{-1})(V\Lambda V^{-1})\cdots(V\Lambda V^{-1}) \\
&= V\Lambda^k V^{-1} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and $\Lambda^k$ is diagonal with entries $\lambda_i^k$. Read from right to left, $A^k x = V\Lambda^k V^{-1}x$ finds the coordinates $c = V^{-1}x$ of $x$ in the eigenvector basis, multiplies the $i$-th by $\lambda_i^k$, and goes back:

$$
A^k x = c_1\lambda_1^k v_1 + \dots + c_n\lambda_n^k v_n .
$$

So $A^k \to 0$ exactly when every $\lvert \lambda_i \rvert \lt 1$ ([Strang, 2011](#references), lecture 22). For the opening example, $x = (1, 0)$ has the coordinates $c_1 = 2/3$ and $c_2 = 1/3$ in the basis $v_1 = (1, 1)$, $v_2 = (1, -2)$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^k x &= \frac23\, 5^k \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \frac13\, 2^k \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} , \\
A^3 x &= \frac{250}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \frac83 \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 86 \\ 78 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The opening questions now have answers. $A^kx$ grows like $5^k$, and its direction approaches $(1, 1)$. The other component shrinks relative to it by the factor $(2/5)^k$, about $10^{-4}$ after 10 steps. The ratio of the second-largest to the largest eigenvalue sets the speed in every iteration of this kind: [power iteration](/notes/math/iterative-methods#the-power-method) for the leading eigenvector, PageRank, and the mixing of Markov chains below. It also explains the [vanishing and exploding gradients](/notes/nlp/recurrent-networks#gradients-through-time) of recurrent networks, whose gradient through $k$ time steps contains $k$ factors of the recurrent weight matrix, and why the error of [gradient descent on a quadratic](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case) shrinks along each eigenvector at its own rate.

Are there always enough independent eigenvectors? Distinct eigenvalues are sufficient, because eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are independent ([Axler, 2024](#references), 5.11). For two of them: if $c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 = 0$, applying $A$ gives $c_1\lambda_1v_1 + c_2\lambda_2v_2 = 0$. Subtracting $\lambda_2$ times the first equation leaves $c_1(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)v_1 = 0$, so $c_1 = 0$, and then $c_2 = 0$.

<details>
<summary>Proof for any number of eigenvectors</summary>

Suppose $v_1, \dots, v_m$ have distinct eigenvalues and are dependent, with $m$ the smallest number for which this happens, so any $m - 1$ of them are independent. Take $c_1v_1 + \dots + c_mv_m = 0$ with not all $c_i$ zero. Applying $A - \lambda_m I$ gives

$$
\sum_{i \lt m} c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_m)v_i = 0 .
$$

The first $m - 1$ vectors are independent, so every $c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_m) = 0$, and since the eigenvalues are distinct, $c_1 = \dots = c_{m-1} = 0$. Then $c_mv_m = 0$ forces $c_m = 0$, a contradiction.

</details>

Repeated eigenvalues may or may not come with enough eigenvectors; the [last section](#where-it-breaks) shows a case where they do not.

### Powers of a Markov transition matrix

The [Markov chains note](/notes/probability/markov-chains#stationary-distributions) of the probability course models the weather (sunny, cloudy, rainy) with the transition matrix

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} ,
$$

where row $i$ holds the probabilities of moving from state $i$. A distribution over states is a row vector $\pi$ that evolves as $\pi_t = \pi_0 P^t$. That note stated two facts that diagonalization explains: the distribution converges to the stationary $\pi = (21, 13, 12)/46$, and the distance to it shrinks like $0.473^t$.

Every row of $P$ sums to 1, so $P(1, 1, 1)^\top = (1, 1, 1)^\top$ and 1 is an eigenvalue. A matrix and its transpose have the same characteristic polynomial, since $P^\top - \lambda I$ is the transpose of $P - \lambda I$ and transposing keeps the determinant, so 1 is also an eigenvalue of $P^\top$. Its eigenvector, written as a row, satisfies $\pi P = \pi$: the stationary distribution is a **left eigenvector** of $P$.

The trace gives $1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = 1.6$, and the characteristic polynomial factors as $(\lambda - 1)(\lambda^2 - 0.6\lambda + 0.06)$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_2 &= 0.3 + \sqrt{0.03} \approx 0.473 , \\
\lambda_3 &= 0.3 - \sqrt{0.03} \approx 0.127 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The determinant checks it: $1 \cdot 0.473 \cdot 0.127 = 0.06 = \det P$. The three eigenvalues are distinct, so $P$ is diagonalizable, and the distribution after $t$ steps is a combination of $1^t$, $0.473^t$ and $0.127^t$ times fixed vectors. The first term is the stationary distribution, and it is the only one that survives:

$$
\pi_t = \pi + \alpha_2\, 0.473^t\, w_2^\top + \alpha_3\, 0.127^t\, w_3^\top ,
$$

where $w_2, w_3$ are the other left eigenvectors and $\alpha_2, \alpha_3$ depend on the starting distribution. The distance to $\pi$ therefore shrinks by a factor approaching $\lvert \lambda_2 \rvert = 0.473$ per step, whatever the start. From a sunny day the total absolute difference $\lVert \pi_t - \pi \rVert_1$ is $1.087$ at $t = 0$, $0.487$ at $t = 1$ and $5.7 \cdot 10^{-4}$ at $t = 10$:

<MarkovConvergence />

The gap between 1 and $\lvert \lambda_2 \rvert$, the **spectral gap**, decides whether a chain mixes fast or slowly, and [MCMC samplers](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo) try to keep it large.

## Where it breaks

### When diagonalization fails

The shear

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

has characteristic polynomial $(1 - \lambda)^2$, so its only eigenvalue is 1, a double root. Its eigenvectors form the kernel of

$$
J - I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
$$

which is the line through $(1, 0)$ alone. The **geometric multiplicity** of the eigenvalue, the dimension of its eigenvector space, is 1; its **algebraic multiplicity**, the multiplicity as a root, is 2. There is no basis of eigenvectors, and $J$ cannot be diagonalized; this is the standard defective example ([Oseledets, 2024](#references), lecture 6).

The eigenvalues then no longer predict the powers. Both eigenvalues of $J$ have absolute value 1, yet

$$
J^k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

grows without bound, linearly in $k$. Every square matrix can still be brought to an almost diagonal form, the **Jordan form**, with blocks like $J$ on the diagonal. Its powers contain terms $k^m\lambda^k$, so the eigenvalues still decide whether $A^k \to 0$, but only up to such polynomial factors.

Exactly defective matrices are rare in practice, since an arbitrarily small change separates the repeated eigenvalue. Nearly defective ones are common and cause the real trouble. Adding $\varepsilon$ to the corner of $J$ gives distinct eigenvalues 1 and $1 + \varepsilon$, with eigenvectors $(1, 0)$ and $(1, \varepsilon)$, almost parallel. For $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$ the condition number of $V$ is $2 \cdot 10^8$, and $V\Lambda^kV^{-1}$, computed from a nearly singular basis, loses about eight digits in floating point; the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) measures such losses. Symmetric matrices avoid the problem with an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors ([next note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices)), and every matrix has a decomposition with orthonormal bases on both sides, the [singular value decomposition](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition).

### The determinant does not measure near-singularity

$\det(0.1\,I) = 10^{-100}$ in 100 dimensions, yet $0.1\,I$ is as easy to invert as $I$. The matrix with rows $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 1 + 10^{-12})$ has a determinant of about $10^{-12}$ too, and it is nearly singular: a change of $10^{-12}$ in one entry makes it singular. A small determinant can come from scale alone. The right measure is the condition number of the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability), the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value: 1 for $0.1\,I$, and $4 \cdot 10^{12}$ for the second matrix.

### Eigenvalues are not computed from the polynomial

The characteristic polynomial is a tool for proofs and small examples. For $n \ge 5$ its roots have no general formula in radicals, so every eigenvalue algorithm is iterative. Its roots are also badly conditioned functions of its coefficients. The diagonal matrix with entries $1, 2, \dots, 20$ has eigenvalues $1, \dots, 20$, which an eigenvalue routine returns exactly. Its characteristic polynomial has coefficients up to about $10^{19}$, which double precision rounds, and the roots of the rounded polynomial are off by up to $0.039$. Libraries work on the matrix itself, with the QR algorithm.

## What you should remember

- $\det A$ is the signed factor by which $A$ scales volume, $ad - bc$ in 2D; it is zero exactly when $A$ is singular, and $\det(AB) = \det A \det B$.
- Compute it by elimination, as $(-1)^s$ times the product of the pivots, and use the log-determinant in likelihoods.
- Eigenvalues are the roots of $\det(A - \lambda I)$; $\operatorname{tr} A$ is their sum and $\det A$ their product.
- With $n$ independent eigenvectors, $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$ and $A^k = V\Lambda^kV^{-1}$; distinct eigenvalues guarantee it.
- The largest $\lvert\lambda\rvert$ dominates $A^k$, and $\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert$ sets the speed; a Markov chain approaches $\pi$ like $\lvert\lambda_2\rvert^t$.
- A shear has one eigenvector for a double eigenvalue, and its powers grow although $\lvert\lambda\rvert = 1$.

## Code

The note's examples checked with NumPy: the determinant by elimination against the library, the log-determinant that does not underflow, the eigenvalues, trace and determinant identities, shifts and powers, matrix powers through diagonalization, the weather chain, and the three failures. It runs in well under a second; exact digits in the last places can differ across platforms and library versions.

```python
import math
import numpy as np

# 1. 2x2 determinants: area 10, a mirror with -3, a flattened square with 0
A = np.array([[4.0, 1.0], [2.0, 3.0]])
C = np.array([[1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 1.0]])
D = np.array([[2.0, 1.0], [4.0, 2.0]])
print([round(float(np.linalg.det(m)), 6) for m in (A, C, D)])
# [10.0, -3.0, 0.0]

# 2. the determinant from the pivots of elimination with partial pivoting: (-1)^swaps times their product
def det_by_elimination(M):
    U = M.astype(float).copy(); n = len(U); sign = 1.0
    for k in range(n):
        p = k + np.argmax(np.abs(U[k:, k]))          # the largest pivot candidate, for stability
        if U[p, k] == 0: return 0.0
        if p != k: U[[k, p]] = U[[p, k]]; sign = -sign
        U[k + 1:] -= np.outer(U[k + 1:, k] / U[k, k], U[k])
    return sign * np.prod(np.diag(U))
M = np.array([[2.0, 1.0, 1.0], [4.0, 3.0, 3.0], [8.0, 7.0, 9.0]])
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
R = rng.normal(size=(6, 6))
print(round(det_by_elimination(M), 6), round(det_by_elimination(R), 6), round(float(np.linalg.det(R)), 6))
# 4.0 -2.340511 -2.340511
# (the hand elimination of the note used no swaps; partial pivoting swaps rows but the determinant is the same)
```

## Next

The eigenvectors of the opening matrix were not orthogonal, the rotation had none in real space, and the shear had too few. The matrices that machine learning builds most often, covariance matrices, Hessians, kernel matrices and $X^\top X$, have none of these problems because they are symmetric. The [next note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices) proves that a symmetric matrix always has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and uses them to read the shape of a quadratic form, to test positive definiteness and to find the directions of largest and smallest curvature.
