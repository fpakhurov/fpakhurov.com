The [previous note](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality) ended with orthogonal matrices, the square matrices that keep every length and every angle. A general square matrix does neither: it stretches some directions, squeezes others and turns most of them. This note asks two questions about such a map. How much does it change volumes? The answer is one number, the **determinant**, and it is zero exactly when the map cannot be undone. Are there directions that the map only stretches, without turning them? Those are the **eigenvectors**, and the stretch factors are the **eigenvalues**. In a basis of eigenvectors the matrix becomes diagonal, and repeated application, the operation behind Markov chains, recurrent networks and every iterative optimizer, becomes the power of a few numbers.

**Notation.** $A \in \mathbb R^{n \times n}$ is a square matrix with columns $a_1, \dots, a_n$ and entries $a_{ij}$. Vectors are lowercase letters written as columns, $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is the standard basis, and $I$ the identity. $\det A$ is the determinant and $\operatorname{tr} A = a_{11} + \dots + a_{nn}$ the trace. An eigenvalue is $\lambda$ and an eigenvector $v$; $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$ and $V = [v_1 \ \cdots \ v_n]$ collect them. Rank, null space and Gaussian elimination are those of the [matrices note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems).

## Baseline: what does a matrix do when applied many times?

Take

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Applying $A$ again and again gives $Ax = (4, 2)$, $A^2x = (18, 14)$ and $A^3x = (86, 78)$. Each step costs a matrix–vector product, $n^2$ multiplications, and the entries of $A$ say little about the questions that matter for a repeated map. Does $A^k x$ grow, shrink or settle? In which direction does it point for large $k$? How fast does it get there? A Markov chain asks these questions about its distribution after $k$ steps, a recurrent network about its gradient after $k$ time steps, and gradient descent about its error after $k$ iterations. This note answers them with two tools. The determinant measures how the map scales volume. The eigenvalues and eigenvectors give coordinates in which the map acts on each axis separately.

## The determinant: how a map scales volume

A linear map sends the unit square, spanned by $e_1$ and $e_2$, to the parallelogram spanned by the columns $Ae_1 = a_1$ and $Ae_2 = a_2$. Every other square of the grid goes to a shifted copy of the same parallelogram, because $A(x + e_1) = Ax + a_1$. So the map multiplies the area of every grid square, and therefore of every region that the grid approximates, by the same factor. That factor, with a sign that records whether the map keeps or reverses orientation, is the determinant.

<DeterminantArea />

Three properties of signed area pin the determinant down completely, and they are its definition in any dimension:

1. $\det I = 1$: the unit cube has volume 1.
2. $\det$ is linear in each column when the other columns are fixed. Scaling one edge of a parallelogram by $c$ scales its area by $c$, and two parallelograms that share all edges but one, placed side by side, have areas that add.
3. $\det$ is zero when two columns are equal: the parallelogram is flat.

Two consequences follow at once. Adding a multiple of one column to another does not change the determinant, by linearity in that column and property 3; geometrically, it is a shear, which moves the parallelogram along one edge without changing its area. Swapping two columns flips the sign: expand $\det(a + b, a + b) = 0$ by linearity, and the terms $\det(a, a)$ and $\det(b, b)$ vanish, which leaves $\det(a, b) = -\det(b, a)$. The sign is the orientation: a positive determinant keeps the turn from $e_1$ to $e_2$ counterclockwise, a negative one makes it clockwise, as a mirror does.

### The formula for 2-by-2 matrices

The idea: write each column in the standard basis and expand by linearity. Every term then has either a repeated basis vector, which gives 0, or the two basis vectors in some order, which gives $\pm 1$. The result is the following.

$$
\boxed{\;\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc\;}
$$

Why it holds:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(ae_1 + ce_2,\; be_1 + de_2)
&= ab\det(e_1, e_1) + ad\det(e_1, e_2) + cb\det(e_2, e_1) + cd\det(e_2, e_2) \\
&= 0 + ad\det(e_1, e_2) + cb\det(e_2, e_1) + 0 \\
&= ad\det(e_1, e_2) - cb\det(e_1, e_2) \\
&= ad - bc .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line expands the first column and then the second by linearity (property 2). The second drops the determinants with a repeated column (property 3). The third swaps the columns of the remaining term, which flips its sign. The fourth uses $\det(e_1, e_2) = \det I = 1$ (property 1).

**Worked example.** The three matrices of the figure:

- $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\det A = 4 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 2 = 10$. The unit square becomes a parallelogram of area 10, and the turn from $Ae_1 = (4, 2)$ to $Ae_2 = (1, 3)$ is still counterclockwise.
- $C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\det C = 1 - 4 = -3$. The area is 3, and the turn from $(1, 2)$ to $(2, 1)$ is clockwise: $C$ mirrors the plane.
- $D = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\det D = 4 - 4 = 0$. Its columns are parallel, the square is flattened onto a segment, and two different points, such as $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$, land on the same image $(2, 4)$. $D$ has no inverse.

For $n \times n$ matrices, expanding all $n$ columns in the same way gives the **Leibniz formula**, a sum of $n!$ signed products, one per permutation of the columns. It proves that the three properties define exactly one function, and it is useless for computation: for $n = 20$ it has $2.4 \cdot 10^{18}$ terms.

### Properties used everywhere

Each of these follows from the definition; the reasons are one line each.

- **Products multiply:** $\det(AB) = \det A \det B$. The map $AB$ applies $B$ and then $A$, so volumes are scaled by $\det B$ and then by $\det A$, and two orientation reversals cancel.
- **Inverses:** $A$ is invertible exactly when $\det A \ne 0$, and then $\det A^{-1} = 1/\det A$, from $\det A \det A^{-1} = \det I = 1$. A zero determinant means a flattened image, so some nonzero $x$ has $Ax = 0$.
- **Transpose:** $\det A^\top = \det A$. The Leibniz formula treats rows and columns the same way, so everything said about columns holds for rows.
- **Triangular matrices:** the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries. Subtracting multiples of earlier columns clears everything above the diagonal without changing the determinant, and a diagonal matrix scales each axis separately.
- **Scaling the whole matrix:** $\det(cA) = c^n \det A$, because each of the $n$ columns is scaled. Doubling a $3 \times 3$ matrix multiplies its determinant by 8, not 2.
- **Orthogonal matrices:** $\det Q = \pm 1$, from $\det(Q^\top Q) = (\det Q)^2 = \det I = 1$. Rotations have $+1$ and reflections $-1$, as the [previous note](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality#orthogonal-matrices) found geometrically.

## Computing a determinant by elimination

The idea: Gaussian elimination turns $A$ into an upper triangular matrix using two kinds of row operations. Adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged, and a row swap flips its sign. The determinant of the triangular result is the product of its diagonal, the pivots. With $s$ row swaps and the pivots $u_{11}, \dots, u_{nn}$ of the [factorization](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#lu-factorization) $PA = LU$ the result is the following.

$$
\boxed{\;\det A = (-1)^s\, u_{11} u_{22} \cdots u_{nn}\;}
$$

Why it holds:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det P \det A &= \det L \det U \\
(-1)^s \det A &= 1 \cdot u_{11} u_{22} \cdots u_{nn} \\
\det A &= (-1)^s\, u_{11} u_{22} \cdots u_{nn} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line takes determinants of both sides of $PA = LU$ and uses the product rule. The second evaluates each factor: $P$ is the identity with rows swapped $s$ times, so $\det P = (-1)^s$; $L$ is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal, so $\det L = 1$; $U$ is upper triangular, so its determinant is the product of the pivots. The third multiplies both sides by $(-1)^s$, using $(-1)^{2s} = 1$.

**Worked example.** For

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix},
$$

subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2 and 4 times row 1 from row 3. That gives the rows $(2, 1, 1)$, $(0, 1, 1)$ and $(0, 3, 5)$. Subtract 3 times the new row 2 from row 3, which gives $(0, 0, 2)$. No swaps were needed, the pivots are 2, 1 and 2, and $\det M = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 4$. Elimination costs about $\tfrac23 n^3$ operations against the $n!$ terms of the Leibniz formula: for $n = 20$ about 5,000 instead of $2.4 \cdot 10^{18}$.

**Where machine learning needs it.** The determinant rarely appears alone; its logarithm does. The density of a [multivariate Gaussian](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#the-density) contains $\lvert \Sigma \rvert^{-1/2}$, so its log-likelihood contains $-\tfrac12 \log \det \Sigma$. A normalizing flow pays $\log \lvert \det J \rvert$ for every layer, the [change-of-variables](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows#change-of-variables) correction that the [multiple integrals note](/notes/math/multiple-integrals) derives; flows use layers with [triangular Jacobians](/notes/generative-models/normalizing-flows#the-trick-a-triangular-jacobian) because then the determinant is the product of the diagonal. The log-determinant is always computed as a sum of logarithms of pivots, never as the logarithm of the product. The determinant of $0.1\,I$ in 1,000 dimensions is $10^{-1000}$, which underflows to 0 in floating point, while its logarithm, $-2302.6$, is an ordinary number. NumPy's `slogdet` returns the sign and the logarithm for that reason, and Gaussian processes take it from a [Cholesky factor](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#regression-the-posterior-in-closed-form), the subject of the [next note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices).

## Eigenvectors: directions the map only stretches

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Most vectors are turned by a matrix. An eigenvector is not: $Av = \lambda v$, so the line through $v$ is mapped onto itself and stretched by $\lambda$. If a matrix has $n$ independent eigenvectors, then in their coordinates it acts on each axis separately, $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$, and $A^k = V\Lambda^k V^{-1}$: the long-run behaviour of a repeated map is read off the largest eigenvalues.</aside>

A nonzero vector $v$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$ with **eigenvalue** $\lambda$ if

$$
Av = \lambda v .
$$

Any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is again one, so it is the direction that matters, the line through $v$. On that line the map is multiplication by $\lambda$: a stretch for $\lambda \gt 1$, a squeeze for $0 \lt \lambda \lt 1$, a flip for negative $\lambda$, and a collapse to $0$ for $\lambda = 0$.

### The characteristic polynomial

The idea: $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue when the matrix $A - \lambda I$ sends some nonzero vector to zero, that is, when it is singular, and singularity is detected by the determinant. The eigenvalues are therefore the roots of a polynomial of degree $n$ in $\lambda$, the **characteristic polynomial**.

$$
\boxed{\;\lambda \text{ is an eigenvalue of } A \iff \det(A - \lambda I) = 0\;}
$$

Why it holds:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Av = \lambda v \text{ for some } v \ne 0
&\iff (A - \lambda I)v = 0 \text{ for some } v \ne 0 \\
&\iff A - \lambda I \text{ is not invertible} \\
&\iff \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first step moves $\lambda v = \lambda Iv$ to the left side. The second uses that a square matrix is invertible exactly when its null space is $\{0\}$ (the [matrices note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems)). The third is the determinant test for invertibility from the previous section. Once $\lambda$ is known, its eigenvectors are the nonzero vectors of the null space of $A - \lambda I$, found by elimination.

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix the polynomial is

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc = \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc),
$$

that is, $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} A)\lambda + \det A$.

**Worked example.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, the trace is 7 and the determinant 10, so the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2)$ and the eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 5$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$.

- For $\lambda_1 = 5$: $A - 5I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$. Both rows say $x_1 = x_2$, so $v_1 = (1, 1)$. Check: $Av_1 = (4 + 1, 2 + 3) = (5, 5) = 5v_1$.
- For $\lambda_2 = 2$: $A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Both rows say $2x_1 + x_2 = 0$, so $v_2 = (1, -2)$. Check: $Av_2 = (4 - 2, 2 - 6) = (2, -4) = 2v_2$.

The two eigenvectors are not orthogonal: $v_1^\top v_2 = 1 - 2 = -1$. For a non-symmetric matrix that is the normal case.

**Complex eigenvalues.** The rotation by $90°$, $R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, turns every direction, so it has no real eigenvector. Its characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 + 1$, with roots $\pm i$. By the fundamental theorem of algebra, every polynomial of degree $n$ has $n$ complex roots counted with multiplicity, so every $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues in $\mathbb C$. Real matrices have their complex eigenvalues in conjugate pairs, and the pairs describe rotation within a plane. The [next note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices) shows that symmetric matrices never have them.

### Trace and determinant from the eigenvalues

The idea: factor the characteristic polynomial over its roots and compare two of its coefficients with the same coefficients computed from the entries of $A$. The constant term gives the determinant and the second-highest coefficient gives the trace. With the $n$ eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$ counted with multiplicity, possibly complex, the result is the following.

$$
\boxed{\;\operatorname{tr} A = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_n, \qquad \det A = \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n\;}
$$

Why it holds. Write $p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$, which has leading coefficient 1 and the same roots as $\det(A - \lambda I)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(\lambda) &= (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2)\cdots(\lambda - \lambda_n) \\
&= \lambda^n - (\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_n)\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + (-1)^n \lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n , \\
p(\lambda) &= (\lambda - a_{11})(\lambda - a_{22})\cdots(\lambda - a_{nn}) + (\text{terms of degree at most } n - 2) \\
&= \lambda^n - (a_{11} + \dots + a_{nn})\lambda^{n-1} + \dots , \\
p(0) &= \det(-A) = (-1)^n \det A .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line factors the polynomial over its $n$ complex roots. The second multiplies out: $\lambda^{n-1}$ arises by choosing $\lambda$ from all brackets but one, and the constant term by choosing $-\lambda_i$ from every bracket. The third expands $\det(\lambda I - A)$ by the Leibniz formula: the product of the diagonal entries is one term, and every other term leaves out at least two diagonal entries, so it contains at most $n - 2$ factors of $\lambda$. The fourth multiplies out that diagonal product. The fifth sets $\lambda = 0$ and uses $\det(cA) = c^n\det A$ with $c = -1$. Comparing the coefficients of $\lambda^{n-1}$ in the second and fourth lines gives the trace; comparing the constant term of the second line with the fifth gives $(-1)^n\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n = (-1)^n\det A$, the determinant.

**Worked example.** For $A$: $5 + 2 = 7 = 4 + 3$, and $5 \cdot 2 = 10 = \det A$. For the rotation $R$: $i + (-i) = 0 = \operatorname{tr} R$ and $i \cdot (-i) = 1 = \det R$. Two quick consequences: a matrix is singular exactly when 0 is one of its eigenvalues, and the total variance $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$ of a covariance matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues, the identity behind [explained variance](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#explained-variance-and-choosing-k) in PCA.

## Diagonalization and matrix powers

The idea: write each eigenvector equation as one column of a matrix equation. If $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, the matrix $V$ that holds them is invertible, and multiplying by $V^{-1}$ turns $A$ into the diagonal matrix of its eigenvalues. Then every power of $A$ is a power of that diagonal matrix.

$$
\boxed{\;A = V\Lambda V^{-1}, \qquad A^k = V\Lambda^k V^{-1}\;}
$$

Why it holds:

$$
\begin{aligned}
AV &= [Av_1 \ \cdots \ Av_n] = [\lambda_1v_1 \ \cdots \ \lambda_nv_n] = V\Lambda , \\
A &= V\Lambda V^{-1} , \\
A^k &= (V\Lambda V^{-1})(V\Lambda V^{-1})\cdots(V\Lambda V^{-1}) = V\Lambda (V^{-1}V)\Lambda (V^{-1}V) \cdots \Lambda V^{-1} = V\Lambda^k V^{-1} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line multiplies $A$ into $V$ column by column and uses the eigenvector equations; multiplying $V$ by a diagonal matrix on the right scales its columns. The second multiplies both sides by $V^{-1}$ on the right, which exists because the columns of $V$ are independent. The third writes out $k$ copies and regroups: every inner $V^{-1}V$ is $I$. And $\Lambda^k$ is diagonal with entries $\lambda_i^k$. Read from right to left, $A^kx = V\Lambda^kV^{-1}x$ says: find the coordinates $c = V^{-1}x$ of $x$ in the eigenvector basis, multiply the $i$-th coordinate by $\lambda_i^k$, and go back:

$$
A^kx = c_1\lambda_1^kv_1 + \dots + c_n\lambda_n^kv_n .
$$

**Worked example.** Return to the baseline, $x = (1, 0)$. Solving $c_1(1, 1) + c_2(1, -2) = (1, 0)$ gives $c_1 = \tfrac23$ and $c_2 = \tfrac13$, so

$$
A^k x = \tfrac23\, 5^k \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \tfrac13\, 2^k \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $k = 3$ this is $\tfrac{250}{3}(1, 1) + \tfrac83(1, -2) = (86, 78)$, as the baseline found by brute force. The formula answers its three questions. $A^kx$ grows like $5^k$. Its direction approaches $(1, 1)$. The other component shrinks relative to it by the factor $(2/5)^k$, about $10^{-4}$ after 10 steps. The ratio of the second-largest to the largest eigenvalue sets the speed in every iteration of this kind: [power iteration](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#computing-the-largest-eigenvalue-power-iteration) for the leading eigenvector, PageRank, and the mixing of Markov chains below. The same picture explains why recurrent networks have [vanishing and exploding gradients](/notes/nlp/recurrent-networks#gradients-through-time), since the gradient through $k$ time steps contains $k$ factors of the recurrent weight matrix, and why the error of [gradient descent on a quadratic](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case) shrinks along each eigenvector at its own rate.

**When are there enough eigenvectors?** A sufficient condition is that the $n$ eigenvalues are distinct, because eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are always linearly independent. For two of them: if $c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 = 0$, applying $A$ gives $c_1\lambda_1v_1 + c_2\lambda_2v_2 = 0$. Subtracting $\lambda_2$ times the first equation leaves $c_1(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)v_1 = 0$, so $c_1 = 0$ because $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$ and $v_1 \ne 0$; then $c_2 = 0$ as well.

<details>
<summary>Proof for any number of eigenvectors</summary>

Suppose $v_1, \dots, v_m$ have distinct eigenvalues and are dependent, and let $m$ be the smallest number for which this happens, so any $m - 1$ of them are independent. Take $c_1v_1 + \dots + c_mv_m = 0$ with not all $c_i$ zero. Applying $A$ gives $\sum_i c_i\lambda_iv_i = 0$, and subtracting $\lambda_m$ times the original equation gives $\sum_{i \lt m} c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_m)v_i = 0$. The first $m - 1$ vectors are independent, so every $c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_m) = 0$, and since the eigenvalues are distinct, $c_1 = \dots = c_{m-1} = 0$. Then $c_mv_m = 0$ forces $c_m = 0$, a contradiction.

</details>

Repeated eigenvalues may or may not come with enough eigenvectors; the [last section](#when-diagonalization-fails) shows a case where they do not.

### Powers of a Markov transition matrix

The [Markov chains note](/notes/probability/markov-chains#stationary-distributions) of the probability course models the weather with the transition matrix

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 & 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

(states sunny, cloudy, rainy), where row $i$ holds the probabilities of moving from state $i$, and a distribution over states is a row vector $\pi$ that evolves as $\pi_t = \pi_0 P^t$. That note stated two facts that diagonalization explains: the distribution converges to the stationary $\pi = (21, 13, 12)/46$, and the distance to it shrinks like $0.473^t$.

**Eigenvalue 1.** Every row of $P$ sums to 1, so $P(1, 1, 1)^\top = (1, 1, 1)^\top$, and 1 is an eigenvalue. A matrix and its transpose have the same characteristic polynomial, because $\det(P^\top - \lambda I) = \det\big((P - \lambda I)^\top\big) = \det(P - \lambda I)$, so 1 is also an eigenvalue of $P^\top$. Its eigenvector, written as a row, satisfies $\pi P = \pi$: the stationary distribution is a **left eigenvector** of $P$.

**The other two.** The trace gives $1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = 1.6$, and the characteristic polynomial factors as $(\lambda - 1)(\lambda^2 - 0.6\lambda + 0.06)$, so $\lambda_{2,3} = 0.3 \pm \sqrt{0.03}$, that is, $\lambda_2 = 0.473$ and $\lambda_3 = 0.127$. Check with the determinant: $1 \cdot 0.473 \cdot 0.127 = 0.06 = \det P$.

**The powers.** The three eigenvalues are distinct, so $P$ is diagonalizable, and the distribution after $t$ steps is a combination of $1^t$, $0.473^t$ and $0.127^t$ times fixed vectors. The first term is the stationary distribution, and it is the only one that survives:

$$
\pi_t = \pi + \alpha_2\, 0.473^t\, w_2^\top + \alpha_3\, 0.127^t\, w_3^\top ,
$$

where $w_2, w_3$ are the left eigenvectors and $\alpha_2, \alpha_3$ depend on the starting distribution. The distance to $\pi$ therefore shrinks by a factor approaching $\lvert \lambda_2 \rvert = 0.473$ per step, whatever the start. From a sunny day the total absolute difference $\lVert \pi_t - \pi \rVert_1$ is $1.087$ at $t = 0$, $0.487$ at $t = 1$ and $5.7 \cdot 10^{-4}$ at $t = 10$:

<MarkovConvergence />

The gap between 1 and $\lvert \lambda_2 \rvert$, the **spectral gap**, is what makes a chain mix fast or slowly, and it is the quantity that [MCMC samplers](/notes/probability/markov-chain-monte-carlo) try to keep large.

## When diagonalization fails

The shear

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

has characteristic polynomial $(1 - \lambda)^2$, so its only eigenvalue is 1, a double root. The eigenvectors are the null space of $J - I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is the line through $(1, 0)$ alone. One independent eigenvector for an eigenvalue of multiplicity two: the **geometric multiplicity** (the dimension of the eigenvector space, here 1) is smaller than the **algebraic multiplicity** (the multiplicity as a root, here 2), there is no basis of eigenvectors, and $J$ cannot be diagonalized.

The eigenvalues then no longer predict the powers. Both eigenvalues of $J$ have absolute value 1, yet

$$
J^k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

which grows without bound, linearly in $k$. Every square matrix can still be brought to an almost diagonal form, the **Jordan form**, with blocks like $J$ on the diagonal, and its powers contain terms $k^m\lambda^k$; the eigenvalues still decide whether $A^k \to 0$, but only up to such polynomial factors.

Exactly defective matrices are rare in practice, since an arbitrarily small change separates the repeated eigenvalue. Nearly defective ones are common and cause the real trouble. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 + \varepsilon \end{pmatrix}$ has distinct eigenvalues 1 and $1 + \varepsilon$ and eigenvectors $(1, 0)$ and $(1, \varepsilon)$, almost parallel. Its $V$ is nearly singular, and $V\Lambda^kV^{-1}$ is computed from a nearly singular basis, which loses accuracy in floating point; the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) measures this loss. The two notes that follow avoid the problem. Symmetric matrices always have an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors ([next note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices)), and every matrix, even a rectangular one, has a decomposition with orthonormal bases on both sides, the [singular value decomposition](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition).

For $-1$: the null space of $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $v = (1, -1)$. (3) The roots of $\lambda^2 - \lambda - 6 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda + 2)$ are 3 and $-2$. $A^kx$ grows like $3^k$ and turns towards the eigenvector of 3; the component along the other eigenvector alternates in sign and shrinks relative to it like $(2/3)^k$.</details></aside>

## What changed

| Question about a square matrix | From the entries | With determinants and eigenvalues |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is it invertible? | run elimination and look for a zero pivot | $\det A \ne 0$, equivalently 0 is not an eigenvalue |
| How does it change volume? | not visible | by the factor $\lvert \det A \rvert$, reversing orientation if $\det A \lt 0$ |
| What does $A^k$ do? | $k$ matrix products | $V\Lambda^kV^{-1}$: each eigen-coordinate is multiplied by $\lambda_i^k$ |
| Which direction wins for large $k$? | not visible | the eigenvector of the largest $\lvert \lambda \rvert$, at speed $\lvert \lambda_2/\lambda_1 \rvert$ |
| Cost of the determinant | $n!$ terms | $\tfrac23 n^3$ by elimination, as a sum of log-pivots |

## Limitations

- **The determinant does not measure closeness to singularity.** $\det(0.1\,I) = 10^{-100}$ in 100 dimensions, yet the matrix is perfectly well behaved; the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 + 10^{-12} \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $10^{-12}$ and is nearly singular. The right measure is the ratio of singular values, the condition number of the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability).
- **Non-symmetric matrices have awkward eigenvectors.** The eigenvalues can be complex, the eigenvectors are not orthogonal, and nearly parallel eigenvectors make $V^{-1}$ inaccurate. Some matrices have too few eigenvectors to diagonalize at all.
- **The characteristic polynomial is a tool for proofs and small examples, not for computation.** For $n \ge 5$ its roots have no general formula in radicals (the Abel–Ruffini theorem), so every eigenvalue algorithm is iterative, and finding the roots of the polynomial from its coefficients is badly conditioned. Libraries use the QR algorithm on the matrix itself ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), lectures 25–29).
- **Eigenvalues of a product or a sum are not products or sums of eigenvalues.** Only the determinant and the trace have such rules: $\det(AB) = \det A\det B$ and $\operatorname{tr}(A + B) = \operatorname{tr}A + \operatorname{tr}B$.

## What you should remember

- $\det A$ is the signed factor by which $A$ scales volume; it is zero exactly when $A$ is singular. In 2D it is $ad - bc$.
- $\det(AB) = \det A\det B$, $\det A^\top = \det A$, $\det A^{-1} = 1/\det A$, $\det(cA) = c^n\det A$, and a triangular matrix has the product of its diagonal as determinant.
- Compute it by elimination, $\det A = (-1)^s \prod_i u_{ii}$, and use the log-determinant, a sum of log-pivots, whenever it enters a likelihood.
- $Av = \lambda v$: eigenvectors are directions mapped onto themselves; eigenvalues are the roots of $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ and can be complex.
- $\operatorname{tr}A = \sum_i\lambda_i$ and $\det A = \prod_i\lambda_i$.
- With $n$ independent eigenvectors, $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$ and $A^k = V\Lambda^kV^{-1}$; the largest $\lvert\lambda\rvert$ dominates, and $\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert$ sets the speed. A Markov chain's distance to stationarity shrinks like $\lvert\lambda_2\rvert^t$.
- Distinct eigenvalues guarantee diagonalizability; a shear such as $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ is not diagonalizable, and its powers grow although $\lvert\lambda\rvert = 1$.

## Code

The note's examples checked with NumPy: the determinant by elimination against the library, the log-determinant that does not underflow, the eigenvalues, trace and determinant identities, matrix powers through diagonalization, the weather chain, and the shear. It runs in well under a second; exact digits in the last places can differ across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np

# 1. 2x2 determinants: area 10, a mirror with -3, a flattened square with 0
A = np.array([[4.0, 1.0], [2.0, 3.0]])
C = np.array([[1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 1.0]])
D = np.array([[2.0, 1.0], [4.0, 2.0]])
print([round(float(np.linalg.det(m)), 6) for m in (A, C, D)])
# [10.0, -3.0, 0.0]

# 2. the determinant from the pivots of elimination with partial pivoting: (-1)^swaps times their product
def det_by_elimination(M):
    U = M.astype(float).copy(); n = len(U); sign = 1.0
    for k in range(n):
        p = k + np.argmax(np.abs(U[k:, k]))          # the largest pivot candidate, for stability
        if U[p, k] == 0: return 0.0
        if p != k: U[[k, p]] = U[[p, k]]; sign = -sign
        U[k + 1:] -= np.outer(U[k + 1:, k] / U[k, k], U[k])
    return sign * np.prod(np.diag(U))
M = np.array([[2.0, 1.0, 1.0], [4.0, 3.0, 3.0], [8.0, 7.0, 9.0]])
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
R = rng.normal(size=(6, 6))
print(round(det_by_elimination(M), 6), round(det_by_elimination(R), 6), round(float(np.linalg.det(R)), 6))
# 4.0 -2.340511 -2.340511
# (the hand elimination of the note used no swaps; partial pivoting swaps rows but the determinant is the same)

# 3. properties: products, transposes, scaling, inverses
B = rng.normal(size=(6, 6))
print(np.isclose(np.linalg.det(R @ B), np.linalg.det(R) * np.linalg.det(B)),
      np.isclose(np.linalg.det(R.T), np.linalg.det(R)),
      np.isclose(np.linalg.det(2 * R), 2**6 * np.linalg.det(R)),
      np.isclose(np.linalg.det(np.linalg.inv(R)), 1 / np.linalg.det(R)))
# True True True True

# 4. the determinant underflows, the log-determinant does not
S = 0.1 * np.eye(1000)
sign, logdet = np.linalg.slogdet(S)
print(np.linalg.det(S), float(sign), round(float(logdet), 4))
# 0.0 1.0 -2302.5851

# 5. eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A; trace = sum and det = product, also for a random matrix
lam, V = np.linalg.eig(A)
lam, V = lam.real, V.real                            # real here; NumPy may return a complex type
print(lam, (V / V[0]).round(6).T.tolist())          # eigenvectors scaled so their first entry is 1
print(np.isclose(lam.sum(), np.trace(A)), np.isclose(lam.prod(), np.linalg.det(A)))
lr = np.linalg.eigvals(R)                            # complex in general, in conjugate pairs
print(np.isclose(lr.sum(), np.trace(R)), np.isclose(lr.prod(), np.linalg.det(R)), np.iscomplexobj(lr))
# [5. 2.] [[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, -2.0]]
# True True
# True True True

# 6. powers through diagonalization: A^k x = V Lambda^k V^-1 x, and the baseline A^3 x = (86, 78)
x = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
c = np.linalg.solve(V, x)
for k in (3, 10):
    print(k, np.linalg.matrix_power(A, k) @ x, (V @ (lam**k * c)).round(6))
print((np.linalg.solve(np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, -2.0]]), x)).round(6))   # coordinates in the basis (1, 1), (1, -2)
# 3 [86. 78.] [86. 78.]
# 10 [6510758. 6509734.] [6510758. 6509734.]
# [0.666667 0.333333]

# 7. the weather chain: eigenvalues, the stationary distribution, and convergence like 0.473^t
P = np.array([[0.7, 0.2, 0.1], [0.3, 0.4, 0.3], [0.2, 0.3, 0.5]])
mu, W = np.linalg.eig(P.T)                           # left eigenvectors of P
order = np.argsort(-np.abs(mu)); mu, W = mu[order].real, W[:, order].real
pi = W[:, 0] / W[:, 0].sum()
print(mu.round(4), (0.3 + np.sqrt(0.03)).round(4), round(float(np.linalg.det(P)), 6), (pi * 46).round(6))
p, dist = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0]), []
for t in range(21):
    dist.append(np.abs(p - pi).sum()); p = p @ P
print([f'{dist[t]:.3g}' for t in (0, 1, 2, 10, 20)], round(dist[20] / dist[19], 4))
# [1.     0.4732 0.1268] 0.4732 0.06 [21. 13. 12.]
# ['1.09', '0.487', '0.227', '0.000567', '3.19e-07'] 0.4732

# 8. the shear: one eigenvector for a double eigenvalue, and powers that grow although |lambda| = 1
J = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [0.0, 1.0]])
print(np.linalg.eigvals(J).real, np.linalg.matrix_rank(J - np.eye(2)), np.linalg.matrix_power(J, 50).tolist())
eps = 1e-8
lam_e, V_e = np.linalg.eig(np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [0.0, 1.0 + eps]]))
print(lam_e.real, f'{np.linalg.cond(V_e):.2g}')          # nearly parallel eigenvectors: V is nearly singular
# [1. 1.] 1 [[1.0, 50.0], [0.0, 1.0]]
# [1.         1.00000001] 2e+08
```
