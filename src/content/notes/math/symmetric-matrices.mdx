The [previous note](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues) found the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a square matrix and used them to take powers. It also found the trouble with general matrices: eigenvectors that are not orthogonal, eigenvalues that are complex, and matrices with too few eigenvectors to diagonalize. The matrices that machine learning builds most often have none of these problems, because they are **symmetric**: a covariance matrix, the Gram matrix $X^\top X$ of least squares, a kernel matrix, and the Hessian of every twice differentiable loss. This note proves the spectral theorem, which gives every symmetric matrix real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. Then it uses that basis to read the shape of a quadratic function, to decide when a matrix is positive definite and to factor it by Cholesky, and to find the directions in which a quadratic grows fastest and slowest.

**Notation.** $A = A^\top \in \mathbb R^{n \times n}$ is symmetric. $Q$ is an orthogonal matrix ($Q^\top Q = I$) with columns $q_1, \dots, q_n$, and $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$. The **quadratic form** of $A$ is $q(x) = x^\top Ax$, and the **Rayleigh quotient** is $R(x) = x^\top Ax / x^\top x$ for $x \ne 0$. $A \succ 0$ means positive definite and $A \succeq 0$ positive semidefinite. $L$ is a lower triangular matrix. Complex vectors appear in one proof, with $\bar{x}$ the entrywise complex conjugate.

## Baseline: what shape is a quadratic function?

Consider two quadratic functions of two variables:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_A(x) &= 5x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2, \\
q_B(x) &= x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + x_2^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

All their coefficients are positive, and they look alike. Yet $q_A$ is positive for every $x \ne 0$, a bowl with its minimum at the origin, while $q_B(1, -1) = 1 - 4 + 1 = -2$, so $q_B$ is a saddle that goes down in some directions. The cross term $x_1x_2$ hides the shape: it couples the coordinates, and neither the signs of the coefficients nor their sizes say in which directions the function is steep or flat. These questions matter whenever a loss is quadratic or nearly so, which near a minimum every smooth loss is. Is the stationary point a minimum? In which direction does the loss rise fastest, and how much faster than in the slowest direction? Is a matrix a valid covariance? The answer to all of them is a rotation that removes the cross term, given by the eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix behind the quadratic.

## The spectral theorem

Every quadratic function can be written with a symmetric matrix:

$$
q_A(x) = x^\top Ax, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix},
$$

with the cross coefficient 4 split equally between the two off-diagonal entries. For symmetric matrices the eigen-decomposition of the previous note always exists and has the best possible form.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> A symmetric matrix is a pure stretch along $n$ perpendicular axes. Its eigenvalues are real, its eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal, and $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$: rotate into the eigen-axes, scale axis $i$ by $\lambda_i$, rotate back. In those axes a quadratic form loses its cross terms, $x^\top Ax = \sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2$, and everything about its shape is read off the signs and sizes of the eigenvalues.</aside>

**Theorem (spectral theorem).** For a symmetric $A \in \mathbb R^{n \times n}$ there is an orthogonal $Q$ and a real diagonal $\Lambda$ such that the following holds.

$$
\boxed{\;A = Q\Lambda Q^\top = \lambda_1q_1q_1^\top + \lambda_2q_2q_2^\top + \dots + \lambda_nq_nq_n^\top\;}
$$

The idea of the proof: symmetry lets a matrix move from one side of an inner product to the other, $(Ax)^\top y = x^\top Ay$. Applied to an eigenvector and its conjugate it forces the eigenvalue to be real; applied to two eigenvectors it forces them to be orthogonal. The two parts follow, and the full statement, which also covers repeated eigenvalues, is proved below them.

**Real eigenvalues.** Let $Ax = \lambda x$ with $x \in \mathbb C^n$, $x \ne 0$, where $\lambda$ may a priori be complex. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar{x}^\top Ax &= \lambda\, \bar{x}^\top x , \\
\overline{\bar{x}^\top Ax} &= x^\top A\bar{x} = (x^\top A\bar{x})^\top = \bar{x}^\top A^\top x = \bar{x}^\top Ax , \\
\bar{x}^\top x &= \lvert x_1\rvert^2 + \dots + \lvert x_n\rvert^2 \gt 0 , \\
\lambda &= \frac{\bar{x}^\top Ax}{\bar{x}^\top x} \in \mathbb R .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line multiplies $Ax = \lambda x$ by $\bar{x}^\top$ on the left. The second conjugates the number $\bar{x}^\top Ax$: $A$ is real, so conjugation only swaps $x$ and $\bar{x}$; a number equals its own transpose; and $A^\top = A$. A number equal to its conjugate is real. The third writes out $\bar{x}^\top x$, a sum of squared absolute values, positive because $x \ne 0$. The fourth divides the first line by it: a real number divided by a positive real number. With $\lambda$ real, $A - \lambda I$ is a real singular matrix, so the eigenvector can be chosen real as well.

**Orthogonal eigenvectors.** Let $Aq_1 = \lambda_1q_1$ and $Aq_2 = \lambda_2q_2$ with $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_1\, q_1^\top q_2 &= (Aq_1)^\top q_2 = q_1^\top A^\top q_2 = q_1^\top Aq_2 = \lambda_2\, q_1^\top q_2 , \\
(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\, q_1^\top q_2 &= 0 , \\
q_1^\top q_2 &= 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line replaces $\lambda_1q_1$ by $Aq_1$, transposes the product, uses $A^\top = A$, and replaces $Aq_2$ by $\lambda_2q_2$. The second subtracts the right end from the left end. The third divides by $\lambda_1 - \lambda_2 \ne 0$.

<details>
<summary>Proof of the full theorem, including repeated eigenvalues</summary>

By induction on $n$; for $n = 1$ there is nothing to prove. The characteristic polynomial has a root, which is real by the first part, so there is a real unit eigenvector $q_1$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_1$. Let $W$ be the subspace of vectors orthogonal to $q_1$, of dimension $n - 1$. $A$ maps $W$ into itself: for $w \in W$, $q_1^\top Aw = (Aq_1)^\top w = \lambda_1q_1^\top w = 0$. Choose an orthonormal basis of $W$ and put it into the columns of $U \in \mathbb R^{n \times (n-1)}$. The matrix $B = U^\top AU$ is symmetric and of size $n - 1$, so by the induction hypothesis $B = P\Lambda' P^\top$ with $P$ orthogonal. The columns of $UP$ are orthonormal, lie in $W$, and are eigenvectors of $A$: $A UP = U(U^\top AU)P = UBP = UP\Lambda'$, where $AU = UU^\top AU$ because the columns of $AU$ lie in $W$ and $UU^\top$ is the orthogonal projection onto $W$. Together with $q_1$ they form an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $A$. A repeated eigenvalue therefore never lacks eigenvectors: its eigenvectors form a subspace whose dimension equals the multiplicity, and any orthonormal basis of that subspace can be used ([Axler, 2024](#references), the real spectral theorem).

</details>

Why $Q\Lambda Q^\top$ is the same as the sum of rank-one terms: $Q\Lambda$ has columns $\lambda_iq_i$, and a product of a matrix with columns $a_i$ and a matrix with rows $b_i^\top$ is $\sum_i a_ib_i^\top$. Each term $q_iq_i^\top$ is the orthogonal projection onto the line through $q_i$ ([projection onto a line](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality#projection-onto-a-line)), so $A$ projects $x$ onto each eigen-axis, scales the projection by $\lambda_i$ and adds the results.

**Worked example.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, the trace is 7 and the determinant 6, so the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = (\lambda - 6)(\lambda - 1)$ and $\lambda_1 = 6$, $\lambda_2 = 1$. The null space of $A - 6I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -4 \end{pmatrix}$ is spanned by $(2, 1)$, and that of $A - I = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ by $(1, -2)$. Their inner product is $2 - 2 = 0$, as the theorem promises, and after normalization $q_1 = (2, 1)/\sqrt5$ and $q_2 = (1, -2)/\sqrt5$. Check the rank-one sum:

$$
6 \cdot \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + 1 \cdot \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 25 & 10 \\ 10 & 10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Orthonormality makes the eigen-coordinates free to compute. For the non-symmetric matrix of the previous note, the coordinates of $x$ in the eigenvector basis needed the solution of a linear system, $c = V^{-1}x$. Here $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$, so the coordinates are inner products, $y_i = q_i^\top x$, and lengths are kept, $\lVert y\rVert = \lVert x\rVert$. Functions of the matrix act on the eigenvalues alone: $A^k = Q\Lambda^kQ^\top$, $A^{-1} = Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^\top$ when no $\lambda_i$ is zero, and $A^{1/2} = Q\Lambda^{1/2}Q^\top$ when none is negative. Whitening a Gaussian with $\Sigma^{-1/2}$, as in the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps), is the last of these.

## Quadratic forms and their level sets

Only the symmetric part of a matrix enters its quadratic form. A number equals its transpose, so $x^\top Mx = x^\top M^\top x$, and averaging the two gives $x^\top Mx = x^\top\tfrac12(M + M^\top)x$. From here on the matrix of a quadratic form is symmetric.

The idea: express $x$ in the orthonormal eigenvector basis. In those coordinates $y = Q^\top x$ the matrix is diagonal, and the quadratic form is a weighted sum of squares without cross terms.

$$
\boxed{\;q(x) = x^\top Ax = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 + \dots + \lambda_n y_n^2, \qquad y = Q^\top x\;}
$$

Why it holds:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^\top Ax &= x^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top x \\
&= (Q^\top x)^\top\Lambda(Q^\top x) \\
&= y^\top\Lambda y \\
&= \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line substitutes the spectral decomposition. The second uses $x^\top Q = (Q^\top x)^\top$. The third names $y = Q^\top x$. The fourth multiplies out with the diagonal $\Lambda$: only the terms $i = j$ survive.

**Level sets.** If all $\lambda_i \gt 0$ and $c \gt 0$, the set where $q(x) = c$ is

$$
\frac{y_1^2}{c/\lambda_1} + \dots + \frac{y_n^2}{c/\lambda_n} = 1 ,
$$

an ellipse in two dimensions and an ellipsoid in more. Its axes point along the eigenvectors $q_i$, and the half-length along $q_i$ is $\sqrt{c/\lambda_i}$. A large eigenvalue means a steep direction and a short axis: the function reaches the level $c$ after a short distance. If some eigenvalues are negative, the level sets are hyperbolas or their higher-dimensional relatives, and the origin is a saddle.

**Worked example.** For $q_A$, in the coordinates $y_1 = (2x_1 + x_2)/\sqrt5$ and $y_2 = (x_1 - 2x_2)/\sqrt5$ the function is $6y_1^2 + y_2^2$. The level set $q_A = 1$ is an ellipse with half-length $1/\sqrt6 = 0.408$ along $(2, 1)/\sqrt5$ and $1$ along $(1, -2)/\sqrt5$; the level set $q_A = 4$ is the same ellipse scaled by 2. The point $0.408\,q_1 = (0.365, 0.183)$ gives $5 \cdot 0.133 + 4 \cdot 0.0667 + 2 \cdot 0.0333 = 1.00$, as it should. For $q_B$, with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the eigenvalues are 3 along $(1, 1)/\sqrt2$ and $-1$ along $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$, so $q_B = 3y_1^2 - y_2^2$: up along one diagonal, down along the other, the saddle of the baseline.

**Where the shape matters.** Every quadratic loss has these level sets around its minimum. The function $f(x) = \tfrac12x^\top Ax - b^\top x$ with $A \succ 0$ has gradient $Ax - b$, which vanishes at $x^* = A^{-1}b$, and expanding around it gives $f(x) = f(x^*) + \tfrac12(x - x^*)^\top A(x - x^*)$: ellipses centred at the minimum, shaped by the eigenvalues of $A$. Least squares is this function with $A = X^\top X$ ([the normal equations](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations)). Near a minimum every smooth loss looks like this, with $A$ its Hessian, by the second-order Taylor expansion of the [gradients note](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians). When the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue is large, the ellipses are long and thin, and [gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case) zigzags across the narrow valley. The density of a Gaussian has the same level sets with the matrix $\Sigma^{-1}$, whose eigenvalues are $1/\lambda_i(\Sigma)$; this is why the half-axes of a [covariance ellipse](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#covariance-as-geometry) are $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ rather than $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

## Positive definite and semidefinite matrices

A symmetric matrix $A$ is **positive definite**, $A \succ 0$, if $x^\top Ax \gt 0$ for every $x \ne 0$, and **positive semidefinite**, $A \succeq 0$, if $x^\top Ax \ge 0$ for every $x$. Geometrically, a positive definite form is a bowl with ellipsoidal level sets, a semidefinite one may have flat directions (a trough), and an **indefinite** one takes both signs (a saddle).

The idea of the tests: by the boxed formula of the last section, the sign of $x^\top Ax$ for all $x$ is the sign of $\sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2$ for all $y$, and that is decided by the signs of the eigenvalues. Several other tests are equivalent and cheaper. For a symmetric $A$ they are the following.

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
A \succ 0 &\iff \text{all } \lambda_i \gt 0 \\
&\iff \text{all pivots of elimination} \gt 0 \\
&\iff A = LL^\top \text{ with } l_{ii} \gt 0 \\
&\iff A = B^\top B \text{ for some } B \text{ with independent columns}
\end{aligned}}
$$

The semidefinite case is the same with the strict inequalities relaxed: $A \succeq 0$ exactly when all $\lambda_i \ge 0$, equivalently when $A = B^\top B$ for some matrix $B$. Why the eigenvalue test holds:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^\top Ax \gt 0 \text{ for all } x \ne 0
&\iff \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2 \gt 0 \text{ for all } y \ne 0 \\
&\iff \lambda_i \gt 0 \text{ for every } i .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first step uses $x^\top Ax = \sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2$ with $y = Q^\top x$, and that $x \mapsto Q^\top x$ is a bijection that maps nonzero vectors to nonzero vectors. The second: if every $\lambda_i \gt 0$, the sum is positive as soon as one $y_i$ is nonzero; conversely, $y = e_i$ gives the sum $\lambda_i$, which must then be positive.

The other tests, each with its reason:

- **$B^\top B$.** $x^\top B^\top Bx = \lVert Bx\rVert^2 \ge 0$, with equality only when $Bx = 0$, which for independent columns means $x = 0$. Conversely, $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top = B^\top B$ with $B = \Lambda^{1/2}Q^\top$ when no eigenvalue is negative.
- **Pivots and Cholesky.** Elimination on a positive definite matrix never needs a row swap, and the pivots are positive. The Cholesky factor of the next subsection carries the square roots of the pivots on its diagonal, so it exists exactly when the pivots are positive.
- **Leading minors (Sylvester's criterion).** The $k$-th leading principal minor, the determinant of the top-left $k \times k$ block, is the product of the first $k$ pivots. So $A \succ 0$ exactly when all $n$ leading minors are positive.

**Worked example.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$: the leading minors are 5 and $\det A = 6$; the pivots are 5 and $2 - \tfrac{2}{5}\cdot 2 = \tfrac65$, whose product is again 6; the eigenvalues are 6 and 1. Every test says positive definite. For $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ the minors are 1 and $-3$ and the eigenvalues 3 and $-1$: indefinite, as $q_B(1, -1) = -2$ showed. A warning about semidefiniteness: for $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ both leading minors are 0, so none is negative, and still $q(0, 1) = -1$. For $A \succeq 0$ the leading minors are not enough; all principal minors, from every subset of rows and the same columns, must be non-negative, or one checks the eigenvalues.

### Why covariance and kernel matrices are positive semidefinite

**Covariance matrices.** For a random vector $Z$ with mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma = E[(Z - \mu)(Z - \mu)^\top]$, and any fixed $a$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^\top\Sigma a &= a^\top E\big[(Z - \mu)(Z - \mu)^\top\big]a \\
&= E\big[a^\top(Z - \mu)(Z - \mu)^\top a\big] \\
&= E\big[(a^\top(Z - \mu))^2\big] = \operatorname{Var}(a^\top Z) \ge 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the definition of $\Sigma$, the second moves the constant vectors inside the expectation by linearity, and the third recognizes the product of a number with itself: the variance of the projection $a^\top Z$, never negative. The covariance is singular exactly when some combination $a^\top Z$ has variance zero, that is, when the data lie in a hyperplane. The sample covariance $S = \frac1n X^\top X$ of centred data is of the form $B^\top B$, so it is positive semidefinite, and it is singular whenever there are no more data points than dimensions, $n \le d$: centring leaves at most $n - 1$ independent rows. PCA in high dimension works with such singular matrices ([dimensionality reduction](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#pca-as-variance-maximization)).

**Gram and kernel matrices.** A kernel $k(x, z) = \langle\phi(x), \phi(z)\rangle$ evaluated on points $x_1, \dots, x_m$ gives $K = \Phi\Phi^\top$, where $\Phi$ has the feature vectors $\phi(x_i)^\top$ as rows. That is again $B^\top B$ with $B = \Phi^\top$, so $c^\top Kc = \lVert\sum_i c_i\phi(x_i)\rVert^2 \ge 0$. The [kernels note](/notes/ml/kernels#which-functions-are-kernels) uses the converse: a function whose matrices on every finite set of points are positive semidefinite is a valid kernel, and one that produces a negative eigenvalue is not.

**Hessians.** The Hessian of least squares, $2X^\top X$, and that of logistic regression, $X^\top SX$ with a diagonal $S$ of positive weights ([the gradient and Hessian](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs)), are of the form $B^\top B$. A loss with a positive semidefinite Hessian everywhere is convex, the subject of the [convexity note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions).

**Adding a multiple of the identity.** $A + \lambda I$ has the same eigenvectors as $A$ and the eigenvalues $\lambda_i + \lambda$. Ridge regression ($X^\top X + \lambda I$), the noise term of a Gaussian process ($K + \sigma_n^2I$) and the small "jitter" added before a Cholesky factorization all use this to turn a semidefinite matrix into a definite one.

### The Cholesky factorization

The idea: a positive definite matrix has a triangular square root. Matching the entries of $LL^\top$ to those of $A$ one column at a time gives each entry of $L$ from entries already computed, at half the cost of an LU factorization, about $\tfrac13 n^3$ operations, and without any row swaps.

$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
A &= LL^\top, \\
l_{jj} &= \sqrt{a_{jj} - \textstyle\sum_{k \lt j} l_{jk}^2}, \\
l_{ij} &= \Big(a_{ij} - \textstyle\sum_{k \lt j} l_{ik}l_{jk}\Big)\big/\, l_{jj} \quad (i \gt j)
\end{aligned}}
$$

Why it holds. For $i \ge j$, since $L$ is lower triangular ($l_{jk} = 0$ for $k \gt j$):

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{ij} = (LL^\top)_{ij} &= \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} l_{ik}l_{jk} = \sum_{k=1}^{j} l_{ik}l_{jk} \\
&= \textstyle\sum_{k \lt j} l_{ik}l_{jk} + l_{ij}l_{jj} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line writes the entry of a product and drops the terms with $k \gt j$, which contain $l_{jk} = 0$. The second splits off the last term. For $i = j$ the last term is $l_{jj}^2$, which gives the formula for $l_{jj}$; for $i \gt j$, dividing by $l_{jj}$ gives $l_{ij}$. Working column by column, everything on the right is already known. The quantity under the square root is the $j$-th pivot of elimination, positive exactly when $A \succ 0$; for any other symmetric matrix the algorithm meets a zero or negative number and stops, which makes it the cheapest numerical test of positive definiteness.

**Worked example.** For

$$
K = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix},
$$

column 1: $l_{11} = \sqrt4 = 2$, $l_{21} = 2/2 = 1$, $l_{31} = 2/2 = 1$. Column 2: $l_{22} = \sqrt{5 - 1^2} = 2$, $l_{32} = (3 - 1 \cdot 1)/2 = 1$. Column 3: $l_{33} = \sqrt{6 - 1^2 - 1^2} = 2$. So

$$
L = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det K = (\det L)^2 = (2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2)^2 = 64, \qquad \log\det K = 2\textstyle\sum_j \log l_{jj} = 6\log 2 = 4.159 .
$$

**Where machine learning uses it.** Three operations with a covariance or kernel matrix go through its Cholesky factor:

- **Sampling.** If $z \sim N(0, I)$, then $\mu + Lz \sim N(\mu, LL^\top)$, the recipe of the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps).
- **Solving and the log-determinant.** $K\alpha = y$ is solved by two triangular systems, $Lu = y$ and then $L^\top\alpha = u$, each in $n^2$ operations, and $\log\det K = 2\sum_j\log l_{jj}$ comes for free. This is how [Gaussian process regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#regression-the-posterior-in-closed-form) computes its posterior and its marginal likelihood, never forming $K^{-1}$.
- **Jitter.** A kernel matrix is positive definite in exact arithmetic but can be numerically singular. For the RBF kernel $\exp(-(x - z)^2/(2 \cdot 0.5^2))$ on 50 equally spaced points of $[0, 1]$ the largest eigenvalue is 38.3, and the smallest one, computed in double precision, comes out as $-9 \cdot 10^{-15}$: the true value is lost in rounding. Cholesky fails; adding $10^{-10}I$ makes it succeed.

## The Rayleigh quotient: the extreme directions

How large can $x^\top Ax$ be for a vector of length 1? This is the question of the steepest direction of a quadratic, of the direction of largest variance in PCA, and of the largest stretch of a matrix. The idea: in eigen-coordinates the quotient $x^\top Ax / x^\top x$ is a weighted average of the eigenvalues, and an average lies between its smallest and largest term. Equality holds on the left at an eigenvector of $\lambda_{\min}$ and on the right at an eigenvector of $\lambda_{\max}$.

$$
\boxed{\;\lambda_{\min} \le R(x) = \frac{x^\top Ax}{x^\top x} \le \lambda_{\max}\;}
$$

Why it holds:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R(x) &= \frac{\textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2}{y^\top y} \\
&= \textstyle\sum_i w_i\lambda_i, \qquad w_i = \dfrac{y_i^2}{\sum_j y_j^2} \ge 0, \quad \sum_i w_i = 1 , \\
\lambda_{\min} = \textstyle\sum_i w_i\lambda_{\min} &\le \textstyle\sum_i w_i\lambda_i \le \sum_i w_i\lambda_{\max} = \lambda_{\max} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line uses the boxed quadratic-form formula in the numerator and $x^\top x = y^\top Q^\top Qy = y^\top y$ in the denominator. The second distributes the denominator over the terms; the weights are squares divided by their sum. The third replaces every $\lambda_i$ by the smallest and by the largest eigenvalue, which can only decrease or increase a sum with non-negative weights, and uses $\sum_i w_i = 1$. At $x = q_1$ all the weight is on $\lambda_1$, so the bound is reached.

**Worked example.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $x = (1, 1)$: $R = (5 + 4 + 2)/2 = 5.5$. In eigen-coordinates $y = (3, -1)/\sqrt5$, so the weights are $\tfrac{9/5}{2} = 0.9$ and $\tfrac{1/5}{2} = 0.1$, and $0.9 \cdot 6 + 0.1 \cdot 1 = 5.5$. On the unit circle $x = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ the quotient is

$$
R(\theta) = 5\cos^2\theta + 4\cos\theta\sin\theta + 2\sin^2\theta = 3.5 + 1.5\cos 2\theta + 2\sin 2\theta ,
$$

which oscillates between 1 and 6 with period $\pi$, and peaks at $\theta = 26.57°$, the direction of $q_1 = (2, 1)/\sqrt5$:

**Where it is used.**

- **PCA.** The first principal component maximizes the variance $w^\top Sw$ over unit vectors $w$, a Rayleigh quotient of the covariance, so it is the top eigenvector ([PCA as variance maximization](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#pca-as-variance-maximization)).
- **The largest stretch of a matrix.** For any matrix $B$, $\lVert Bx\rVert^2/\lVert x\rVert^2 = x^\top B^\top Bx/x^\top x$, so the largest factor by which $B$ stretches a vector is $\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(B^\top B)}$, the spectral norm. The [next note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) builds the singular value decomposition on exactly this observation.
- **Curvature and step sizes.** Along a unit direction $u$, the second derivative of a function with Hessian $H$ is $u^\top Hu$, which lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalue of $H$. Gradient descent with step $\eta$ is stable only if $\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}(H)$, and the ratio $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$, the condition number, sets its speed ([gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case)).

### Computing the largest eigenvalue: power iteration

The previous note showed that $A^kx$ turns towards the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue in absolute value. **Power iteration** uses this: start from a random $x_0$, repeat $x_{k+1} = Ax_k / \lVert Ax_k\rVert$, and read off the eigenvalue as $R(x_k)$. The direction error shrinks by $\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert$ per step. The Rayleigh quotient converges faster, by $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^2$ per step for a symmetric matrix, because it is flat at its maximum: an error $\varepsilon$ in the direction changes $R$ only by order $\varepsilon^2$. For our $A$, from $x_0 = (1, 0)$, the quotients are $5$, $5.966$, $5.9990$ and $5.99997$: the error falls from 1 to $0.034$, $9.6 \cdot 10^{-4}$ and $2.7 \cdot 10^{-5}$, about $1/36 = (1/6)^2$ per step. Each step costs one matrix–vector product, which is why power iteration and its relatives are used for very large or implicitly given matrices: the spectral norm of a weight matrix in spectral normalization of GANs, PageRank, and the top eigenvalues of a network's Hessian.

### Intermediate eigenvalues

*Optional depth: the min–max characterization of every eigenvalue. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

With eigenvalues sorted $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n$, the **Courant–Fischer theorem** says that the $k$-th one is the best worst-case Rayleigh quotient over $k$-dimensional subspaces:

$$
\lambda_k = \max_{\dim S = k}\ \min_{x \in S,\ x \ne 0} R(x) .
$$

The maximum is attained by the span of $q_1, \dots, q_k$: on it, $R$ is a weighted average of $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$, so its minimum is $\lambda_k$. No other $k$-dimensional subspace does better, because it must contain a unit vector orthogonal to $q_1, \dots, q_{k-1}$ (a subspace of dimension $k$ and one of dimension $n - k + 1$ intersect), and on such a vector $R$ is an average of $\lambda_k, \dots, \lambda_n$, at most $\lambda_k$. Two consequences used in machine learning: the $k$ leading eigenvectors span the $k$-dimensional subspace that keeps the most variance, which is PCA with $k$ components; and eigenvalues of symmetric matrices move by at most the size of a perturbation, $\lvert\lambda_k(A + E) - \lambda_k(A)\rvert \le \lVert E\rVert_2$ (Weyl's inequality), so the spectrum of a sample covariance is a stable estimate when the sample is large ([Horn and Johnson, 2013](#references), sections 4.2 and 4.3).

## What changed

| | A general square matrix | A symmetric matrix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eigenvalues | possibly complex | real |
| Eigenvectors | possibly too few, not orthogonal | an orthonormal basis, $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ |
| Coordinates in the eigenbasis | solve $Vc = x$ | inner products $q_i^\top x$ |
| Quadratic form $x^\top Ax$ | cross terms hide the shape | $\sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2$: ellipsoids, troughs or saddles |
| Positive definiteness | not defined | all $\lambda_i \gt 0$; tested by Cholesky in $\tfrac13n^3$ operations |
| Extreme values on the unit sphere | not directly readable | $\lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_{\max}$, at the eigenvectors |

## Limitations

- **Symmetry must be exact.** A matrix that is symmetric only up to rounding, such as $X^\top X$ computed in floating point or a Hessian estimated by finite differences, may give tiny imaginary parts in a general eigenvalue routine. Symmetrize it, $\tfrac12(A + A^\top)$, and call a symmetric solver such as `numpy.linalg.eigh`.
- **Positive semidefiniteness is fragile numerically.** Matrices that are semidefinite in exact arithmetic come out with eigenvalues of order $-10^{-15}$ times the largest, and Cholesky fails on them. Jitter fixes the factorization, at the price of changing the matrix slightly.
- **Repeated eigenvalues do not have unique eigenvectors.** Only the eigenspace is determined: for $A = I$ every orthonormal basis is an eigenbasis. Algorithms that track individual eigenvectors, such as the components of PCA, become unstable when two eigenvalues are close.
- **The full decomposition costs $O(n^3)$.** For a kernel matrix on $10^5$ points that is out of reach; one computes a few extreme eigenpairs by iterative methods, or approximates the matrix, as in the [cost section](/notes/ml/kernels#the-cost-and-random-fourier-features) of the kernels note.

## What you should remember

- A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis: $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top = \sum_i\lambda_iq_iq_i^\top$.
- In eigen-coordinates $y = Q^\top x$, $x^\top Ax = \sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2$; for $A \succ 0$ the level set at height $c$ is an ellipsoid with half-axes $\sqrt{c/\lambda_i}$ along $q_i$.
- $A \succ 0$ iff all eigenvalues, pivots or leading minors are positive, iff $A = LL^\top$ (Cholesky), iff $A = B^\top B$ with independent columns. For $A \succeq 0$, leading minors are not enough.
- Covariance, Gram and kernel matrices, and the Hessians of least squares and logistic regression, are positive semidefinite because each is of the form $B^\top B$; adding $\lambda I$ makes them definite.
- Cholesky costs $\tfrac13n^3$, samples Gaussians as $\mu + Lz$, solves systems by two triangular solves, and gives $\log\det A = 2\sum_j\log l_{jj}$.
- $\lambda_{\min} \le x^\top Ax/x^\top x \le \lambda_{\max}$, with equality at the eigenvectors; power iteration finds the top eigenpair with one matrix–vector product per step.

## Code

The spectral theorem, the quadratic-form identity, the definiteness tests, Cholesky by the formula of the note against NumPy, the jitter example, the Rayleigh quotient and power iteration. It runs in well under a second; exact digits in the last places can differ across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np

# 1. the spectral theorem: real eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenvectors, A = Q Lambda Q^T = sum of rank-one terms
A = np.array([[5.0, 2.0], [2.0, 2.0]])
lam, Q = np.linalg.eigh(A)                      # eigh: for symmetric matrices, eigenvalues in ascending order
print(lam, np.allclose(Q.T @ Q, np.eye(2)), np.allclose(sum(l * np.outer(q, q) for l, q in zip(lam, Q.T)), A))
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
M = rng.normal(size=(6, 6)); S = M + M.T        # a random symmetric matrix
ls, Qs = np.linalg.eigh(S)
print(np.isrealobj(np.linalg.eigvals(S)) or np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(S).imag).max() < 1e-12,
      np.allclose(Qs.T @ Qs, np.eye(6)), np.allclose(Qs @ np.diag(ls) @ Qs.T, S))
# [1. 6.] True True
# True True True

# 2. the quadratic form in eigen-coordinates: x^T A x = sum lambda_i y_i^2 with y = Q^T x
x = rng.normal(size=2); y = Q.T @ x
print(np.isclose(x @ A @ x, (lam * y**2).sum()))
q1 = np.array([2.0, 1.0]) / np.sqrt(5)
print(round(float((q1 / np.sqrt(6)) @ A @ (q1 / np.sqrt(6))), 6), (q1 / np.sqrt(6)).round(3))   # the end of the short half-axis
# True
# 1.0 [0.365 0.183]

# 3. definiteness tests: eigenvalues, leading minors, Cholesky; and the semidefinite trap
def leading_minors(M): return [round(float(np.linalg.det(M[:k, :k])), 6) for k in range(1, len(M) + 1)]
def is_pd_by_cholesky(M):
    try: np.linalg.cholesky(M); return True
    except np.linalg.LinAlgError: return False
for name, M in [('A', A), ('B', np.array([[1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 1.0]])), ('trap', np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [0.0, -1.0]]))]:
    print(name, np.linalg.eigvalsh(M), leading_minors(M), is_pd_by_cholesky(M))
# A [1. 6.] [5.0, 6.0] True
# B [-1.  3.] [1.0, -3.0] False
# trap [-1.  0.] [0.0, 0.0] False

# 4. covariance and kernel matrices are positive semidefinite; X^T X is singular when n <= d
X = rng.normal(size=(5, 8)); Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)
ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Xc.T @ Xc / 5)
print(ev.min() > -1e-12, int((ev > 1e-10).sum()))   # 5 centred points in 8 dimensions: rank at most 4
pts = rng.normal(size=(30, 2))
K_rbf = np.exp(-((pts[:, None] - pts[None]) ** 2).sum(-1) / 2)
K_dist = np.sqrt(((pts[:, None] - pts[None]) ** 2).sum(-1))       # a distance is not a kernel
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(K_rbf).min() > -1e-12, round(float(np.linalg.eigvalsh(K_dist).min()), 2))
# True 4
# True -19.26

# 5. Cholesky by the formula of the note, column by column, against NumPy
def cholesky(A):
    n = len(A); L = np.zeros_like(A)
    for j in range(n):
        d = A[j, j] - L[j, :j] @ L[j, :j]
        if d <= 0: raise ValueError(f'not positive definite: pivot {j + 1} is {d:.3g}')
        L[j, j] = np.sqrt(d)
        for i in range(j + 1, n):
            L[i, j] = (A[i, j] - L[i, :j] @ L[j, :j]) / L[j, j]
    return L
K = np.array([[4.0, 2.0, 2.0], [2.0, 5.0, 3.0], [2.0, 3.0, 6.0]])
L = cholesky(K)
print(L.tolist(), np.allclose(L, np.linalg.cholesky(K)), round(2 * np.log(np.diag(L)).sum(), 4), round(float(np.log(np.linalg.det(K))), 4))
# [[2.0, 0.0, 0.0], [1.0, 2.0, 0.0], [1.0, 1.0, 2.0]] True 4.1589 4.1589

# 6. sampling with the Cholesky factor, and solving K alpha = y by two triangular solves
from scipy.linalg import solve_triangular
Z = rng.normal(size=(200_000, 3)) @ L.T
print(np.cov(Z.T).round(1).tolist())
yv = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
alpha = solve_triangular(L.T, solve_triangular(L, yv, lower=True), lower=False)
print(np.allclose(K @ alpha, yv))
# [[4.0, 2.0, 2.0], [2.0, 5.0, 3.0], [2.0, 3.0, 6.0]]
# True

# 7. jitter: an RBF kernel matrix that is numerically singular
t = np.linspace(0, 1, 50)
Kt = np.exp(-(t[:, None] - t[None]) ** 2 / (2 * 0.5**2))
e = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Kt)
print(f'{e.max():.1f} {e.min():.1g}', is_pd_by_cholesky(Kt), is_pd_by_cholesky(Kt + 1e-10 * np.eye(50)))
# 38.3 -9e-15 False True

# 8. the Rayleigh quotient on the unit circle, and power iteration
theta = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 100_001)
U = np.stack([np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)])
Rq = np.einsum('in,ij,jn->n', U, A, U)
print(round(Rq.max(), 6), round(np.degrees(theta[Rq.argmax()]), 1), round(Rq.min(), 6), round(np.degrees(theta[Rq.argmin()]), 1),
      round(np.degrees(np.arctan2(1, 2)), 2))   # the angle of q1 = (2, 1)/sqrt(5)
v = np.array([1.0, 0.0]); quot = []
for k in range(4):
    quot.append(v @ A @ v); v = A @ v; v /= np.linalg.norm(v)
print([round(float(r), 5) for r in quot], v.round(4))
# 6.0 26.6 1.0 116.6 26.57
# [5.0, 5.96552, 5.99904, 5.99997] [0.8946 0.4469]
```

## Next

Every symmetric matrix is a stretch along perpendicular axes. A general matrix, even a rectangular one that maps $\mathbb R^n$ to $\mathbb R^m$, is almost as simple: it maps one set of perpendicular axes onto another, stretching each by a factor. The [next note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) builds that decomposition, the SVD, from the eigendecomposition of the symmetric matrix $A^\top A$, and uses it to read off rank, to approximate a matrix by one of lower rank, and to solve least-squares problems that have no unique solution.
