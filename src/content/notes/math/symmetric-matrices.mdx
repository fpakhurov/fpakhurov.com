Here are two quadratic functions of two variables:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_A(x) &= 5x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2, \\
q_B(x) &= x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + x_2^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

All their coefficients are positive, and they look alike. Yet $q_A$ is positive for every $x \ne 0$, a bowl with its minimum at the origin, while $q_B(1, -1) = 1 - 4 + 1 = -2$: $q_B$ is a saddle that goes down in some directions. The cross term $x_1x_2$ couples the coordinates and hides the shape. Neither the signs of the coefficients nor their sizes say in which directions the function is steep or flat.

The same questions come up whenever a loss is quadratic or nearly so, and near a minimum every smooth loss is. Is a stationary point a minimum? In which direction does the loss rise fastest, and how much faster than in the slowest one? Is a given matrix a valid covariance? Each has one answer: rotate the coordinates until the cross term disappears. The rotation comes from eigenvectors, and the [previous note](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues) found that general matrices may have complex eigenvalues or too few eigenvectors. The matrices behind quadratic functions are symmetric, and for them nothing goes wrong.

## The spectral theorem

Every quadratic function can be written as $x^\top Ax$ with a **symmetric** matrix, $A = A^\top$. For $q_A$ the cross coefficient 4 is split equally between the two off-diagonal entries:

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Nothing is lost by insisting on symmetry. A number equals its transpose, so $x^\top Mx = x^\top M^\top x$ for any square $M$, and averaging the two gives $x^\top Mx = x^\top \tfrac12(M + M^\top)\,x$: only the symmetric part of a matrix enters its quadratic form. Covariance matrices, the Gram matrix $X^\top X$ of least squares, kernel matrices and the Hessian of every twice differentiable loss are symmetric too.

**Spectral theorem.** For a symmetric $A \in \mathbb R^{n \times n}$ there is an orthogonal matrix $Q$ ($Q^\top Q = I$) with columns $q_1, \dots, q_n$, and a real diagonal $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$, such that

$$
\boxed{\;A = Q\Lambda Q^\top = \lambda_1q_1q_1^\top + \dots + \lambda_nq_nq_n^\top .\;}
$$

Both halves of the proof use one property: a symmetric matrix moves from one side of an inner product to the other, $(Ax)^\top y = x^\top Ay$.

### Real eigenvalues

Let $Ax = \lambda x$ for a vector $x \in \mathbb C^n$, $x \ne 0$, and a number $\lambda$ that may a priori be complex; $\bar x$ is the entrywise complex conjugate. Look at the number $\bar x^\top Ax$ and take its conjugate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\overline{\bar x^\top Ax} &= x^\top A\bar x && A \text{ is real} \\
&= (x^\top A\bar x)^\top && \text{a number} \\
&= \bar x^\top A^\top x \\
&= \bar x^\top Ax && A^\top = A .
\end{aligned}
$$

A number equal to its conjugate is real. The same number is $\lambda\, \bar x^\top x$, and $\bar x^\top x = \lvert x_1\rvert^2 + \dots + \lvert x_n\rvert^2$ is positive, so

$$
\lambda = \frac{\bar x^\top Ax}{\bar x^\top x} \in \mathbb R .
$$

With $\lambda$ real, $A - \lambda I$ is a real singular matrix, and the eigenvector can be chosen real as well.

### Orthogonal eigenvectors

Let $Aq_1 = \lambda_1q_1$ and $Aq_2 = \lambda_2q_2$ with $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$. Move $A$ across the inner product:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_1\, q_1^\top q_2 &= (Aq_1)^\top q_2 \\
&= q_1^\top Aq_2 && A^\top = A \\
&= \lambda_2\, q_1^\top q_2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

So $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\, q_1^\top q_2 = 0$, and since $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$, the eigenvectors are orthogonal.

<details>
<summary>Proof of the full theorem, including repeated eigenvalues</summary>

By induction on $n$; for $n = 1$ there is nothing to prove. The characteristic polynomial has a root, which is real by the first part, so there is a real unit eigenvector $q_1$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_1$. Let $W$ be the subspace of vectors orthogonal to $q_1$, of dimension $n - 1$. $A$ maps $W$ into itself: for $w \in W$, $q_1^\top Aw = (Aq_1)^\top w = \lambda_1q_1^\top w = 0$.

Put an orthonormal basis of $W$ into the columns of $U \in \mathbb R^{n \times (n-1)}$. The matrix $B = U^\top AU$ is symmetric of size $n - 1$, so by the induction hypothesis $B = P\Lambda' P^\top$ with $P$ orthogonal. The columns of $UP$ are orthonormal, lie in $W$, and are eigenvectors of $A$: $AUP = U(U^\top AU)P = UBP = UP\Lambda'$, where $AU = UU^\top AU$ because the columns of $AU$ lie in $W$ and $UU^\top$ is the orthogonal projection onto $W$. Together with $q_1$ they form an orthonormal eigenbasis of $A$.

A repeated eigenvalue therefore never lacks eigenvectors: its eigenvectors form a subspace whose dimension equals the multiplicity, and any orthonormal basis of that subspace will do ([Axler, 2024](#references), the real spectral theorem).

</details>

The two forms of the theorem are one product written two ways. $Q\Lambda$ has the columns $\lambda_iq_i$, and a matrix with columns $a_i$ times a matrix with rows $b_i^\top$ is $\sum_i a_ib_i^\top$. Each $q_iq_i^\top$ is the orthogonal [projection onto the line](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality#projection-onto-a-line) through $q_i$. So $A$ projects $x$ onto each eigen-axis, scales the projection by $\lambda_i$ and adds the results.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> A symmetric matrix is a pure stretch along $n$ perpendicular axes: rotate into the eigenvectors, scale axis $i$ by $\lambda_i$, rotate back.</aside>

**Example.** For our $A$ the trace is 7 and the determinant 6, so the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = (\lambda - 6)(\lambda - 1)$. The null space of $A - 6I$ is spanned by $(2, 1)$, and that of $A - I$ by $(1, -2)$. Their inner product is $2 - 2 = 0$, as the theorem promises, and after normalization $q_1 = (2, 1)/\sqrt5$ and $q_2 = (1, -2)/\sqrt5$. The rank-one terms add up to $A$:

$$
6 \cdot \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + 1 \cdot \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Orthonormality makes the eigen-coordinates free. For the non-symmetric matrix of the previous note, the coordinates of $x$ in the eigenbasis needed a linear solve, $c = V^{-1}x$. Here $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$, so the coordinates are inner products, $y_i = q_i^\top x$, and lengths are kept, $\lVert y\rVert = \lVert x\rVert$.

Functions of the matrix act on the eigenvalues alone: $A^k = Q\Lambda^kQ^\top$, $A^{-1} = Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^\top$ when no $\lambda_i$ is zero, and $A^{1/2} = Q\Lambda^{1/2}Q^\top$ when none is negative. Whitening a Gaussian with $\Sigma^{-1/2}$, in the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps), is the last of these.

## Quadratic forms and their level sets

The **quadratic form** of $A$ is $q(x) = x^\top Ax$. In the eigen-coordinates $y = Q^\top x$ it loses its cross terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^\top Ax &= x^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top x \\
&= y^\top\Lambda y && y = Q^\top x \\
&= \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \dots + \lambda_n y_n^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Every question about the shape of $q$ is now a question about the signs and sizes of the $\lambda_i$.

If all $\lambda_i > 0$ and $c > 0$, the level set $q(x) = c$ is

$$
\frac{y_1^2}{c/\lambda_1} + \dots + \frac{y_n^2}{c/\lambda_n} = 1 ,
$$

an ellipse in two dimensions and an ellipsoid in more. Its axes point along the eigenvectors $q_i$, with half-length $\sqrt{c/\lambda_i}$ along $q_i$. A large eigenvalue means a steep direction and a short axis: the function reaches the level $c$ after a short distance. If some eigenvalues are negative, the level sets are hyperbolas or their higher-dimensional relatives, and the origin is a saddle.

**Example.** For $q_A$, in the coordinates $y_1 = (2x_1 + x_2)/\sqrt5$ and $y_2 = (x_1 - 2x_2)/\sqrt5$, the function is $6y_1^2 + y_2^2$. The level set $q_A = 1$ is an ellipse with half-length $1/\sqrt6 = 0.408$ along $q_1$ and $1$ along $q_2$; the level set $q_A = 4$ is the same ellipse scaled by 2. For $q_B$, with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the eigenvalues are 3 along $(1, 1)/\sqrt2$ and $-1$ along $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$. So $q_B = 3y_1^2 - y_2^2$: up along one diagonal, down along the other, the saddle of the opening.

<QuadraticForms />

Every quadratic loss has these level sets around its minimum. The function $f(x) = \tfrac12x^\top Ax - b^\top x$ with all $\lambda_i > 0$ has gradient $Ax - b$, which vanishes at $x^* = A^{-1}b$. Expanding around that point gives $f(x) = f(x^*) + \tfrac12(x - x^*)^\top A(x - x^*)$: ellipses centred at the minimum, shaped by the eigenvalues of $A$. Least squares is this function with $A = X^\top X$ ([the normal equations](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations)), and near a minimum every smooth loss looks like it, with $A$ its Hessian ([second-order Taylor](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians#the-hessian-and-the-second-order-taylor-expansion)).

When the largest eigenvalue is much larger than the smallest, the ellipses are long and thin, and [gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case) zigzags across the narrow valley. The density of a Gaussian has the same level sets with the matrix $\Sigma^{-1}$, whose eigenvalues are $1/\lambda_i(\Sigma)$. That is why the half-axes of a [covariance ellipse](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#covariance-as-geometry) are $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, where those of $x^\top \Sigma x = 1$ would be $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

## Positive definite and semidefinite matrices

A symmetric matrix $A$ is **positive definite**, $A \succ 0$, if $x^\top Ax > 0$ for every $x \ne 0$, and **positive semidefinite**, $A \succeq 0$, if $x^\top Ax \ge 0$ for every $x$. A positive definite form is a bowl with ellipsoidal level sets. A semidefinite one may have flat directions, a trough. An **indefinite** form takes both signs, a saddle.

The definition asks about infinitely many vectors $x$. Four finite tests are equivalent to it for a symmetric $A$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A \succ 0 &\iff \text{all eigenvalues } \lambda_i > 0 \\
&\iff \text{all pivots of elimination} > 0 \\
&\iff \text{all leading minors} > 0 \\
&\iff A = B^\top B,\ B \text{ with independent columns.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The eigenvalue test follows from the eigen-coordinates. $x^\top Ax = \sum_i \lambda_iy_i^2$ with $y = Q^\top x$, and $x \mapsto Q^\top x$ maps nonzero vectors to nonzero vectors. If every $\lambda_i > 0$, the sum is positive as soon as one $y_i$ is nonzero. Conversely, $x = q_i$ gives $y = e_i$ and the value $\lambda_i$, which must then be positive.

The factored form works in both directions. If $A = B^\top B$, then $x^\top Ax = \lVert Bx\rVert^2 \ge 0$, with equality only when $Bx = 0$, which independent columns allow only for $x = 0$. If all $\lambda_i > 0$, then $B = \Lambda^{1/2}Q^\top$ does the job.

Elimination on a positive definite matrix never needs a row swap, and its pivots are positive; the [Cholesky factorization](#the-cholesky-factorization) below carries their square roots on its diagonal. The $k$-th **leading minor**, the determinant of the top-left $k \times k$ block, is the product of the first $k$ pivots, so positive pivots and positive leading minors are the same condition (Sylvester's criterion).

### Which test for which question

The tests are equivalent, but each answers a different question cheaply ([Strang, 2018](#references), lecture 5).

- If $S \succ 0$ and $T \succ 0$, is $S + T$? The definition answers at once, $x^\top(S + T)x = x^\top Sx + x^\top Tx > 0$; the eigenvalues of a sum are not the sums of eigenvalues.
- Is $S^{-1}$? The eigenvalues answer: they are $1/\lambda_i > 0$.
- Is $Q^\top SQ$ for an orthogonal $Q$? The definition again, with $y = Qx$: $x^\top Q^\top SQx = y^\top Sy > 0$.
- Is a matrix of numbers positive definite? Attempt a Cholesky factorization, the cheapest test.

Two intuitions fail ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 1). Positive entries do not make a matrix positive definite: the matrix of $q_B$ has only positive entries and is a saddle. And a positive definite matrix may have negative entries: $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues 1 and 3.

### The two examples, and the boundary between them

| | $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| leading minors | $5$, $6$ | $1$, $-3$ |
| pivots | $5$, $6/5$ | $1$, $-3$ |
| eigenvalues | $6$, $1$ | $3$, $-1$ |
| verdict | positive definite | indefinite |

Every test agrees, and the pivots multiply to the minors: $5 \cdot 6/5 = 6$.

Semidefinite matrices sit on the boundary. Replace the corner entry 2 of $A$ by $c$. The second leading minor is $5c - 4$, so $c > 0.8$ gives a bowl and $c < 0.8$ a saddle. At $c = 0.8$ the eigenvalues are $5.8$ and $0$: a trough, flat along $(2, -5)$. In floating point the boundary blurs: NumPy's Cholesky accepts this matrix, because rounding leaves its second pivot at about $10^{-16}$ instead of 0.

For semidefiniteness the eigenvalue and $B^\top B$ tests carry over with $\ge$, and $B$ may have dependent columns. The leading minors do not. For $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ both leading minors are 0, none is negative, and still $q(0, 1) = -1$. A semidefinite test by minors needs all principal minors, from every subset of rows and the same columns; in practice one checks the eigenvalues.

### Why covariance and kernel matrices are positive semidefinite

A covariance matrix, $\Sigma = \mathbb E\big[(Z - \mu)(Z - \mu)^\top\big]$ for a random vector $Z$ with mean $\mu$, gives for any fixed vector $a$

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^\top\Sigma a &= \mathbb E\big[a^\top(Z - \mu)(Z - \mu)^\top a\big] && \text{linearity} \\
&= \mathbb E\big[(a^\top(Z - \mu))^2\big] \\
&= \operatorname{Var}(a^\top Z) \ge 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The quadratic form of a covariance is the variance of a projection. It is zero exactly when some combination $a^\top Z$ is constant, so $\Sigma$ is singular exactly when the data lie in a hyperplane.

The sample covariance $S = \frac1n X^\top X$ of centred data is of the form $B^\top B$. With no more points than dimensions, $n \le d$, it is singular, because centring leaves at most $n - 1$ independent rows; PCA in high dimension works with such matrices ([dimensionality reduction](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#pca-as-variance-maximization)).

A kernel $k(x, z) = \langle\phi(x), \phi(z)\rangle$ evaluated on points $x_1, \dots, x_m$ gives $K = \Phi\Phi^\top$, where $\Phi$ has the rows $\phi(x_i)^\top$. Then $c^\top Kc = \lVert\sum_i c_i\phi(x_i)\rVert^2 \ge 0$. The [kernels note](/notes/ml/kernels#which-functions-are-kernels) uses the converse: a function whose matrices on every finite set of points are positive semidefinite is a valid kernel. The Hessians of least squares, $2X^\top X$, and of [logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs), $X^\top SX$ with positive weights in the diagonal $S$, have the same form; a loss whose Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere is [convex](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions).

Adding a multiple of the identity turns any of these into a definite matrix: $A + \lambda I$ has the eigenvectors of $A$ and the eigenvalues $\lambda_i + \lambda$. Ridge regression ($X^\top X + \lambda I$), the noise term of a Gaussian process ($K + \sigma_n^2I$) and the small jitter added before a Cholesky factorization all do this.

## The Cholesky factorization

To sample from $\mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)$ or to solve $K\alpha = y$ for a kernel matrix, we need a square root of a positive definite matrix that is cheap to compute and to apply. The eigendecomposition gives one, $Q\Lambda^{1/2}$, at the price of an iterative algorithm. A triangular one is cheaper. Every $A \succ 0$ factors as $A = LL^\top$ with $L$ lower triangular and $l_{jj} > 0$.

To find $L$, write the entry $(i, j)$ of $LL^\top$ for $i \ge j$. Only $k \le j$ contribute, since $l_{jk} = 0$ for $k > j$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_{ij} &= \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} l_{ik}l_{jk} \\
&= \textstyle\sum_{k < j} l_{ik}l_{jk} + l_{ij}l_{jj} .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $i = j$ the last term is $l_{jj}^2$, and for $i > j$ we divide by $l_{jj}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
l_{jj} &= \sqrt{a_{jj} - \textstyle\sum_{k < j} l_{jk}^2}, \\
l_{ij} &= \Big(a_{ij} - \textstyle\sum_{k < j} l_{ik}l_{jk}\Big) \big/ l_{jj} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Column by column, everything on the right is already known. The quantity under the root is the $j$-th pivot of elimination, positive exactly when $A \succ 0$. On any other symmetric matrix the algorithm meets a zero or negative number and stops, which makes it the cheapest numerical test of definiteness. It costs about $\tfrac13 n^3$ operations, half of an LU factorization, and needs no row swaps ([Rakhuba, 2025](#references), lecture 12).

**Example.** For

$$
K = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}
$$

the three columns of $L$ come out as follows:

| column | computation | entries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $l_{11} = \sqrt4$, $l_{21} = 2/2$, $l_{31} = 2/2$ | $2, 1, 1$ |
| 2 | $l_{22} = \sqrt{5 - 1^2}$, $l_{32} = (3 - 1 \cdot 1)/2$ | $2, 1$ |
| 3 | $l_{33} = \sqrt{6 - 1^2 - 1^2}$ | $2$ |

So

$$
L = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix},
$$

and the determinant comes with it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det K &= (\det L)^2 = 8^2 = 64, \\
\log\det K &= 2\textstyle\sum_j \log l_{jj} = 6\log 2 = 4.159 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Sampling from a Gaussian goes through this factor: if $z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$, then $\mu + Lz \sim \mathcal N(\mu, LL^\top)$, the recipe of the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#affine-maps). So does solving with a kernel matrix: $K\alpha = y$ becomes two triangular systems, $Lu = y$ and $L^\top\alpha = u$, each in $n^2$ operations, with $\log\det K$ for free; this is how [Gaussian process regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#regression-the-posterior-in-closed-form) computes its posterior and marginal likelihood without forming $K^{-1}$.

The factorization also exposes a numerical problem. A kernel matrix that is positive definite in exact arithmetic can be numerically singular. For the RBF kernel $\exp\big(-(x - z)^2/(2 \cdot 0.5^2)\big)$ on 50 equally spaced points of $[0, 1]$, the largest eigenvalue is 38.3, and the smallest, computed in double precision, comes out as $-9 \cdot 10^{-15}$: the true value is lost in rounding. Cholesky fails. Adding the jitter $10^{-10}I$ makes it succeed, at the price of a slightly different matrix.

## The Rayleigh quotient: the extreme directions

How large can $x^\top Ax$ be for a vector of length 1? This is the steepest direction of a quadratic, the direction of largest variance in PCA, and the largest stretch of a matrix. The **Rayleigh quotient** $R(x) = x^\top Ax/x^\top x$, for $x \ne 0$, asks the same question without fixing the length.

In eigen-coordinates, with $x^\top x = y^\top Q^\top Qy = y^\top y$, the quotient is an average of the eigenvalues:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R(x) &= \frac{\lambda_1y_1^2 + \dots + \lambda_ny_n^2}{y_1^2 + \dots + y_n^2} \\
&= w_1\lambda_1 + \dots + w_n\lambda_n ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with weights $w_i = y_i^2/\lVert y\rVert^2$ that are non-negative and sum to 1. An average lies between its smallest and its largest term:

$$
\lambda_{\min} \le R(x) \le \lambda_{\max} .
$$

All the weight sits on $\lambda_{\max}$ when $x$ is its eigenvector, so the bound is reached there, and likewise for $\lambda_{\min}$.

**Example.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $x = (1, 1)$, $R = (5 + 4 + 2)/2 = 5.5$. In eigen-coordinates $y = (3, -1)/\sqrt5$, so the weights are $0.9$ and $0.1$, and $0.9 \cdot 6 + 0.1 \cdot 1 = 5.5$. On the unit circle $x = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ the quotient is

$$
\begin{aligned}
R(\theta) &= 5\cos^2\theta + 4\cos\theta\sin\theta + 2\sin^2\theta \\
&= 3.5 + 1.5\cos 2\theta + 2\sin 2\theta ,
\end{aligned}
$$

which oscillates between 1 and 6 with period $\pi$ and peaks at $\theta = 26.57°$, the direction of $q_1 = (2, 1)/\sqrt5$:

The first principal component maximizes the variance $w^\top Sw$ over unit vectors $w$, a Rayleigh quotient of the covariance, so it is the top eigenvector ([PCA as variance maximization](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#pca-as-variance-maximization)). For any matrix $B$, $\lVert Bx\rVert^2/\lVert x\rVert^2 = x^\top B^\top Bx/x^\top x$, so the largest factor by which $B$ stretches a vector is $\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(B^\top B)}$, the spectral norm; the [next note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) builds the SVD on this observation.

Along a unit direction $u$, the second derivative of a function with Hessian $H$ is $u^\top Hu$, which lies between the extreme eigenvalues of $H$. Gradient descent with step $\eta$ is stable only if $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}(H)$, and the ratio $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$, the condition number, sets its speed ([gradient descent](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#how-large-a-step-the-quadratic-case)).

### Computing the largest eigenvalue: power iteration

For a large matrix the top eigenpair is found without any factorization: repeat $x \leftarrow Ax/\lVert Ax\rVert$ and read off $R(x)$, one matrix–vector product per step. The [iterative methods note](/notes/math/iterative-methods#the-power-method) derives how fast this converges and why the Rayleigh quotient converges twice as fast as the direction.

### Intermediate eigenvalues

*Optional depth: the min–max characterization of every eigenvalue. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

With eigenvalues sorted $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_n$, the **Courant–Fischer theorem** says that the $k$-th one is the best worst-case Rayleigh quotient over $k$-dimensional subspaces:

$$
\lambda_k = \max_{\dim S = k}\ \min_{x \in S,\ x \ne 0} R(x) .
$$

The span of $q_1, \dots, q_k$ attains the maximum: on it, $R$ is an average of $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k$, so its minimum is $\lambda_k$. No other $k$-dimensional subspace does better. It must contain a unit vector orthogonal to $q_1, \dots, q_{k-1}$, because a subspace of dimension $k$ and one of dimension $n - k + 1$ intersect, and on such a vector $R$ is an average of $\lambda_k, \dots, \lambda_n$, at most $\lambda_k$.

Two consequences are used in machine learning. The $k$ leading eigenvectors span the $k$-dimensional subspace that keeps the most variance, which is PCA with $k$ components. And eigenvalues of symmetric matrices move by at most the size of a perturbation, $\lvert\lambda_k(A + E) - \lambda_k(A)\rvert \le \lVert E\rVert_2$ (Weyl's inequality), so the spectrum of a sample covariance is a stable estimate when the sample is large ([Horn and Johnson, 2013](#references), sections 4.2 and 4.3).

## Where it breaks: nearly equal eigenvalues

Weyl's inequality protects the eigenvalues. Nothing protects the eigenvectors when two eigenvalues are close. Take $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$ and

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 + \varepsilon \end{pmatrix},
$$

whose eigenvectors are $e_1$ and $e_2$, and add $\delta = 10^{-6}$ to both off-diagonal entries. The eigenvalues move by $6.2 \cdot 10^{-7}$ each, within $\delta$ as Weyl promises. The eigenvector of the larger one turns by $\tfrac12\arctan(2\delta/\varepsilon) = 31.7°$. With $\delta$ ten times larger it would turn by almost $45°$.

The angle is set by the ratio of the perturbation to the gap between the eigenvalues, here $\delta/\varepsilon = 1$. Only the eigenspace of a cluster of close eigenvalues is well determined; for $A = I$ every orthonormal basis is an eigenbasis. Principal components with nearly equal variances can therefore swap or rotate between two samples of the same data, and an algorithm that tracks individual components across runs must compare subspaces instead.

Symmetry itself can be inexact. $X^\top X$ computed in floating point, or a Hessian estimated by finite differences, may be symmetric only up to rounding, and a general eigenvalue routine can then return tiny imaginary parts. Symmetrize, $\tfrac12(A + A^\top)$, and call a symmetric solver such as `numpy.linalg.eigh`. The full decomposition costs $O(n^3)$, out of reach for a kernel matrix on $10^5$ points; there one computes a few extreme eigenpairs iteratively, or approximates the matrix, as in the [cost section](/notes/ml/kernels#the-cost-and-random-fourier-features) of the kernels note.

## What you should remember

- A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis: $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top = \sum_i\lambda_iq_iq_i^\top$.
- In $y = Q^\top x$, $x^\top Ax = \sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2$; for $A \succ 0$ the level set $q = c$ has half-axes $\sqrt{c/\lambda_i}$ along $q_i$.
- $A \succ 0$ iff all eigenvalues, pivots or leading minors are positive, iff $A = B^\top B$ with independent columns; for $A \succeq 0$ leading minors are not enough.
- Covariance, Gram and kernel matrices are $B^\top B$, hence semidefinite; $+\lambda I$ makes them definite.
- Cholesky $A = LL^\top$ costs $\tfrac13n^3$, samples Gaussians as $\mu + Lz$, and gives $\log\det A = 2\sum_j\log l_{jj}$.
- $\lambda_{\min} \le x^\top Ax/x^\top x \le \lambda_{\max}$; eigenvectors of close eigenvalues are unstable.

## Code

The spectral theorem, the quadratic-form identity, the definiteness tests and the semidefinite boundary, Cholesky by the formula of the note against NumPy, jitter, the Rayleigh quotient on the circle, and the turning eigenvector. It runs in well under a second; digits in the last places can differ across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_triangular

# 1. the spectral theorem: real eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenvectors, A = Q Lambda Q^T = sum of rank-one terms
A = np.array([[5.0, 2.0], [2.0, 2.0]])
lam, Q = np.linalg.eigh(A)                      # eigh: for symmetric matrices, eigenvalues in ascending order
print(lam, np.allclose(Q.T @ Q, np.eye(2)), np.allclose(sum(l * np.outer(q, q) for l, q in zip(lam, Q.T)), A))
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
M = rng.normal(size=(6, 6)); S = M + M.T        # a random symmetric matrix
ls, Qs = np.linalg.eigh(S)
print(np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(S).imag).max() < 1e-12, np.allclose(Qs.T @ Qs, np.eye(6)), np.allclose(Qs @ np.diag(ls) @ Qs.T, S))
# [1. 6.] True True
# True True True

# 2. the quadratic form in eigen-coordinates: x^T A x = sum lambda_i y_i^2 with y = Q^T x
x = rng.normal(size=2); y = Q.T @ x
q1 = np.array([2.0, 1.0]) / np.sqrt(5)
print(np.isclose(x @ A @ x, (lam * y**2).sum()), round(float((q1 / np.sqrt(6)) @ A @ (q1 / np.sqrt(6))), 6))
# True 1.0: the end of the short half-axis 1/sqrt(6) along q1 lies on the level set q = 1

# 3. definiteness tests: eigenvalues, leading minors, Cholesky; the boundary c = 0.8; the semidefinite trap
def leading_minors(M): return [round(float(np.linalg.det(M[:k, :k])), 6) for k in range(1, len(M) + 1)]
def is_pd_by_cholesky(M):
    try: np.linalg.cholesky(M); return True
    except np.linalg.LinAlgError: return False
for name, M in [('A', A), ('B', np.array([[1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 1.0]])), ('c=0.8', np.array([[5.0, 2.0], [2.0, 0.8]])),
                ('neg', np.array([[2.0, -1.0], [-1.0, 2.0]])), ('trap', np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [0.0, -1.0]]))]:
    print(name, np.linalg.eigvalsh(M).round(6) + 0.0, leading_minors(M), is_pd_by_cholesky(M))
# A [1. 6.] [5.0, 6.0] True
# B [-1.  3.] [1.0, -3.0] False
# c=0.8 [0.  5.8] [5.0, 0.0] True    (rounding leaves the second pivot at about 1e-16)
# neg [1. 3.] [2.0, 3.0] True
# trap [-1.  0.] [0.0, 0.0] False

# 4. covariance and kernel matrices are positive semidefinite; X^T X is singular when n <= d
X = rng.normal(size=(5, 8)); Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)
ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Xc.T @ Xc / 5)
print(ev.min() > -1e-12, int((ev > 1e-10).sum()))   # 5 centred points in 8 dimensions: rank at most 4
pts = rng.normal(size=(30, 2))
K_rbf = np.exp(-((pts[:, None] - pts[None]) ** 2).sum(-1) / 2)
K_dist = np.sqrt(((pts[:, None] - pts[None]) ** 2).sum(-1))       # a distance is not a kernel
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(K_rbf).min() > -1e-12, round(float(np.linalg.eigvalsh(K_dist).min()), 2))
# True 4
# True -19.26

# 5. Cholesky by the formula of the note, column by column, against NumPy; sampling and two triangular solves
def cholesky(A):
    n = len(A); L = np.zeros_like(A)
    for j in range(n):
        d = A[j, j] - L[j, :j] @ L[j, :j]
        if d <= 0: raise ValueError(f'not positive definite: pivot {j + 1} is {d:.3g}')
        L[j, j] = np.sqrt(d)
        for i in range(j + 1, n):
            L[i, j] = (A[i, j] - L[i, :j] @ L[j, :j]) / L[j, j]
    return L
K = np.array([[4.0, 2.0, 2.0], [2.0, 5.0, 3.0], [2.0, 3.0, 6.0]])
L = cholesky(K)
print(L.tolist(), np.allclose(L, np.linalg.cholesky(K)), round(2 * np.log(np.diag(L)).sum(), 4), round(float(np.log(np.linalg.det(K))), 4))
Z = rng.normal(size=(200_000, 3)) @ L.T
alpha = solve_triangular(L.T, solve_triangular(L, np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0]), lower=True), lower=False)
print(np.cov(Z.T).round(1).tolist(), np.allclose(K @ alpha, [1.0, 2.0, 3.0]))
# [[2.0, 0.0, 0.0], [1.0, 2.0, 0.0], [1.0, 1.0, 2.0]] True 4.1589 4.1589
# [[4.0, 2.0, 2.0], [2.0, 5.0, 3.0], [2.0, 3.0, 6.0]] True

# 6. jitter: an RBF kernel matrix that is numerically singular
t = np.linspace(0, 1, 50)
Kt = np.exp(-(t[:, None] - t[None]) ** 2 / (2 * 0.5**2))
e = np.linalg.eigvalsh(Kt)
print(f'{e.max():.1f} {e.min():.1g}', is_pd_by_cholesky(Kt), is_pd_by_cholesky(Kt + 1e-10 * np.eye(50)))
# 38.3 -9e-15 False True

# 7. the Rayleigh quotient on the unit circle
theta = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 100_001)
U = np.stack([np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)])
Rq = np.einsum('in,ij,jn->n', U, A, U)
print(round(Rq.max(), 6), round(np.degrees(theta[Rq.argmax()]), 1), round(Rq.min(), 6), round(np.degrees(theta[Rq.argmin()]), 1),
      round(np.degrees(np.arctan2(1, 2)), 2))   # the angle of q1 = (2, 1)/sqrt(5)
# 6.0 26.6 1.0 116.6 26.57

# 8. nearly equal eigenvalues: the eigenvalues barely move, the eigenvector turns by 31.7 degrees
eps = delta = 1e-6
for E in (np.diag([1.0, 1.0 + eps]), np.array([[1.0, delta], [delta, 1.0 + eps]])):
    l, V = np.linalg.eigh(E)
    print(np.round((l - [1.0, 1.0 + eps]) * 1e7, 1), round(float(np.degrees(np.arccos(abs(V[1, 1])))), 1))
# [0. 0.] 0.0
# [-6.2  6.2] 31.7
```

## Next

A symmetric matrix stretches space along perpendicular axes. A general matrix, even a rectangular one that maps $\mathbb R^n$ to $\mathbb R^m$, is almost as simple: it maps one set of perpendicular axes onto another, stretching each by a factor. The [next note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) builds that decomposition, the SVD, from the eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $A^\top A$, and uses it to read off rank, to approximate a matrix by one of lower rank, and to solve least-squares problems that have no unique solution.
