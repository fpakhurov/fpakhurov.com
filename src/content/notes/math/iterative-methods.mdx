Take a linear system with a million unknowns: the values of a field on a $1000 \times 1000$ grid, the weights of a large regression, the coefficients of a kernel model. Every method in the course so far would factorize the matrix. For $n = 10^6$ that is out of reach:

| Quantity | Dense LU | One sparse product $Ax$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| storage | $n^2$ doubles, 8 TB | 5 nonzeros per row, 64 MB |
| arithmetic | $\tfrac23 n^3 = 6.7 \cdot 10^{17}$ flops | $2 \cdot 5n = 10^7$ flops |

The LU count is derived in the [linear systems note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#what-elimination-costs): step $k$ updates an $(n - k) \times (n - k)$ block, and the sum of squares gives $\tfrac13 n^3$ multiply–adds ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), Appendix C.3). At $10^{13}$ flops per second the factorization would take most of a day, on a machine with 8 TB of memory. The product with a sparse matrix takes milliseconds. Many matrices of machine learning are not even stored: a Hessian is available only through Hessian–vector products, a kernel matrix through a routine that multiplies it by a vector.

What can be computed from products $v \mapsto Av$ alone? More than one might expect: the largest eigenvalue, by the power method, and the solution of $Ax = b$ for a symmetric positive definite $A$, by conjugate gradient. Both build their answers from the same vectors $b, Ab, A^2b, \dots$, and both are slowed down by the same thing, a spectrum that is hard to separate.

## The power method

Multiply any vector by $A$ again and again, and its direction turns towards the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue, as the [eigenvalue note](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues#diagonalization-and-matrix-powers) showed for matrix powers. The **power method** turns this into an algorithm. Start from a random $x_0$ and repeat

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{k+1} &= \frac{Ax_k}{\lVert Ax_k\rVert} , \\
\mu_k &= R(x_k) = \frac{x_k^\top Ax_k}{x_k^\top x_k} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The normalization only keeps the numbers from overflowing; $\mu_k$ is the [Rayleigh quotient](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-rayleigh-quotient-the-extreme-directions), the estimate of the eigenvalue.

Why it works: let $A$ be symmetric with orthonormal eigenvectors $q_i$ and eigenvalues ordered by size, $\lvert\lambda_1\rvert \gt \lvert\lambda_2\rvert \ge \dots \ge \lvert\lambda_n\rvert$. Expand the start as $x_0 = \sum_i c_i q_i$ with $c_1 \ne 0$. Each multiplication scales the $i$-th component by $\lambda_i$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^k x_0 &= \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \lambda_i^k q_i \\
&= c_1 \lambda_1^k \Big(q_1 + \sum_{i \ge 2} \frac{c_i}{c_1} \Big(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\Big)^k q_i\Big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Every term of the sum shrinks at least as fast as $\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert^k$. The normalized $x_k$ is $A^k x_0$ divided by its length, so it has the same angle $\theta_k$ to $q_1$. The $q_i$ are orthonormal, so the tangent of that angle is the length of the part orthogonal to $q_1$ over the part along it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tan\theta_k &= \frac{\big(\sum_{i \ge 2} c_i^2\lambda_i^{2k}\big)^{1/2}}{\lvert c_1\lambda_1^k\rvert} \\
&\le \Big\lvert\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\Big\rvert^k \frac{\big(\sum_{i \ge 2} c_i^2\big)^{1/2}}{\lvert c_1\rvert} \\
&= \Big\lvert\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\Big\rvert^k \tan\theta_0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The distance from $\pm q_1$ falls by the factor $\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert$ per step: the **convergence rate** of the power method.

The eigenvalue estimate converges twice as fast. Write a unit vector as $x = (q_1 + \varepsilon v)/\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon^2}$, with $v$ a unit vector orthogonal to $q_1$. Then $q_1^\top A v = \lambda_1 q_1^\top v = 0$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
R(x) &= \frac{\lambda_1 + \varepsilon^2\, v^\top Av}{1 + \varepsilon^2} \\
&= \lambda_1 - \frac{\varepsilon^2\,(\lambda_1 - v^\top Av)}{1 + \varepsilon^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\varepsilon = \tan\theta$. An error $\varepsilon$ in the direction costs only $\varepsilon^2$ in the eigenvalue, because the Rayleigh quotient is stationary at an eigenvector, and $\lvert\lambda_1 - v^\top Av\rvert$ is at most $2\lvert\lambda_1\rvert$. With $\tan\theta_k \le \lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert^k\tan\theta_0$ from above, the error of $\mu_k$ for a symmetric matrix falls like $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^{2k}$.

**Example.** For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalues 6 and 1, start from $x_0 = (1, 0)$:

| Step $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mu_k$ | 5 | 5.96552 | 5.99904 | 5.99997 |
| $6 - \mu_k$ | 1 | $3.4 \cdot 10^{-2}$ | $9.6 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $2.7 \cdot 10^{-5}$ |

The error falls by about $1/36 = (\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^2$ per step.

Each step costs one product with $A$ and two vectors of memory, which is why the method and its relatives run wherever a matrix is too large to decompose. [Spectral normalization](/notes/generative-models/wgan#sn-gan-spectral-normalization) of a GAN estimates the largest singular value of each weight matrix with one power step per training step, warm-started from the previous step's vector. [PageRank](/notes/probability/markov-chains#pagerank) is the dominant eigenvector of a transition matrix with one row per web page. The step size $1/L$ of gradient descent needs $L$, the largest eigenvalue of a Hessian, which the power method estimates from Hessian–vector products.

### Where it breaks: a small gap

The rate $\lvert\lambda_2/\lambda_1\rvert$ is close to 1 when the top two eigenvalues are close, and then the method crawls. A matrix met everywhere in numerical work shows it. The **grid Laplacian** on an $N \times N$ grid has the row

$$
(Lx)_{ij} = 4x_{ij} - x_{i-1,j} - x_{i+1,j} - x_{i,j-1} - x_{i,j+1} ,
$$

with neighbours outside the grid set to zero. It has $n = N^2$ unknowns, at most five nonzeros per row, and eigenvalues known in closed form,

$$
\lambda_{jk} = 4 - 2\cos\frac{j\pi}{N + 1} - 2\cos\frac{k\pi}{N + 1} , \quad 1 \le j, k \le N ,
$$

between $4 - 4\cos\frac{\pi}{N+1} \approx 2\pi^2/N^2$ and $4 + 4\cos\frac{\pi}{N+1} \lt 8$. Divided by $h^2$, with grid spacing $h$, each row is the five-point difference approximation of $-\Delta$, the operator behind diffusion and smoothing on images and meshes.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: the eigenvalues of the grid Laplacian</summary>

Start with one dimension: the $N \times N$ matrix $T$ with 2 on the diagonal and $-1$ beside it. Try the vector $(u_j)_i = \sin(i\theta_j)$ with $\theta_j = j\pi/(N + 1)$. The identity $\sin(a - b) + \sin(a + b) = 2\sin a\cos b$ gives, for each row $i$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
(Tu_j)_i &= 2\sin(i\theta_j) - \sin((i - 1)\theta_j) - \sin((i + 1)\theta_j) \\
&= (2 - 2\cos\theta_j)\sin(i\theta_j) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first and last rows lack a neighbour, but the missing terms are $\sin(0) = 0$ and $\sin((N + 1)\theta_j) = \sin(j\pi) = 0$, so the formula holds there too. The grid Laplacian is $L = I \otimes T + T \otimes I$, one $T$ along each grid direction, and $L(u_j \otimes u_k) = (\mu_j + \mu_k)(u_j \otimes u_k)$ with $\mu_j = 2 - 2\cos\theta_j$. The $N^2$ products $u_j \otimes u_k$ give all eigenvalues. Finally $1 - \cos\theta \approx \theta^2/2$ gives the smallest one.

</details>

For $N = 100$ the ratio $\lambda_2/\lambda_1$ is $0.99964$. Each step removes 0.036% of the unwanted components, and the power method needs 17,142 steps to get $\lambda_1$ to six digits. SciPy's `eigsh`, which wraps the implicitly restarted Lanczos method of the ARPACK library, gets it to full precision with 511 products. The difference is in how the products are used. The power method keeps only the latest vector $A^k x_0$, while Lanczos finds the best estimate in the span of all of them, the Krylov subspace of the section below.

Two other remedies change the matrix instead. **Inverse iteration with a shift** applies the power method to $(A - \sigma I)^{-1}$. Its eigenvalues are $1/(\lambda_i - \sigma)$, so the largest belongs to the eigenvalue of $A$ nearest to $\sigma$, and the rate of the power method becomes $\lvert(\lambda_1 - \sigma)/(\lambda_2 - \sigma)\rvert$, where here $\lambda_1$ is the eigenvalue nearest to $\sigma$ and $\lambda_2$ the next-nearest. A shift close to $\lambda_1$ makes it tiny, at the price of a linear solve per step. **Rayleigh quotient iteration** updates the shift to the current $\mu_k$ at every step and converges cubically for symmetric matrices ([Rakhuba, 2025](#references), lecture 17). For several eigenvectors at once, **subspace iteration** multiplies a block of $p$ vectors and re-orthonormalizes it by QR after each step; its rate is $\lvert\lambda_{p+1}/\lambda_p\rvert$.

## Solving Ax = b by minimizing a quadratic

For a symmetric positive definite $A$, the system $Ax = b$ is an optimization problem in disguise. Define

$$
f(x) = \tfrac12 x^\top Ax - b^\top x .
$$

Its gradient is $\nabla f(x) = Ax - b$, so its only stationary point, and its minimum, is the solution $x^*$. The negative gradient is the **residual** $r = b - Ax$. The distance to the minimum is measured best in the **energy norm** $\lVert v\rVert_A = \sqrt{v^\top Av}$, because the excess of $f$ is exactly half its square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) - f(x^*) &= \tfrac12 x^\top Ax - x^{*\top}Ax + \tfrac12 x^{*\top}Ax^* \\
&= \tfrac12 (x - x^*)^\top A (x - x^*) \\
&= \tfrac12 \lVert x - x^*\rVert_A^2 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the first line substitutes $b = Ax^*$.

The [unconstrained optimization note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization#gradient-descent-on-a-smooth-function) has a method for this: gradient descent. On a quadratic the best step along the residual can be computed exactly. Setting the derivative of $f(x + \alpha r)$ to zero,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tfrac{d}{d\alpha} f(x + \alpha r) &= r^\top\big(A(x + \alpha r) - b\big) \\
&= -r^\top r + \alpha\, r^\top Ar = 0 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

gives $\alpha = r^\top r / r^\top Ar$ and **steepest descent**. The same equation says that the new residual is orthogonal to the old one. With the condition number $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ of $A$, the error of steepest descent in the energy norm shrinks per step by at least the factor

$$
\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1} ,
$$

derived below as the one-step case of the CG bound ([Shewchuk, 1994](#references), §6.2). Reducing the error by a factor $\varepsilon$ takes about $\tfrac\kappa2 \ln(1/\varepsilon)$ steps, because $\ln\frac{1 + y}{1 - y} \approx 2y$ for small $y$, here $y = 1/\kappa$. Because each new gradient is perpendicular to the old one, on an elongated bowl the iterates [zigzag](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization#gradient-descent-against-newton) across the valley, undoing part of the progress they made along it.

Even on a $2 \times 2$ system the method never finishes. For the matrix of the example above and $b = (1, 1)$, with solution $x^* = (0, 0.5)$, the relative error in the energy norm after steps 1 to 6 is

$$
0.522, \ 0.273, \ 0.142, \ 0.074, \ 0.039, \ 0.020 ,
$$

roughly halving at every step, forever.

## Conjugate directions

In a round bowl, $A = I$, the zigzag disappears. Minimizing along each coordinate axis in turn finds the minimum in $n$ steps, because a step along one axis does not spoil the minimum already found along the others. An elongated bowl is a round one in stretched coordinates: with $y = A^{1/2}x$ the quadratic becomes $\tfrac12 y^\top y - (A^{-1/2}b)^\top y$ ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 12). A direction $d$ in $x$ is $A^{1/2}d$ in $y$, and $(A^{1/2}d_i)^\top(A^{1/2}d_j) = d_i^\top A d_j$. So directions that are orthogonal in $y$ are, back in $x$, **conjugate** or **$A$-orthogonal**:

$$
d_i^\top A d_j = 0 \quad \text{for } i \ne j .
$$

Exact line searches along $n$ conjugate directions $d_0, \dots, d_{n-1}$ reach $x^*$ in $n$ steps. To see why, expand the initial error in the directions, $x^* - x_0 = \sum_i \alpha_i d_i$. This is possible because nonzero conjugate directions are linearly independent: multiplying $\sum_i c_i d_i = 0$ by $d_k^\top A$ leaves $c_k\, d_k^\top Ad_k = 0$, and $d_k^\top Ad_k \gt 0$, so every $c_k = 0$. Multiplying the expansion by $d_k^\top A$ kills every term but one, and $A(x^* - x_0) = b - Ax_0 = r_0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_k^\top A (x^* - x_0) &= \alpha_k\, d_k^\top A d_k , \\
\alpha_k &= \frac{d_k^\top r_0}{d_k^\top A d_k} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The line search along $d_k$ from $x_k$ chooses $\alpha = d_k^\top r_k / d_k^\top A d_k$, by the same derivative as for steepest descent with $d_k$ in place of $r$. The residual $r_k = r_0 - \sum_{i \lt k} \alpha_i A d_i$ differs from $r_0$ only by vectors $A d_i$ that are orthogonal to $d_k$, so $d_k^\top r_k = d_k^\top r_0$. Each line search therefore removes exactly one component of the error and leaves the others alone. After $n$ steps none are left ([Shewchuk, 1994](#references), section 7).

The difficulty is to find conjugate directions cheaply. The eigenvectors of $A$ are conjugate, but computing them is harder than solving the system. Gram–Schmidt in the inner product $u^\top A v$ makes any basis conjugate, but each new direction must be orthogonalized against all previous ones, at a cost that grows with every step.

## Conjugate gradient

The **conjugate gradient method** (CG) of [Hestenes and Stiefel (1952)](#references) makes conjugate directions out of the residuals, and, as shown below, the residuals need almost no orthogonalization. Start with $x_0 = 0$, $r_0 = b$, $d_0 = r_0$, and repeat

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_k &= \frac{r_k^\top r_k}{d_k^\top A d_k} , \\
x_{k+1} &= x_k + \alpha_k d_k , \\
r_{k+1} &= r_k - \alpha_k A d_k , \\
\beta_k &= \frac{r_{k+1}^\top r_{k+1}}{r_k^\top r_k} , \\
d_{k+1} &= r_{k+1} + \beta_k d_k .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the exact line search along $d_k$; the derivation below shows why its numerator can be $r_k^\top r_k$. The next two move the point and update the residual with the product $Ad_k$, the only product with $A$ in the step. The last two make the new direction from the new residual, with $\beta_k$ chosen so that $d_{k+1}$ is conjugate to $d_k$.

A step costs one product with $A$, two inner products and three vector updates, and the method stores four vectors. Conjugacy to $d_k$ is all that the formula enforces, yet the new direction comes out conjugate to every earlier one as well. As long as no residual is zero, three facts hold at every step $k$ ([Shewchuk, 1994](#references), section 8; [Rakhuba, 2025](#references), lecture 15):

1. The residuals are mutually orthogonal, and $r_k$ is orthogonal to $d_0, \dots, d_{k-1}$.
2. The residuals $r_0, \dots, r_{k-1}$ and the directions $d_0, \dots, d_{k-1}$ both span the space of $b, Ab, \dots, A^{k-1}b$.
3. The directions are conjugate: $d_k^\top A d_i = 0$ for $i \lt k$.

The heart of the induction is one line. For $i \lt k$, the update $r_{i+1} = r_i - \alpha_i Ad_i$ gives $Ad_i = (r_i - r_{i+1})/\alpha_i$, a vector in the span of $r_0, \dots, r_k$, to which $r_{k+1}$ is orthogonal by fact 1. So $r_{k+1}^\top Ad_i = 0$, and Gram–Schmidt of $r_{k+1}$ against $d_0, \dots, d_{k-1}$ has nothing to subtract: only the last direction needs a correction. The name is a misnomer: it is the directions that are conjugate, and they are built from gradients.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: the induction behind CG</summary>

Assume the three facts up to step $k$; at $k = 0$ they say only that $d_0 = r_0 = b$ spans the space of $b$. Write the line-search step as $\alpha_k = d_k^\top r_k/d_k^\top Ad_k$.

*Residual against directions.* From $r_{k+1} = r_k - \alpha_k Ad_k$, the choice of $\alpha_k$ gives $d_k^\top r_{k+1} = d_k^\top r_k - \alpha_k d_k^\top Ad_k = 0$. For $i \lt k$, $d_i^\top r_{k+1} = d_i^\top r_k - \alpha_k d_i^\top Ad_k = 0 - 0$ by facts 1 and 3.

*Residuals against residuals.* Each $r_i$ with $i \le k$ lies in the span of $d_0, \dots, d_i$ by fact 2, so $r_{k+1}^\top r_i = 0$.

*The two formulas of the algorithm.* Since $d_k = r_k + \beta_{k-1}d_{k-1}$ and $d_{k-1}^\top r_k = 0$, we have $d_k^\top r_k = r_k^\top r_k$, so $\alpha_k = r_k^\top r_k/d_k^\top Ad_k \gt 0$. Gram–Schmidt in the $A$-inner product sets $\beta_k = -r_{k+1}^\top Ad_k/d_k^\top Ad_k$. Substitute $Ad_k = (r_k - r_{k+1})/\alpha_k$ and $d_k^\top Ad_k = r_k^\top r_k/\alpha_k$, and use $r_{k+1}^\top r_k = 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\beta_k &= -\frac{r_{k+1}^\top r_k - r_{k+1}^\top r_{k+1}}{r_k^\top r_k} \\
&= \frac{r_{k+1}^\top r_{k+1}}{r_k^\top r_k} .
\end{aligned}
$$

*Conjugacy.* $d_{k+1}^\top Ad_k = 0$ by the choice of $\beta_k$. For $i \lt k$, $d_{k+1}^\top Ad_i = r_{k+1}^\top Ad_i + \beta_k d_k^\top Ad_i$; the first term is zero by the one-line argument above and the second by fact 3.

*Spans.* $r_{k+1}$ and $d_{k+1}$ lie in the space of $b, \dots, A^{k+1}b$, because $Ad_k$ does. The $k + 2$ residuals are nonzero and mutually orthogonal, hence independent, so they span that space, whose dimension is at most $k + 2$. The directions are nonzero, since $d_{k+1}^\top r_{k+1} = r_{k+1}^\top r_{k+1} \gt 0$, and conjugate, hence independent as well.

</details>

**Example.** The same system, $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $b = (1, 1)$, by hand:

| Step | $\alpha$ | new $x$ | new $r$ | $\beta$ | new $d$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | $\tfrac{2}{11}$ | $(\tfrac{2}{11}, \tfrac{2}{11})$ | $(-\tfrac{3}{11}, \tfrac{3}{11})$ | $\tfrac{9}{121}$ | $\tfrac{1}{121}(-24, 42)$ |
| 1 | $\tfrac{11}{12}$ | $(0, \tfrac12)$ | $(0, 0)$ | | |

The first step is a step of steepest descent, with $Ad_0 = (7, 4)$. The second direction differs from the residual $r_1$, and it is conjugate to the first: $(-24, 42) \cdot (7, 4) = 0$. Two steps give the exact solution, where steepest descent was still 2% away after six.

## Krylov subspaces

Every vector CG computes is a combination of $b, Ab, A^2b, \dots$. The span of the first $k$ of them is the **Krylov subspace**

$$
\mathcal K_k(A, b) = \operatorname{span}\{b, Ab, \dots, A^{k-1}b\} .
$$

Its vectors are cheap, one product with $A$ each. CG picks the best point of the growing space: $x_k$ minimizes the energy-norm error $\lVert x - x^*\rVert_A$ over all of $\mathcal K_k$, equivalently minimizes $f$ there ([Rakhuba, 2025](#references), lecture 15). The power method computes the same vectors and keeps only the last one.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: CG is optimal over the Krylov space</summary>

The proof is Pythagoras in the energy norm. The iterate $x_k = \sum_{i \lt k}\alpha_i d_i$ lies in $\mathcal K_k$, and its error $e_k = x^* - x_k$ has $Ae_k = b - Ax_k = r_k$, which is orthogonal to $r_0, \dots, r_{k-1}$ and hence to all of $\mathcal K_k$. For any $x \in \mathcal K_k$ put $z = x - x_k \in \mathcal K_k$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert x - x^*\rVert_A^2 &= \lVert z - e_k\rVert_A^2 \\
&= \lVert z\rVert_A^2 - 2\, z^\top r_k + \lVert e_k\rVert_A^2 \\
&= \lVert z\rVert_A^2 + \lVert e_k\rVert_A^2 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

which is smallest at $z = 0$, that is at $x = x_k$.

</details>

The best point can be described by polynomials. A point of $\mathcal K_k$ is $\phi(A)b$ for a polynomial $\phi$ of degree below $k$, so its error is $x^* - \phi(A)Ax^* = p(A)x^*$ with $p(\lambda) = 1 - \lambda \phi(\lambda)$: a polynomial of degree $k$ with $p(0) = 1$. Expanding $x^* = \sum_i c_i q_i$ in orthonormal eigenvectors of $A$, as for the power method, gives $p(A)x^* = \sum_i c_i\,p(\lambda_i)\,q_i$ and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert p(A)x^*\rVert_A^2 &= \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2\, p(\lambda_i)^2 \\
&\le \max_i p(\lambda_i)^2 \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2 \\
&= \max_i p(\lambda_i)^2\, \lVert x^*\rVert_A^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

This turns the minimization into a bound on the spectrum, with $\lVert x^*\rVert_A = \lVert x_0 - x^*\rVert_A$ for the start $x_0 = 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert x_k - x^*\rVert_A &= \min_{p(0) = 1} \lVert p(A)\, x^*\rVert_A \\
&\le \min_{p(0) = 1}\ \max_i \lvert p(\lambda_i)\rvert \cdot \lVert x^*\rVert_A .
\end{aligned}
$$

CG converges as fast as a polynomial with $p(0) = 1$ can be made small at all the eigenvalues at once. The spread of the eigenvalues matters as much as their range:

1. With $m$ distinct eigenvalues, the polynomial $\prod_j (1 - \lambda/\lambda_j)$ of degree $m$ vanishes at all of them, so CG is exact after $m$ steps, in exact arithmetic.
2. Eigenvalues in a tight cluster are almost one eigenvalue. On a cluster of width $\delta$ around $c$, the single factor $(1 - \lambda/c)$ is at most $\delta/(2c)$.
3. An isolated outlier $\lambda_j$ costs about one step. The factor $(1 - \lambda/\lambda_j)$ vanishes at it and stays below 1 in absolute value on $(0, \lambda_j]$, so multiplying it into a polynomial that is small on the other eigenvalues keeps that polynomial small there.

Two spectra with the same condition number show the difference. A diagonal matrix with 1,000 eigenvalues spread evenly over $[1, 1000]$ needs 176 CG steps to bring the relative residual to $10^{-8}$. One with 995 eigenvalues in $[1, 2]$ and five outliers up to 1,000 needs 24.

In floating point the vectors $b, Ab, A^2b, \dots$ make a terrible basis. They all turn towards the top eigenvector, as in the power method, so their matrix is badly conditioned; [Strang (2006)](#references) works out a $4 \times 4$ example in which it already has condition number about 1,000. Krylov methods therefore orthogonalize as they go. For symmetric $A$ the orthogonalization needs only the two previous vectors, a three-term recurrence called the **Lanczos** process, and CG is one way of organizing it. The reason: with the orthonormal basis $Q$ built so far, the matrix $H = Q^\top AQ$ of coefficients has zeros below its first subdiagonal, because $A$ times the $j$-th basis vector lies in the span of the first $j + 1$; for symmetric $A$ it is also symmetric, hence tridiagonal ([Strang, 2006](#references)). The restriction of $A$ to the orthonormal basis is a small tridiagonal matrix whose eigenvalues approximate the extreme eigenvalues of $A$ ([Rakhuba, 2025](#references), lectures 17–18), which is how `eigsh` found the top eigenvalue above.

For a nonsymmetric $A$, Arnoldi's process orthogonalizes against all previous vectors, and **GMRES** picks the point of $\mathcal K_k$ with the smallest residual; for a symmetric indefinite $A$ the short-recurrence version is MINRES ([Rakhuba, 2025](#references), lecture 16).

## The square root of the condition number

Without knowing the eigenvalues, only their range $[\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]$, we can put a specific polynomial into the bound: any $p$ with $p(0) = 1$ gives an upper bound. The right choice is a shifted and scaled Chebyshev polynomial, which is in fact the polynomial of degree $k$ that stays smallest on the interval among the polynomials equal to 1 at zero ([Shewchuk, 1994](#references), appendix C3). Its size on the interval gives (Shewchuk, section 9.2; [Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 12, Theorem 2)

$$
\lVert x_k - x^*\rVert_A \le 2\Big(\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}\Big)^k \lVert x_0 - x^*\rVert_A .
$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation: the square-root bound</summary>

The Chebyshev polynomials are $T_0 = 1$, $T_1(x) = x$ and $T_{k+1}(x) = 2xT_k(x) - T_{k-1}(x)$, so $T_k$ has degree $k$. Two facts follow from the recurrence. On $[-1, 1]$, write $x = \cos\theta$: then $T_k(x) = \cos k\theta$, because $\cos(k + 1)\theta + \cos(k - 1)\theta = 2\cos\theta\cos k\theta$ is the same recurrence with the same first two values, so $\lvert T_k\rvert \le 1$ there. For $x \ge 1$, write $x = (z + z^{-1})/2$ with $z = x + \sqrt{x^2 - 1} \ge 1$: then $T_k(x) = (z^k + z^{-k})/2$, by the same recurrence for $z^k + z^{-k}$, which also starts from $1$ and $x$, so $T_k(x) \ge z^k/2$.

Map the interval onto $[-1, 1]$ by $\ell(\lambda) = (\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min} - 2\lambda)/(\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min})$ and take $p(\lambda) = T_k(\ell(\lambda))/T_k(\ell(0))$, so that $p(0) = 1$. On the interval $\lvert p\rvert \le 1/T_k(\sigma)$, with $\sigma = \ell(0) = (\kappa + 1)/(\kappa - 1)$. Since $\sigma^2 - 1 = 4\kappa/(\kappa - 1)^2$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= \sigma + \sqrt{\sigma^2 - 1} = \frac{\kappa + 1 + 2\sqrt\kappa}{\kappa - 1} \\
&= \frac{(\sqrt\kappa + 1)^2}{(\sqrt\kappa - 1)(\sqrt\kappa + 1)} = \frac{\sqrt\kappa + 1}{\sqrt\kappa - 1} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and $\max_i\lvert p(\lambda_i)\rvert \le 2/z^k$, the bound above. For $k = 1$ the value is exact, $T_1(\sigma) = \sigma$, so one step from any starting point reduces the energy-norm error by at least $1/\sigma = (\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$. A step of steepest descent is such a step: it minimizes $f$ along the current residual, the one-dimensional Krylov space at the current point. This proves the steepest-descent factor quoted earlier.

</details>

Compare the steepest-descent factor $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$: CG has the same expression with $\sqrt\kappa$ in place of $\kappa$. To reduce the error by a factor $\varepsilon$, using $\ln\frac{1 + y}{1 - y} \approx 2y$ with $y = 1/\kappa$ and $y = 1/\sqrt\kappa$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k_{\text{SD}} &\approx \frac{\kappa}{2} \ln\frac1\varepsilon , \\
k_{\text{CG}} &\approx \frac{\sqrt\kappa}{2} \ln\frac2\varepsilon .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $\kappa = 10^4$ and six digits that is the difference between about 69,000 and 725 steps.

The grid Laplacian with $N = 100$ has $\kappa = 4{,}134$. With $b$ all ones, reducing the energy-norm error a millionfold takes:

| Method | Bound | Steps taken |
| --- | --- | --- |
| steepest descent | 28,554 | 28,369 |
| conjugate gradient | 466 | 144 |

Steepest descent follows its bound almost exactly. CG beats its own bound threefold. The bound replaced each $p(\lambda_i)^2$ in $\sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2\, p(\lambda_i)^2$ by the worst one, and here many $c_i$ are zero. The eigenvectors are products of the sine vectors $\sin(i\theta_j)$, and for even $j$ such a vector is antisymmetric about the middle of the grid, so its sum, the component of $b$ along it, is zero. Only the quarter of the eigenvectors with $j$ and $k$ both odd take part.

[Momentum](/notes/ml/stochastic-and-adaptive-optimizers#momentum-remember-the-direction) gives gradient descent the same $\sqrt\kappa$ rate on a quadratic, but only with step and momentum tuned from $\lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_{\max}$. CG needs neither. Started from 0, gradient descent with or without momentum adds at each step multiples of the current residual and of the previous step, so its $k$-th iterate lies in $\mathcal K_k$; CG's minimization over that space makes it at least as good in the energy norm.

The $\sqrt\kappa$ makes CG practical at the size of the opening example. The Laplacian with $N = 1000$ has $n = 10^6$ unknowns and $\kappa \approx 4 \cdot 10^5$. CG brings the relative residual to $10^{-8}$ in 1,853 steps, about $4 \cdot 10^{10}$ flops and seconds of time, with 64 MB for the matrix and 32 MB for the four vectors.

### Where it breaks: rounding and ill-conditioning

Finite termination is a property of exact arithmetic. In floating point the residuals slowly lose their orthogonality and the directions their conjugacy, so information that CG had already used comes back. Ridge regression with 200 features whose scales range from 1 to 1,000 leads to the system $(X^\top X + I)w = X^\top y$ with $\kappa = 1.1 \cdot 10^6$. CG needs 2,904 steps on this $200 \times 200$ system, almost fifteen times the dimension. The method still converges. This behaviour made numerical analysts set CG aside in the 1960s, and it returned in the 1970s as an iterative method, judged by how fast its residual falls ([Shewchuk, 1994](#references), section 9).

The stopping test has the same subtlety. CG never sees the error, only the residual, and the usual test is $\lVert r_k\rVert \le \text{tol} \cdot \lVert b\rVert$. A small residual guarantees a small error only up to the factor $\kappa$: the relative error is at most $\kappa$ times the relative residual, by the bound of the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability#the-condition-number-of-a-matrix). The recursively updated $r_k$ also drifts from the true $b - Ax_k$, since rounding errors in the update $r_k - \alpha_k Ad_k$ are never corrected against $b$, and long runs recompute it from time to time (Shewchuk, sections 4 and 9).

## Preconditioning

If $\kappa$ sets the speed, change the system so that $\kappa$ becomes small. A **preconditioner** is a symmetric positive definite $M \approx A$ whose systems $Mz = r$ are cheap to solve. CG is then applied to $M^{-1}Ax = M^{-1}b$, written symmetrically through a factor $M = C^\top C$ as $C^{-\top}AC^{-1}y = C^{-\top}b$ with $y = Cx$. The factor never appears in the algorithm: each step needs one solve with $M$ ([Shewchuk, 1994](#references), section 12; [Rakhuba, 2025](#references), lecture 17). The rate is set by the eigenvalues of $M^{-1}A$, because $C^{-\top}AC^{-1}$ has the same ones: $C^{-1}(C^{-\top}AC^{-1})C = M^{-1}A$ is a similar matrix.

The simplest choice, $M = \operatorname{diag}(A)$ or **Jacobi**, rescales the variables; for the ridge system above it lowers $\kappa$ from $1.1 \cdot 10^6$ to 3.55 and the step count from 2,904 to 20. It is the matrix version of [scaling the features](/notes/ml/gradient-descent#scaling-the-features) before gradient descent. Incomplete Cholesky factors, which drop the fill-in of the exact factorization, are a stronger choice (Shewchuk, section 12).

CG also runs inside other methods. Newton's method needs the step $H p = -g$, and for a network with millions of weights the Hessian $H$ cannot be stored. **Hessian-free** optimization solves that system approximately by a few CG steps that need only products of a vector with $H$ or with its Gauss–Newton approximation, which automatic differentiation computes at the cost of a few gradients ([Martens, 2010](#references)). SciPy's `Newton-CG` method implements the same idea.

## Code

The costs of the opening, the power method on the $2 \times 2$ example and on the grid Laplacian against `eigsh`, CG written out on the $2 \times 2$ system, steepest descent against CG with their bounds, two spectra with the same $\kappa$, Jacobi preconditioning of a ridge system, and CG with a million unknowns. It runs in about 15 seconds, most of them in the last part; exact digits can differ across platforms and library versions, and the step count of `eigsh` with the ARPACK version.

```python
import numpy as np
import scipy.sparse as sp
from scipy.sparse.linalg import eigsh, spsolve, LinearOperator

# the 5-point Laplacian on an N x N grid: n = N^2 unknowns, at most 5 nonzeros per row
def laplacian_2d(N):
    T = sp.diags([-1.0, 2.0, -1.0], [-1, 0, 1], shape=(N, N))
    I = sp.identity(N)
    return (sp.kron(I, T) + sp.kron(T, I)).tocsr()

# 1. what n = 10^6 costs: dense LU against one sparse matrix-vector product
n = 10**6
print(f'{2 / 3 * n**3:.1e} flops, {8 * n**2 / 1e12:.0f} TB dense; {2 * 5 * n:.0e} flops per sparse product')
# 6.7e+17 flops, 8 TB dense; 1e+07 flops per sparse product

# 2. the power method on a 2 x 2 matrix with eigenvalues 6 and 1
A = np.array([[5.0, 2.0], [2.0, 2.0]])
x = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
quotients = []
for k in range(4):
    quotients.append(x @ A @ x / (x @ x))                # Rayleigh quotient of the current vector
    x = A @ x / np.linalg.norm(A @ x)
print(np.round(quotients, 5), [f'{6 - q:.1e}' for q in quotients])
# [5.      5.96552 5.99904 5.99997] ['1.0e+00', '3.4e-02', '9.6e-04', '2.7e-05']

# 3. the power method where the gap is small: the Laplacian with N = 100 (n = 10,000)
N = 100
L = laplacian_2d(N)
lam1 = 4 + 4 * np.cos(np.pi / (N + 1))                    # the eigenvalues are known in closed form
lam2 = 4 + 2 * np.cos(np.pi / (N + 1)) + 2 * np.cos(2 * np.pi / (N + 1))
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(size=N * N)
for k in range(1, 100_001):
    y = L @ x
    q = x @ y / (x @ x)
    x = y / np.linalg.norm(y)
    if (lam1 - q) / lam1 < 1e-6:
        break
count = [0]
def matvec(v):
    count[0] += 1
    return L @ v
top = eigsh(LinearOperator(L.shape, matvec=matvec, dtype=float), k=1, which='LA', tol=1e-10,
            v0=np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=N * N))[0][0]
print(round(lam2 / lam1, 5), k, count[0], abs(top - lam1) < 1e-12)
# 0.99964 17142 511 True

# 4. conjugate gradient, written out; keep=True also returns the iterates
def conjugate_gradient(A, b, tol=1e-10, maxiter=100_000, Minv=None, keep=False):
    x = np.zeros_like(b)
    r = b.copy()
    z = r if Minv is None else Minv * r                   # Minv: a diagonal preconditioner, or none
    d = z.copy()
    rz = r @ z
    xs = [x.copy()] if keep else None
    for k in range(1, maxiter + 1):
        Ad = A @ d                                         # the only product with A in the step
        alpha = rz / (d @ Ad)
        x = x + alpha * d
        r = r - alpha * Ad
        if keep:
            xs.append(x.copy())
        if np.linalg.norm(r) <= tol * np.linalg.norm(b):
            break
        z = r if Minv is None else Minv * r
        rz, rz_old = r @ z, rz
        d = z + (rz / rz_old) * d
    return x, k, xs

def steepest_descent(A, b, x_star, tol, maxiter):
    x, r = np.zeros_like(b), b.copy()
    e0 = np.sqrt(x_star @ (A @ x_star))
    errors = []
    for k in range(1, maxiter + 1):
        Ar = A @ r
        x = x + (r @ r) / (r @ Ar) * r                    # exact line search along the residual
        r = r - (r @ r) / (r @ Ar) * Ar
        e = x - x_star
        errors.append(np.sqrt(e @ (A @ e)) / e0)
        if errors[-1] <= tol:
            break
    return k, errors

b = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
x, k, xs = conjugate_gradient(A, b, keep=True)
print(k, np.round(xs[1], 4), np.round(xs[2], 4))
print(np.round(steepest_descent(A, b, np.linalg.solve(A, b), 0, 6)[1], 3))
# 2 [0.1818 0.1818] [0.  0.5]
# [0.522 0.273 0.142 0.074 0.039 0.02 ]

# 5. steepest descent against CG on the Laplacian, b = all ones, error in the A-norm reduced 10^6-fold
b = np.ones(N * N)
x_star = spsolve(L.tocsc(), b)
kappa = lam1 / (4 - 4 * np.cos(np.pi / (N + 1)))
k_sd, _ = steepest_descent(L, b, x_star, 1e-6, 100_000)
_, _, xs = conjugate_gradient(L, b, tol=1e-12, keep=True)
e0 = np.sqrt(x_star @ (L @ x_star))
k_cg = next(k for k, x in enumerate(xs) if np.sqrt((x - x_star) @ (L @ (x - x_star))) <= 1e-6 * e0)
bound_sd = np.log(1e-6) / np.log((kappa - 1) / (kappa + 1))
bound_cg = np.log(1e-6 / 2) / np.log((np.sqrt(kappa) - 1) / (np.sqrt(kappa) + 1))
print(round(kappa), k_sd, round(bound_sd), k_cg, round(bound_cg))
print([round(c * np.log(f / 1e-6)) for c, f in ((1e4 / 2, 1), (1e2 / 2, 2))])   # kappa = 10^4: SD and CG estimates
# 4134 28369 28554 144 466
# [69078, 725]

# 6. the same condition number, two spectra: 1,000 eigenvalues spread over [1, 1000],
#    or 995 of them in [1, 2] and five outliers up to 1000
spread = np.linspace(1, 1000, 1000)
clustered = np.concatenate([np.linspace(1, 2, 995), [100, 200, 400, 700, 1000]])
for lam in (spread, clustered):
    print(lam.max() / lam.min(), conjugate_gradient(sp.diags(lam).tocsr(), np.ones(1000), tol=1e-8)[1])
# 1000.0 176
# 1000.0 24

# 7. preconditioning: ridge regression with features on scales from 1 to 1000
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.normal(size=(2000, 200)) * np.logspace(0, 3, 200)
y = X @ (rng.normal(size=200) / np.logspace(0, 3, 200)) + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=2000)
H, g = X.T @ X + np.eye(200), X.T @ y                     # (X^T X + I) w = X^T y
Minv = 1 / np.diag(H)                                      # Jacobi: divide by the diagonal
ev = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)
evp = np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.sqrt(Minv)[:, None] * H * np.sqrt(Minv)[None, :])
w_plain, k_plain, _ = conjugate_gradient(H, g)
w_jac, k_jac, _ = conjugate_gradient(H, g, Minv=Minv)
print(f'{ev[-1] / ev[0]:.1e} {evp[-1] / evp[0]:.2f}', k_plain, k_jac, np.allclose(w_jac, np.linalg.solve(H, g)))
# 1.1e+06 3.55 2904 20 True

# 8. n = 10^6: the Laplacian with N = 1000, stored in about 64 MB, solved by CG to a relative residual of 1e-8
L = laplacian_2d(1000)
b = np.ones(L.shape[0])
x, k, _ = conjugate_gradient(L, b, tol=1e-8, maxiter=10_000)
mem = (L.data.nbytes + L.indices.nbytes + L.indptr.nbytes) / 1e6
kappa = (4 + 4 * np.cos(np.pi / 1001)) / (4 - 4 * np.cos(np.pi / 1001))
flops = k * (2 * L.nnz + 10 * L.shape[0])                  # per step: one product, two inner products, three updates
print(L.shape[0], L.nnz, round(mem), f'{kappa:.1e}', k, f'{flops:.1e}', f'{np.linalg.norm(b - L @ x) / np.linalg.norm(b):.1e}')
# 1000000 4996000 64 4.1e+05 1853 3.7e+10 9.9e-09
```

## The course in one paragraph

Machine learning is written in linear algebra, differentiated with calculus, trained by optimization and run in floating point; the course built each layer on the one before.

In linear algebra, data are vectors and models are linear maps between them ([vectors and linear maps](/notes/math/vectors-and-linear-maps)). Solving a system is elimination, the factorization $A = LU$ ([linear systems](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems)), and least squares is a projection ([orthogonality](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality)). Eigenvalues describe repeated application ([eigenvalues](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues)), symmetric matrices give the axes of quadratic forms, covariances and Hessians ([symmetric matrices](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices)), and the SVD gives rank, low-rank approximation and PCA for every matrix ([SVD](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition)).

In calculus, derivatives are best linear approximations ([derivatives](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series)). In many variables they become gradients, Jacobians and Hessians chained by matrix products ([gradients](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians)), written compactly for vector and matrix expressions ([matrix calculus](/notes/math/matrix-calculus)); the Jacobian determinant changes variables in integrals ([multiple integrals](/notes/math/multiple-integrals)).

In optimization, convexity turns a zero gradient into a certificate of a global minimum ([convexity](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions)). Gradient descent needs about $\kappa$ steps, and Newton's method removes $\kappa$ near the minimum ([unconstrained optimization](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization)). Constraints are balanced by multipliers in the KKT conditions ([KKT](/notes/math/lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt)), whose dual gives lower bounds and the SVM's form ([duality](/notes/math/duality)).

In numerical computation, the condition number that set the speed of gradient descent also sets how many digits a computer can trust ([conditioning](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability)). When a matrix is too large to factorize, products with it are enough: the power method finds its top eigenvector, and conjugate gradient solves a positive definite system in about $\sqrt\kappa$ steps.
