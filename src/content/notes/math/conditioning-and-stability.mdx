Solve

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.0001 \end{pmatrix} x = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2.0001 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The solution is $x = (1, 1)$. Change the second entry of the right-hand side by $10^{-4}$, to $2.0002$, and the solution becomes $(0, 2)$. A relative change of the data of $3.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ produced a relative change of the answer of $1$, a factor of $28{,}000$.

NumPy computes both solutions correctly to about 12 digits, so nothing went wrong in the arithmetic. The two equations describe two nearly parallel lines, and nearly parallel lines meet at a point that moves far when one of them shifts slightly. If the right-hand side came from a measurement with four significant digits, no algorithm could recover even the first digit of $x$.

Every number in the course so far was computed in floating point and treated as exact. How many digits of an answer do the data support, and how many does the algorithm lose on top of that? Two separate quantities answer this. The **condition number** belongs to the problem and measures how much it amplifies changes in its data. **Backward stability** belongs to the algorithm and says whether it adds more error than the data already carry.

## Floating point in one paragraph

A double-precision number has 53 significant bits, about 16 decimal digits, and an exponent that reaches about $10^{\pm 308}$. Every input is rounded to the nearest such number, and every arithmetic operation is correctly rounded: the computed result $\mathrm{fl}(a \circ b)$ equals $(a \circ b)(1 + \delta)$ with $\lvert\delta\rvert \le u$, for $\circ$ any of $+, -, \times, /$. Here $u$ is the **unit roundoff**, $u = 2^{-53} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-16}$ in double precision. Results above about $1.8 \cdot 10^{308}$ overflow to infinity, and tiny results underflow to zero. The [floating point note](/notes/acos/floating-point#rounding-ulp-and-machine-epsilon) of the computer architecture course derives the bound and the formats. What matters here is the consequence: a datum carries a relative error of up to $u$ before any computation starts, and every operation may add another.

Lower precisions shrink the budget. Single precision has $u = 2^{-24} \approx 6.0 \cdot 10^{-8}$ and overflows above $3.4 \cdot 10^{38}$. In bfloat16, with 8 significant bits, $u = 2^{-8} \approx 3.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$, so a problem that amplifies errors a hundredfold already loses most of its digits: $100u \approx 0.39$, a relative error of 39%. This is why [mixed-precision training](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#mixed-precision-fp16-bf16-and-loss-scaling) keeps accumulations and some reductions in single precision.

## The condition number of a problem

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> The error of a computed answer is about the condition number of the problem times the backward error of the algorithm. A stable algorithm has backward error near $u$, so it loses about $\log_{10}\kappa$ of the 16 digits.</aside>

A problem maps data $x$ to an answer $f(x)$. Its **relative condition number** is the largest amplification of a small relative change in the data:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_f(x) &= \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0}\ \sup_{\lVert\delta x\rVert \le \varepsilon\lVert x\rVert} \frac{\lVert\delta f\rVert / \lVert f(x)\rVert}{\lVert\delta x\rVert / \lVert x\rVert} , \\
\delta f &= f(x + \delta x) - f(x) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where norms are Euclidean. For a function of one variable, the derivative gives the change to first order, and comparing relative changes leaves a ratio that depends only on $f$ and $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + \delta x) - f(x) &= f'(x)\,\delta x + o(\delta x) \\
\frac{f(x + \delta x) - f(x)}{f(x)} &= \frac{x f'(x)}{f(x)} \cdot \frac{\delta x}{x} + o\Big(\frac{\delta x}{x}\Big) \\
\kappa_f(x) &= \left\lvert \frac{x\, f'(x)}{f(x)} \right\rvert .
\end{aligned}
$$

The square root has $\kappa = \lvert x \cdot \frac{1}{2\sqrt x} / \sqrt x\rvert = \frac12$ everywhere, so it halves relative errors. The exponential has $\kappa = \lvert x e^x / e^x\rvert = \lvert x\rvert$, so $e^{100}$ is accurate only to about $100u$. The function $f(x) = x - 1$ has $\kappa = \lvert x/(x - 1)\rvert$, which is huge near $x = 1$.

Take $x = 1.0000001$, where $\kappa = 10^7$. The stored $x$ is off by at most $u$ relative, so $x - 1$ may be off by up to $10^7 u = 1.1 \cdot 10^{-9}$ relative. In double precision $x - 1$ evaluates to $1.0000000005838672 \cdot 10^{-7}$, a relative error of $5.8 \cdot 10^{-10}$, although the subtraction itself was exact (the section on cancellation below shows why). The error came from rounding the input, amplified by the problem.

## The condition number of a matrix

Consider the problem "solve $Ax = b$ for $x$", with $A$ square and invertible, and change $b$ by $\delta b$. For the spectral norm $\lVert A\rVert$, the largest stretch $\sigma_{\max}$ of the [SVD note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition), the relative error is bounded by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\lVert\delta x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert} &\le \kappa(A)\,\frac{\lVert\delta b\rVert}{\lVert b\rVert} , \\
\kappa(A) &= \lVert A\rVert\,\lVert A^{-1}\rVert = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Two inequalities, each from the definition of the matrix norm, multiply to give it:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert\delta x\rVert &= \lVert A^{-1}\delta b\rVert \le \lVert A^{-1}\rVert\,\lVert\delta b\rVert , \\
\lVert b\rVert &= \lVert Ax\rVert \le \lVert A\rVert\,\lVert x\rVert .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first holds because subtracting $Ax = b$ from $A(x + \delta x) = b + \delta b$ leaves $A\,\delta x = \delta b$. Dividing the first inequality by $\lVert x\rVert$ and using $1/\lVert x\rVert \le \lVert A\rVert/\lVert b\rVert$ from the second gives the bound.

The spectral norm is $\lVert A\rVert = \sigma_{\max}$ ([SVD note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition#what-the-singular-values-measure)), and $A^{-1} = V\Sigma^{-1}U^\top$ is an SVD with largest singular value $1/\sigma_{\min}$, so $\lVert A^{-1}\rVert = 1/\sigma_{\min}$; this gives the ratio of singular values. The bound is attained. Take $b = u_1$, the left singular vector of $\sigma_{\max}$, and $\delta b = \varepsilon u_n$, along that of $\sigma_{\min}$. Then $x = v_1/\sigma_{\max}$ and $\delta x = \varepsilon v_n/\sigma_{\min}$, so the ratio of relative changes is $\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$.

Perturbations of $A$ itself obey the same law to first order. Subtract $Ax = b$ from $(A + \delta A)(x + \delta x) = b$ and drop the second-order term $\delta A\,\delta x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A\,\delta x &\approx -\delta A\, x , \\
\lVert\delta x\rVert &\lesssim \lVert A^{-1}\rVert\,\lVert\delta A\rVert\,\lVert x\rVert , \\
\frac{\lVert\delta x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert} &\lesssim \kappa(A)\,\frac{\lVert\delta A\rVert}{\lVert A\rVert} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line multiplies and divides by $\lVert A\rVert$ ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), Lecture 12).

Always $\kappa \ge 1$, since $\sigma_{\max} \ge \sigma_{\min}$; $\kappa = 1$ for orthogonal matrices, whose singular values are all 1, and $\kappa = \infty$ for singular ones, where $\sigma_{\min} = 0$. For the opening matrix the singular values are $2.00005$ and $0.00005$, so $\kappa = 4.0 \cdot 10^4$, and the observed amplification of $28{,}000$ stays below it. The figure above shows the same effect with a milder matrix, $\kappa = 42$.

The bound is normwise. An error of relative size $\varepsilon$ in the norm may sit entirely in a component of $x$ much smaller than $\varepsilon\lVert x\rVert$, which then has no correct digits; componentwise condition numbers exist for that case ([Higham, 2002](#references)). Computing $\kappa$ exactly also costs more than the solve: `numpy.linalg.cond` computes all singular values, an SVD, while LAPACK solvers estimate $\kappa$ cheaply from the factorization they already have.

## The Hilbert matrix

The **Hilbert matrix** $H_{ij} = 1/(i + j - 1)$ is the Gram matrix of the monomials $1, t, \dots, t^{n-1}$ on $[0, 1]$:

$$
\int_0^1 t^{i-1}t^{j-1}\,dt = \frac{1}{i + j - 1} .
$$

It is what the normal equations of polynomial least squares become with many evenly spread points $t_1, \dots, t_N$ in $[0, 1]$: the entry $\frac1N\sum_k t_k^{i+j-2}$ of $\frac1N X^\top X$ is a Riemann sum of the integral above. In the computed values its condition number grows by a factor of about 30 per row, from $524$ at $n = 3$ to $1.6 \cdot 10^{13}$ at $n = 10$ and $5.6 \cdot 10^{17}$ at $n = 13$, beyond $1/u$.

Solve $Hx = b$ by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting, with $b = H(1, \dots, 1)^\top$ computed in floating point, so that the exact solution of the intended problem is the vector of ones:

| $n$ | $\kappa(H)$ | $\kappa u$ | relative error of $\hat x$ | relative residual |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | $5.2 \cdot 10^{2}$ | $5.8 \cdot 10^{-14}$ | $8.0 \cdot 10^{-15}$ | $7.8 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 5 | $4.8 \cdot 10^{5}$ | $5.3 \cdot 10^{-11}$ | $3.5 \cdot 10^{-13}$ | $4.9 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 8 | $1.5 \cdot 10^{10}$ | $1.7 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | $2.2 \cdot 10^{-7}$ | $2.4 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 10 | $1.6 \cdot 10^{13}$ | $1.8 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $1.6 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $2.7 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 12 | $1.7 \cdot 10^{16}$ | $1.9$ | $0.17$ | $5.6 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 13 | $5.6 \cdot 10^{17}$ | $62$ | $2.0$ | $3.3 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |

The error grows with $\kappa$ and stays below $\kappa u$ in every row: at $n = 10$ about four of the sixteen digits survive, at $n = 13$ none. The bound says how bad the error can get; in most rows it is several times smaller. The last column tells a different story, the subject of the next section.

## Backward stability

The relative residual $\lVert b - H\hat x\rVert/(\lVert H\rVert\,\lVert\hat x\rVert)$, computed exactly from the stored numbers, stays below $10^{-16}$ in every row, even where $\hat x$ is entirely wrong. A small residual means that $\hat x$ is the exact solution of a slightly different system. With $r = b - H\hat x$, the matrix

$$
\Delta H = \frac{r\,\hat x^\top}{\lVert\hat x\rVert^2}
$$

satisfies $(H + \Delta H)\hat x = b$, because $\Delta H\,\hat x = r$. A rank-one matrix $r\hat x^\top$ has spectral norm $\lVert r\rVert\,\lVert\hat x\rVert$, its only nonzero singular value, so $\lVert\Delta H\rVert = \lVert r\rVert/\lVert\hat x\rVert$, and $\lVert\Delta H\rVert/\lVert H\rVert$ is exactly that relative residual ([Rigal and Gaches, 1967](#references)). Elimination did its job perfectly, up to rounding; the answer is wrong because the problem is ill-conditioned.

This is the general pattern. An algorithm for $f$ is **backward stable** if its computed answer is the exact answer for slightly perturbed data: $\hat y = f(x + \Delta x)$ with $\lVert\Delta x\rVert/\lVert x\rVert = O(u)$. Such an algorithm makes an error no larger than an error in the data would cause, and the condition number converts the one into the other:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat y - f(x) &= f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) , \\
\frac{\lVert\hat y - f(x)\rVert}{\lVert f(x)\rVert} &\lesssim \kappa_f(x)\,\frac{\lVert\Delta x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert} = \kappa_f(x) \cdot O(u) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line is the definition of $\kappa_f$ applied to the small perturbation $\Delta x$.

Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting is backward stable in practice. Its backward error is a modest multiple of $u$ times the **growth factor**, the largest entry produced during elimination relative to the largest entry of $A$ ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), Lecture 22). The [linear systems note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#row-exchanges-and-pivoting) derives why pivoting bounds the growth by $2^{n-1}$: multipliers at most 1 at most double the entries per step. Contrived matrices reach that bound. On the matrices met in practice the growth stays small; this is an observation, not a theorem (Trefethen and Bau, Lecture 22).

Cholesky factorization $A = LL^\top$ of a positive definite matrix needs no pivoting at all, because its entries cannot grow. The diagonal entry of $A = LL^\top$ in row $i$ is

$$
a_{ii} = \textstyle\sum_{k \le i} l_{ik}^2 ,
$$

so every $\lvert l_{ik}\rvert \le \sqrt{a_{ii}}$. This is why [Gaussian processes](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#regression-the-posterior-in-closed-form) use it.

A stable algorithm cannot rescue an ill-conditioned problem; it can only avoid making things worse. The cure lies in the problem: rescale features, drop near-duplicate ones, or regularize. Adding $\lambda I$ to a positive semidefinite matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_{\max} \ge \dots \ge \lambda_{\min} \ge 0$ shifts every eigenvalue by $\lambda$, so its condition number becomes $(\lambda_{\max} + \lambda)/(\lambda_{\min} + \lambda) \le (\lambda_{\max} + \lambda)/\lambda$. That is one reason why ridge regression and the jitter added to kernel matrices help numerically as well as statistically.

## Least squares: why the normal equations square κ

Least squares, $\min_w\lVert Xw - y\rVert^2$, can be solved through the normal equations $X^\top X w = X^\top y$ of the [least squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations), or through a factorization of $X$ itself. Forming $X^\top X$ squares every singular value, and therefore their ratio. With the thin SVD $X = U\Sigma V^\top$, where $U^\top U = I$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
X^\top X &= V\Sigma U^\top U\Sigma V^\top = V\Sigma^2 V^\top , \\
\kappa(X^\top X) &= \frac{\sigma_{\max}^2}{\sigma_{\min}^2} = \kappa(X)^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

A backward stable solver applied to the normal equations therefore loses about $2\log_{10}\kappa(X)$ digits. The QR factorization $X = QR$ of the [orthogonality note](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality#gram-schmidt-and-qr) avoids the squaring. $Q$ has orthonormal columns, so $\lVert Xv\rVert = \lVert Rv\rVert$ for every $v$ and $\kappa(R) = \kappa(X)$, and the solution comes from the triangular system $Rw = Q^\top y$.

Householder QR and the SVD are backward stable for least squares ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), Lecture 19), so their error is the condition number of the least-squares problem times about $u$. That condition number, for perturbations of $X$, is at most $\kappa + \kappa^2\tan\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $y$ and the column space of $X$ (Trefethen and Bau, Lecture 18; [Bindel, 2025](#references)).

<details>
<summary>Derivation: where κ² tan θ comes from</summary>

Perturb $X$ to $X + \delta X$ in the normal equations $X^\top(y - Xw) = 0$. With the residual $r = y - Xw$, keeping first-order terms,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta X^\top r - X^\top(\delta X\, w + X\,\delta w) &= 0 , \\
\delta w &= (X^\top X)^{-1}\delta X^\top r - X^{+}\delta X\, w ,
\end{aligned}
$$

with $X^{+} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$. The second term is bounded by $\lVert\delta X\rVert\lVert w\rVert/\sigma_{\min}$, giving $\kappa$. In the first, $\lVert(X^\top X)^{-1}\rVert = 1/\sigma_{\min}^2$, $\lVert r\rVert = \lVert y\rVert\sin\theta$ and $\lVert w\rVert \ge \lVert Xw\rVert/\sigma_{\max} = \lVert y\rVert\cos\theta/\sigma_{\max}$, so

$$
\frac{\lVert(X^\top X)^{-1}\delta X^\top r\rVert}{\lVert w\rVert} \le \kappa^2\tan\theta\,\frac{\lVert\delta X\rVert}{\lVert X\rVert} .
$$

</details>

The $\kappa^2$ term belongs to the problem when the residual is large, but it vanishes when the fit is exact ($\theta = 0$), while the normal equations pay $\kappa(X^\top X)\,u = \kappa^2 u$ always.

Take a $100 \times 6$ matrix $X$ with singular values spaced evenly on a log scale from 1 to $1/\kappa(X)$, and the true weights $w = (1, \dots, 1)$. The relative errors of the three solvers:

| $\kappa(X)$ | residual $\lVert y - Xw\rVert$ | normal equations | QR | SVD (`lstsq`) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10^3$ | 0 | $3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ | $8 \cdot 10^{-15}$ | $7 \cdot 10^{-15}$ |
| $10^3$ | $10^{-2}$ | $2 \cdot 10^{-12}$ | $6 \cdot 10^{-14}$ | $5 \cdot 10^{-14}$ |
| $10^6$ | 0 | $7 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | $3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ | $2 \cdot 10^{-12}$ |
| $10^6$ | $10^{-2}$ | $6 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | $4 \cdot 10^{-8}$ | $4 \cdot 10^{-8}$ |

At $\kappa(X) = 10^6$ the normal equations keep five digits; QR and the SVD keep eleven with an exact fit and seven with a residual. The [least squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#when-the-solution-is-unstable) shows the same gap on polynomial features. The formula $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ is for derivations; numerical code factorizes $X$.

## Log-sum-exp and the stable softmax

The softmax, the cross-entropy loss and every mixture model need

$$
\operatorname{lse}(z) = \log\sum_{i=1}^K e^{z_i} .
$$

Computed as written it overflows as soon as one $z_i$ exceeds about 709 in double precision or 88.7 in single precision, and the probabilities $e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$ become $\infty/\infty$. The remedy is to factor out the largest exponential before summing. With $m = \max_i z_i$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\sum_i e^{z_i} &= \log\Big(e^{m}\sum_i e^{z_i - m}\Big) \\
&= m + \log\sum_i e^{z_i - m} , \\
\operatorname{softmax}(z)_i &= \frac{e^{z_i - m}}{\sum_j e^{z_j - m}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Every shifted exponent is at most 0, so the sum cannot overflow, and the term with $z_i = m$ equals 1, so it cannot be zero. The softmax is unchanged by the shift because the factor $e^{-m}$ cancels between numerator and denominator. The sum lies between 1 and $K$, which also bounds the result:

$$
m \le \operatorname{lse}(z) \le m + \log K .
$$

For $z = (1000, 999, 998)$ the naive formulas give $\operatorname{lse} = \infty$ and the probabilities $(\text{nan}, \text{nan}, \text{nan})$. The shifted ones give $1000 + \log(1 + e^{-1} + e^{-2}) = 1000.4076$ and $(0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$, the softmax of $(0, -1, -2)$. The bounds make lse a smooth maximum; it is convex, as shown in the [convexity note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-second-order-condition-a-positive-semidefinite-hessian), and its gradient is the softmax: by the chain rule, $\partial\operatorname{lse}/\partial z_i = e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$. The deep learning course works through the same shift in single precision for the cross-entropy loss, including the underflow of very small probabilities ([why log-softmax is numerically stable](/notes/deep-learning/losses-and-regularization#why-log-softmax-is-numerically-stable)).

The two-class case is the **softplus** $\log(1 + e^t)$, the logistic loss of a margin. The same factoring, $\log(1 + e^t) = t + \log(1 + e^{-t})$ for $t \ge 0$, gives

$$
\log(1 + e^t) = \max(t, 0) + \log\big(1 + e^{-\lvert t\rvert}\big) ,
$$

and the last logarithm should be computed by `log1p`, which is accurate for tiny arguments where $1 + e^{-\lvert t\rvert}$ would round to 1. At $t = 800$ the naive formula gives $\infty$ and the stable one $800$. At $t = -40$ the naive formula gives $0$, because $1 + 4.2 \cdot 10^{-18}$ rounds to 1, and the stable one gives $4.25 \cdot 10^{-18}$.

## Catastrophic cancellation

Subtraction of two nearly equal numbers is the most common source of lost digits. The absolute errors of the operands survive the subtraction, but the result is small, so the relative error is large. If $\hat a = a(1 + \varepsilon_1)$ and $\hat b = b(1 + \varepsilon_2)$ with $\lvert\varepsilon_i\rvert \le \varepsilon$, then $(\hat a - \hat b) - (a - b) = a\varepsilon_1 - b\varepsilon_2$, and the triangle inequality gives

$$
\frac{\lvert(\hat a - \hat b) - (a - b)\rvert}{\lvert a - b\rvert} \le \varepsilon\,\frac{\lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert}{\lvert a - b\rvert} .
$$

The factor $(\lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert)/\lvert a - b\rvert$ is the condition number of subtraction when both operands carry errors. In the example $x - 1$ above it is $2 \cdot 10^7$, twice the $\kappa = 10^7$ found there, where only $x$ was perturbed.

The subtraction itself is exact when $b \le a \le 2b$, for positive doubles $a$ and $b$. Let $2^e \le b \lt 2^{e+1}$. Doubles in that range are spaced $2^{e-52}$ apart, and doubles above it are spaced by multiples of that, so both $a$ and $b$ are integer multiples of $2^{e-52}$. Then $a - b = k \cdot 2^{e-52}$ with $0 \le a - b \le b \lt 2^{e+1}$, so $k \lt 2^{53}$: the difference fits in 53 bits and is itself a double, which correct rounding returns unchanged. The damage comes from errors made earlier.

Take $(1 - \cos x)/x^2$. Its limit at 0 is $\frac12$, and the problem is well conditioned there. The Taylor series $\cos x = 1 - x^2/2 + x^4/24 - \dots$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= \tfrac12 - \tfrac{x^2}{24} + O(x^4) , \\
\kappa_f(x) &= \left\lvert\frac{x f'(x)}{f(x)}\right\rvert \approx \frac{x^2/12}{1/2} = \frac{x^2}{6} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The formula as written is not:

| $x$ | $(1 - \cos x)/x^2$ | correct digits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $10^{-4}$ | $0.49999999696$ | 8 |
| $10^{-6}$ | $0.50004$ | 4 |
| $10^{-8}$ | $0$ | 0 |

At $x = 10^{-8}$, $\cos x = 1 - 5 \cdot 10^{-17}$ rounds to exactly 1. The algorithm is unstable for a well-conditioned problem: the rounding error of $\cos x$, at most $u$, meets the condition number $(1 + \cos x)/(1 - \cos x) \approx 4/x^2$ of the subtraction. The identity $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$ removes the subtraction, and $2\sin^2(x/2)/x^2$ is correct to full precision at all three points: $0.49999999958333$, $0.49999999999996$ and $0.5$, matching $\frac12 - x^2/24$. Finite-difference derivatives suffer from the same cancellation, which is why their error grows again for very small steps ([finite differences](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series#finite-differences-and-their-error)).

The one-pass formula for the variance, $\operatorname{Var} = E[x^2] - (E[x])^2$, subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers. For the data $10^8 + (1, 2, 3)$ the squares are about $10^{16}$, where neighbouring doubles are 2 apart, and the formula returns $0$ instead of $\frac23$. The two-pass formula, which subtracts the mean first and then averages the squared deviations, returns $0.6667$. For streaming data, Welford's update ([Welford, 1962](#references)) keeps a running mean and a running sum of squared deviations, so it never forms the large squares ([Higham, 2002](#references), §1.9).

The same care applies to the batch statistics of the [normalization layers](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#batch-normalization-in-training-and-in-evaluation) of deep learning, where activations with a large mean and a small spread are common and the arithmetic is often single or half precision.

## What you should remember

- Every input and operation carries a relative error up to $u$: $1.1 \cdot 10^{-16}$ in double, $6 \cdot 10^{-8}$ in single, $3.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$ in bfloat16.
- $\kappa_f = \lvert xf'(x)/f(x)\rvert$ for one variable; $\kappa(A) = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ for $Ax = b$.
- Error $\approx \kappa \times$ backward error; a stable algorithm loses about $\log_{10}\kappa$ digits.
- A small residual means a small backward error, not an accurate solution.
- $\kappa(X^\top X) = \kappa(X)^2$: solve least squares with QR or the SVD.
- Shift log-sum-exp by the maximum; rewrite formulas that subtract nearly equal numbers.

    for k in range(n):
        p = max(range(k, n), key=lambda i: abs(A[i][k]))
        A[k], A[p] = A[p], A[k]; b[k], b[p] = b[p], b[k]
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            m = A[i][k] / A[k][k]
            for j in range(k, n):
                A[i][j] = A[i][j] - m * A[k][j]
            b[i] = b[i] - m * b[k]
    x = [0.0] * n
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        s = b[i]
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            s = s - A[i][j] * x[j]
        x[i] = s / A[i][i]
    return x
def power(M, it=2000):                                       # the largest eigenvalue of a symmetric positive definite matrix
    n = len(M); v = [1.0] * n; lam = 0.0
    for _ in range(it):
        w = [dot(M[i], v) for i in range(n)]
        lam = dot(w, v) / dot(v, v)
        s = max(abs(t) for t in w); v = [t / s for t in w]
    return lam
def hilbert_inverse(n):                                      # the exact inverse of the Hilbert matrix has integer entries
    return [[(-1) ** (i + j) * (i + j - 1) * comb(n + i - 1, n - j) * comb(n + j - 1, n - i) * comb(i + j - 2, i - 1) ** 2
             for j in range(1, n + 1)] for i in range(1, n + 1)]

# the Hilbert matrix H_ij = 1/(i + j - 1): solve H x = H 1 and compare the error with kappa * u
norm = lambda v: dot(v, v) ** 0.5
for n in range(3, 14):
    H = [[1.0 / (i + j + 1) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    kappa = power(H) * power([[float(t) for t in row] for row in hilbert_inverse(n)])   # lambda_max(H) / lambda_min(H)
    b = [dot(H[i], [1.0] * n) for i in range(n)]
    xh = gepp(H, b)
    err = norm([t - 1 for t in xh]) / n ** 0.5
    r = [float(Fraction(b[i]) - sum(Fraction(H[i][j]) * Fraction(xh[j]) for j in range(n))) for i in range(n)]
    res = norm(r) / (power(H) * norm(xh))                    # exact residual of the stored data, relative: a backward error
    print(n, f'{kappa:.2e} {kappa * u:.1e} {err:.1e} {res:.1e}')

## Next

Every method in this note factorizes the matrix: elimination, Cholesky, QR and the SVD cost on the order of $n^3$ operations and $n^2$ numbers of memory. For a million unknowns that is 8 TB of storage, while a sparse matrix of the same size with about five nonzeros per row fits in 64 MB and is multiplied by a vector in milliseconds. The [next note](/notes/math/iterative-methods) computes with such products alone. The power method finds the largest eigenvalue, and conjugate gradient solves a positive definite system in a number of steps set by the square root of the condition number met here.
