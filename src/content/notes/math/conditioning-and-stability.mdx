The Optimization section ended with the [duality gap](/notes/math/duality#the-duality-gap-as-a-stopping-criterion), a certificate computed from two numbers. Every number in the course so far was computed in floating point and treated as exact. Mostly that is harmless, but a Newton step solves a linear system whose matrix can be nearly singular, least squares can be solved in two ways that differ by many digits of accuracy, and the softmax of the maximum-entropy solution overflows for large scores. This note separates two sources of error: how sensitive the **problem** is to small changes in its data, measured by a condition number, and whether the **algorithm** adds more error than the problem deserves, measured by its backward stability. Their product estimates the accuracy of any computed answer.

**Notation.** $u$ is the **unit roundoff**, $u = 2^{-53} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-16}$ in double precision. $\mathrm{fl}(\cdot)$ is the result of a computation in floating point, and $\hat x$ a computed approximation of the exact $x$. The **relative error** of $\hat x$ is $\lVert\hat x - x\rVert/\lVert x\rVert$. Norms are Euclidean, and for a matrix $\lVert A\rVert$ is the spectral norm, the largest singular value $\sigma_{\max}$ of the [SVD note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition). $\kappa$ is a condition number; vectors are lowercase letters and matrices capital letters, as throughout the course.

## Floating point in one paragraph

A double-precision number has 53 significant bits, about 16 decimal digits, and an exponent that reaches about $10^{\pm 308}$. Every input is rounded to the nearest such number, and every arithmetic operation is correctly rounded: $\mathrm{fl}(a \circ b) = (a \circ b)(1 + \delta)$ with $\lvert\delta\rvert \le u$, for $\circ$ any of $+, -, \times, /$. In single precision $u = 2^{-24} \approx 6.0 \cdot 10^{-8}$, and in bfloat16, with 8 significant bits, $u = 2^{-8} \approx 3.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$. Results above the largest number, about $1.8 \cdot 10^{308}$ in double and $3.4 \cdot 10^{38}$ in single precision, overflow to infinity, and tiny results underflow to zero. The [floating point note](/notes/acos/floating-point#rounding-ulp-and-machine-epsilon) of the computer architecture course derives the bound $u$, the formats and the special values. What matters here is the consequence: a datum carries a relative error of up to $u$ before any computation starts, and every operation may add another.

## Baseline: a small change in the data, a large change in the answer

Solve

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.0001 \end{pmatrix} x = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2.0001 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The solution is $x = (1, 1)$. Change the second entry of the right-hand side by $10^{-4}$, to $2.0002$, and the solution becomes $(0, 2)$: a relative change of the data of $3.5 \cdot 10^{-5}$ produced a relative change of the answer of $1$, a factor of $28{,}000$. NumPy's solver computes both solutions correctly to about 12 digits. Nothing went wrong in the arithmetic; the two equations describe two nearly parallel lines, and nearly parallel lines meet at a point that moves far when one of them shifts slightly. If the right-hand side came from a measurement with four significant digits, no algorithm could recover even the first digit of $x$. The question is how to tell, before trusting an answer, how many of its digits the data support, and how many the algorithm loses on top of that.

## The condition number of a problem

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Accuracy has two factors. The condition number $\kappa$ belongs to the problem: it says how much a relative change of the data can change the answer. Backward stability belongs to the algorithm: a stable algorithm returns the exact answer to data perturbed by about $u$. Their product, $\kappa \cdot u$, estimates the relative error of the result, so about $\log_{10}\kappa$ of the 16 digits are lost whatever stable algorithm is used, and unstable formulas can lose more.</aside>

A problem maps data $x$ to an answer $f(x)$. Its **relative condition number** is the largest amplification of a small relative change:

$$
\kappa_f(x) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0}\ \sup_{\lVert\delta x\rVert \le \varepsilon\lVert x\rVert} \frac{\lVert f(x + \delta x) - f(x)\rVert / \lVert f(x)\rVert}{\lVert\delta x\rVert / \lVert x\rVert} .
$$

The idea for one variable: to first order a change $\delta x$ changes $f$ by $f'(x)\,\delta x$, and comparing relative changes leaves a ratio that depends only on $f$ and $x$:

$$
\boxed{\; \kappa_f(x) = \left\lvert \frac{x\, f'(x)}{f(x)} \right\rvert . \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x + \delta x) - f(x) &= f'(x)\,\delta x + o(\delta x) && \text{the derivative as the best linear approximation} \\
\frac{f(x + \delta x) - f(x)}{f(x)} &= \frac{x f'(x)}{f(x)} \cdot \frac{\delta x}{x} + o\Big(\frac{\delta x}{x}\Big) && \text{divide by } f(x) \text{, multiply and divide by } x .
\end{aligned}
$$

Examples: $\sqrt x$ has $\kappa = \frac12$ everywhere, so square roots halve relative errors; $e^x$ has $\kappa = \lvert x\rvert$, so $e^{100}$ is accurate only to about $100u$; and $f(x) = x - 1$ has $\kappa = \lvert x/(x - 1)\rvert$, which is huge near $x = 1$. **Worked example.** For $x = 1.0000001$, $\kappa = 10^7$. The stored $x$ is off by at most $u$ relative, so $x - 1$ may be off by up to $10^7 u = 1.1 \cdot 10^{-9}$ relative. In double precision $x - 1$ evaluates to $1.0000000005838672 \cdot 10^{-7}$, a relative error of $5.8 \cdot 10^{-10}$, although the subtraction itself was exact. The error came from rounding the input, amplified by the problem.

## The condition number of a matrix

For the problem "solve $Ax = b$ for $x$", with $A$ square and invertible, the idea is that a change $\delta b$ moves the solution by $A^{-1}\delta b$, which is at most $\lVert A^{-1}\rVert$ times larger, while $b = Ax$ is at most $\lVert A\rVert$ times larger than $x$; the two factors multiply:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\lVert\delta x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert} &\le \kappa(A)\,\frac{\lVert\delta b\rVert}{\lVert b\rVert} , \\
\kappa(A) &= \lVert A\rVert\,\lVert A^{-1}\rVert = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)} .
\end{aligned}
}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(x + \delta x) = b + \delta b \ &\Longrightarrow\ \delta x = A^{-1}\delta b,\quad \lVert\delta x\rVert \le \lVert A^{-1}\rVert\,\lVert\delta b\rVert && \text{subtract } Ax = b \text{; definition of the matrix norm} \\
\lVert b\rVert = \lVert Ax\rVert \le \lVert A\rVert\,\lVert x\rVert \ &\Longrightarrow\ \frac{1}{\lVert x\rVert} \le \frac{\lVert A\rVert}{\lVert b\rVert} && \text{the same definition} \\
\frac{\lVert\delta x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert} &\le \lVert A\rVert\,\lVert A^{-1}\rVert\,\frac{\lVert\delta b\rVert}{\lVert b\rVert} && \text{multiply the two inequalities.}
\end{aligned}
$$

In the SVD $A = U\Sigma V^\top$, $\lVert A\rVert = \sigma_{\max}$ and $\lVert A^{-1}\rVert = 1/\sigma_{\min}$, which gives the ratio of singular values. The bound is attained: take $b$ along the left singular vector of $\sigma_{\max}$ and $\delta b$ along that of $\sigma_{\min}$. Perturbations of $A$ itself obey the same law to first order, $\lVert\delta x\rVert/\lVert x\rVert \lesssim \kappa(A)\,\lVert\delta A\rVert/\lVert A\rVert$ ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), Lecture 12). Always $\kappa \ge 1$; $\kappa = 1$ for orthogonal matrices, and $\kappa = \infty$ for singular ones. For the baseline matrix the singular values are $2.00005$ and $0.00005$, $\kappa = 4.0 \cdot 10^4$, and the observed amplification of $28{,}000$ stays below it. The figure shows the same effect with a milder matrix, $\kappa = 42$.

## The Hilbert matrix

The **Hilbert matrix** $H_{ij} = 1/(i + j - 1)$ is the Gram matrix of the monomials $1, t, \dots, t^{n-1}$ on $[0, 1]$, since $\int_0^1 t^{i-1}t^{j-1}\,dt = 1/(i + j - 1)$: it is what the normal equations of polynomial least squares become with many evenly spread points. Its condition number grows by a factor of about 30 per row, from $524$ at $n = 3$ to $1.6 \cdot 10^{13}$ at $n = 10$ and $5.6 \cdot 10^{17}$ at $n = 13$, beyond $1/u$. Solve $Hx = b$ with $b = H(1, \dots, 1)^\top$ computed in floating point, so that the exact solution of the intended problem is the vector of ones, by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting:

| $n$ | $\kappa(H)$ | $\kappa u$ | relative error of $\hat x$ | relative residual |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | $5.2 \cdot 10^{2}$ | $5.8 \cdot 10^{-14}$ | $8.0 \cdot 10^{-15}$ | $7.8 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 5 | $4.8 \cdot 10^{5}$ | $5.3 \cdot 10^{-11}$ | $3.5 \cdot 10^{-13}$ | $4.9 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 8 | $1.5 \cdot 10^{10}$ | $1.7 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | $2.2 \cdot 10^{-7}$ | $2.4 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 10 | $1.6 \cdot 10^{13}$ | $1.8 \cdot 10^{-3}$ | $1.6 \cdot 10^{-4}$ | $2.7 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 12 | $1.7 \cdot 10^{16}$ | $1.9$ | $0.17$ | $5.6 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |
| 13 | $5.6 \cdot 10^{17}$ | $62$ | $2.0$ | $3.3 \cdot 10^{-17}$ |

## Backward stability

The relative residual $\lVert b - H\hat x\rVert/(\lVert H\rVert\,\lVert\hat x\rVert)$, computed exactly from the stored numbers, stays below $10^{-16}$ in every row, even where $\hat x$ is entirely wrong. A small residual means that $\hat x$ is the exact solution of a slightly different system: with $r = b - H\hat x$, the matrix $H + \Delta H$ with $\Delta H = r\hat x^\top/\lVert\hat x\rVert^2$ satisfies $(H + \Delta H)\hat x = b$, and $\lVert\Delta H\rVert/\lVert H\rVert$ is exactly that relative residual ([Rigal and Gaches, 1967](#references)). Elimination did its job perfectly, up to rounding; the answer is wrong because the problem is ill-conditioned.

This is the general pattern. An algorithm for $f$ is **backward stable** if its computed answer is the exact answer for slightly perturbed data, $\hat y = f(x + \Delta x)$ with $\lVert\Delta x\rVert/\lVert x\rVert = O(u)$. The idea: a backward stable algorithm makes an error no larger than an error in the data would cause, so the condition number converts its backward error into the forward error:

$$
\boxed{\; \frac{\lVert\hat y - f(x)\rVert}{\lVert f(x)\rVert} \lesssim \kappa_f(x) \cdot \frac{\lVert\Delta x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert} = \kappa_f(x) \cdot O(u) . \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat y - f(x) &= f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) && \text{backward stability: } \hat y \text{ is exact for } x + \Delta x \\
\frac{\lVert\hat y - f(x)\rVert}{\lVert f(x)\rVert} &\le \kappa_f(x)\,\frac{\lVert\Delta x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert}\,\big(1 + o(1)\big) && \text{definition of } \kappa_f \text{ for a small perturbation.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting is backward stable in practice: its backward error is bounded by a modest multiple of $u$ times the **growth factor**, the largest entry produced during elimination relative to the largest entry of $A$. The growth factor can reach $2^{n-1}$ on contrived matrices but stays small on the matrices met in practice ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), Lecture 22; [Higham, 2002](#references), Chapter 9). Pivoting is what keeps it small, as the [linear systems note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#row-exchanges-and-pivoting) shows on a $2 \times 2$ example; Cholesky factorization of a positive definite matrix needs no pivoting at all, which is why [Gaussian processes](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#regression-the-posterior-in-closed-form) use it. A stable algorithm cannot rescue an ill-conditioned problem; it can only avoid making things worse. The cure for ill-conditioning lies in the problem: rescale features, drop near-duplicate ones, or regularize. Adding $\lambda I$ to a positive semidefinite matrix with largest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$ caps its condition number at $(\lambda_{\max} + \lambda)/\lambda$, which is one reason ridge regression and the jitter added to kernel matrices help numerically as well as statistically.

## Least squares: why the normal equations square κ

Least squares, $\min_w\lVert Xw - y\rVert^2$, can be solved through the normal equations $X^\top X w = X^\top y$ of the [least squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations), or through a factorization of $X$ itself. The idea: forming $X^\top X$ squares every singular value, and therefore squares their ratio.

$$
\boxed{\; \kappa(X^\top X) = \kappa(X)^2 . \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
X &= U\Sigma V^\top && \text{thin SVD, } U^\top U = I,\ \Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_d) \\
X^\top X &= V\Sigma U^\top U\Sigma V^\top = V\Sigma^2 V^\top && \text{transpose of a product; } U^\top U = I \\
\kappa(X^\top X) &= \frac{\sigma_{\max}^2}{\sigma_{\min}^2} = \kappa(X)^2 && \text{the singular values of } X^\top X \text{ are the } \sigma_i^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

A backward stable solver applied to the normal equations therefore loses about $2\log_{10}\kappa(X)$ digits. The QR factorization $X = QR$ of the [orthogonality note](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality#gram-schmidt-and-qr) avoids the squaring: $Q$ has orthonormal columns, so $\lVert Xv\rVert = \lVert Rv\rVert$ for every $v$ and $\kappa(R) = \kappa(X)$, and the solution comes from the triangular system $Rw = Q^\top y$. Householder QR and the SVD are backward stable for least squares, and their error is about $\big(\kappa + \kappa^2\tan\theta/\eta\big)u$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $y$ and the column space of $X$ and $\eta = \lVert X\rVert\lVert w\rVert/\lVert Xw\rVert \ge 1$ ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), Theorem 18.1 and Lecture 19). The $\kappa^2$ term is part of the problem when the residual is large, but it vanishes when the fit is close, while the normal equations pay $\kappa^2 u$ always.

**Worked example.** A $100 \times 6$ matrix $X$ with singular values spaced evenly on a log scale from 1 to $1/\kappa(X)$ and the true weights $w = (1, \dots, 1)$ (the Code section):

| $\kappa(X)$ | residual $\lVert y - Xw\rVert$ | normal equations | QR | SVD (`lstsq`) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $10^3$ | 0 | $3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ | $8 \cdot 10^{-15}$ | $7 \cdot 10^{-15}$ |
| $10^3$ | $10^{-2}$ | $2 \cdot 10^{-12}$ | $6 \cdot 10^{-14}$ | $5 \cdot 10^{-14}$ |
| $10^6$ | 0 | $7 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | $3 \cdot 10^{-12}$ | $2 \cdot 10^{-12}$ |
| $10^6$ | $10^{-2}$ | $6 \cdot 10^{-6}$ | $4 \cdot 10^{-8}$ | $4 \cdot 10^{-8}$ |

At $\kappa(X) = 10^6$ the normal equations keep five digits, QR and the SVD eleven with an exact fit and seven with a residual. The [least squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#when-the-solution-is-unstable) shows the same gap on polynomial features. The formula $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ is for derivations; numerical code factorizes $X$.

## Log-sum-exp and the stable softmax

The softmax, the cross-entropy loss and every mixture model need $\operatorname{lse}(z) = \log\sum_{i=1}^K e^{z_i}$. Computed as written it overflows as soon as one $z_i$ exceeds about 709 in double precision or 88.7 in single precision, and the probabilities $e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j}$ become $\infty/\infty$. The idea: factor out the largest exponential before summing, so that every remaining term lies in $(0, 1]$ and at least one equals 1. With $m = \max_i z_i$,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{lse}(z) &= m + \log\sum_{i=1}^K e^{z_i - m} , \\
m &\le \operatorname{lse}(z) \le m + \log K , \\
\operatorname{softmax}(z)_i &= \frac{e^{z_i - m}}{\sum_j e^{z_j - m}} .
\end{aligned}
}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log\sum_i e^{z_i} &= \log\Big(e^{m}\sum_i e^{z_i - m}\Big) && \text{factor } e^m \text{ out of every term} \\
&= m + \log\sum_i e^{z_i - m} && \log(ab) = \log a + \log b \\
1 \le \sum_i e^{z_i - m} \le K\ &\Longrightarrow\ m \le \operatorname{lse}(z) \le m + \log K && \text{the term with } z_i = m \text{ is } 1 \text{, every term is at most } 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The shifted sum can neither overflow, since every exponent is at most 0, nor be zero, since one term is 1. The softmax is unchanged by the shift because the factor $e^{-m}$ cancels between numerator and denominator. **Numbers:** for $z = (1000, 999, 998)$ the naive formulas give $\operatorname{lse} = \infty$ and the probabilities $(\text{nan}, \text{nan}, \text{nan})$; the shifted ones give $1000 + \log(1 + e^{-1} + e^{-2}) = 1000.4076$ and $(0.665, 0.245, 0.090)$, the softmax of $(0, -1, -2)$. The bounds make lse a smooth maximum; it is convex, as shown in the [convexity note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-second-order-condition-a-positive-semidefinite-hessian), and its gradient is the softmax. The deep learning course works through the same shift in single precision for the cross-entropy loss, including the underflow of very small probabilities ([why log-softmax is numerically stable](/notes/deep-learning/losses-and-regularization#why-log-softmax-is-numerically-stable)).

The two-class case is the **softplus** $\log(1 + e^t)$, the logistic loss of a margin. The same factoring gives $\log(1 + e^t) = \max(t, 0) + \log(1 + e^{-\lvert t\rvert})$, and the last logarithm should be computed by `log1p`, which is accurate for tiny arguments where $1 + e^{-\lvert t\rvert}$ would round to 1. At $t = 800$ the naive formula gives $\infty$ and the stable one $800$; at $t = -40$ the naive formula gives $0$, because $1 + 4.2 \cdot 10^{-18}$ rounds to 1, and the stable one $4.25 \cdot 10^{-18}$.

## Catastrophic cancellation

Subtraction of two nearly equal numbers is the most common source of lost digits. The idea: the absolute errors of the operands survive the subtraction, but the result is small, so the relative error is large. If $\hat a = a(1 + \varepsilon_1)$ and $\hat b = b(1 + \varepsilon_2)$ with $\lvert\varepsilon_i\rvert \le \varepsilon$,

$$
\boxed{\; \frac{\lvert(\hat a - \hat b) - (a - b)\rvert}{\lvert a - b\rvert} \le \varepsilon\,\frac{\lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert}{\lvert a - b\rvert} , \;}
$$

because $(\hat a - \hat b) - (a - b) = a\varepsilon_1 - b\varepsilon_2$, whose absolute value is at most $\varepsilon(\lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert)$ by the triangle inequality. The factor $(\lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert)/\lvert a - b\rvert$ is the condition number of subtraction; it was $2 \cdot 10^7$ in the example $x - 1$ above. The subtraction itself is exact when $a$ and $b$ are within a factor of 2 of each other; the damage comes from errors made earlier.

**Worked example: $(1 - \cos x)/x^2$.** The limit at 0 is $\frac12$, and the problem is well conditioned there: $\kappa \approx x^2/6$. The formula as written is not. At $x = 10^{-4}$ it gives $0.49999999696$, eight correct digits; at $x = 10^{-6}$, $0.50004$, four; at $x = 10^{-8}$, $0$, because $\cos 10^{-8} = 1 - 5 \cdot 10^{-17}$ rounds to exactly 1. The algorithm is unstable for a well-conditioned problem: the rounding error of $\cos x$, at most $u$, meets the condition number $2/(1 - \cos x) \approx 4/x^2$ of the subtraction. The identity $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$ removes the subtraction, and $2\sin^2(x/2)/x^2$ gives $0.5$ to full precision at all three points. Finite-difference derivatives suffer from the same cancellation, which is why their error grows again for very small steps ([finite differences](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series#finite-differences-and-their-error)).

**Worked example: the variance.** The one-pass formula $\operatorname{Var} = E[x^2] - (E[x])^2$ subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers. For the data $10^8 + (1, 2, 3)$ the squares are about $10^{16}$, where neighbouring doubles are 2 apart, and the formula returns $0$ instead of $\frac23$. The two-pass formula, which subtracts the mean first and then averages the squared deviations, returns $0.6667$; for streaming data Welford's update keeps a running mean and a running sum of squared deviations with the same effect ([Higham, 2002](#references), §1.9). The same care applies to the batch statistics of the [normalization layers](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#batch-normalization-in-training-and-in-evaluation) of deep learning, where activations with a large mean and a small spread are common and the arithmetic is often single or half precision.

## What changed

| Computation | Formula as written | Stable version | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $Ax = b$ | $x = A^{-1}b$ | LU with partial pivoting, Cholesky if $A \succ 0$ | backward stable, cheaper |
| least squares | normal equations, error $\sim\kappa^2 u$ | QR or SVD of $X$, error $\sim\kappa u$ for a close fit | no squaring of $\kappa$ |
| $\log\sum_i e^{z_i}$, softmax | overflows above 709 (double) | shift by $m = \max_i z_i$ | exponents at most 0 |
| $\log(1 + e^t)$ | $\infty$ or $0$ | $\max(t, 0) + \operatorname{log1p}(e^{-\lvert t\rvert})$ | no overflow, no lost tiny terms |
| $(1 - \cos x)/x^2$ | $0$ at $x = 10^{-8}$ | $2\sin^2(x/2)/x^2$ | no cancellation |
| variance | $E[x^2] - (E[x])^2$ | two-pass or Welford | no cancellation |

## Limitations

- **Condition numbers are bounds.** The error is often well below $\kappa u$, as in most rows of the Hilbert table; $\kappa$ says how bad it can get, not how bad it is.
- **Normwise measures hide structure.** A relative error in the norm can conceal small components computed with no correct digits; componentwise condition numbers and error bounds exist for that ([Higham, 2002](#references), Chapter 7).
- **Computing κ exactly costs more than the solve.** Practical codes estimate it cheaply from the factorization; `numpy.linalg.cond` computes a full SVD.
- **Low precision changes the scale of everything.** In bfloat16, with $u = 3.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$, a condition number of 100 already costs most of the digits, which is why mixed-precision training keeps accumulations and some reductions in single precision ([mixed precision](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#mixed-precision-fp16-bf16-and-loss-scaling)).
- **Stability is a property of the whole program.** A stable algorithm fed with cancelled or overflowed inputs returns garbage just as reliably.

## What you should remember

- Floating point: every input and every operation carries a relative error up to $u = 2^{-53} \approx 1.1 \cdot 10^{-16}$ (double), $6 \cdot 10^{-8}$ (single), $3.9 \cdot 10^{-3}$ (bfloat16).
- The condition number of a problem bounds the amplification of relative errors; for $f$ of one variable $\kappa = \lvert xf'(x)/f(x)\rvert$, for $Ax = b$ $\kappa(A) = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$.
- A backward stable algorithm gives the exact answer to data perturbed by $O(u)$; its error is then about $\kappa u$, so roughly $\log_{10}\kappa$ digits are lost. A small residual does not mean an accurate solution.
- $\kappa(X^\top X) = \kappa(X)^2$: solve least squares with QR or the SVD, not the normal equations.
- Compute log-sum-exp and softmax after subtracting the maximum, softplus as $\max(t, 0) + \operatorname{log1p}(e^{-\lvert t\rvert})$.
- Subtracting nearly equal numbers amplifies earlier errors by $(\lvert a\rvert + \lvert b\rvert)/\lvert a - b\rvert$; rewrite formulas to avoid it.

## Code

The baseline system, the subtraction near 1, the Hilbert matrices with Gaussian elimination written out, least squares three ways, cancellation, and the stable log-sum-exp and softplus. The elimination, the power iteration and the sums use plain loops so that the figure, which repeats them, gets the same digits. Exact digits of the least-squares experiment can differ slightly across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import comb
from fractions import Fraction
u = 2.0 ** -53                                               # unit roundoff of double precision

# a nearly singular 2 x 2 system: a change of 1e-4 in b moves the solution from (1, 1) to (0, 2)
A = np.array([[1, 1], [1, 1.0001]])
print(np.linalg.solve(A, [2, 2.0001]), np.linalg.solve(A, [2, 2.0002]).round(10), round(np.linalg.cond(A)))
# [1. 1.] [0. 2.] 40002

# subtraction near 1 is ill conditioned: kappa = |x / (x - 1)| = 1e7 amplifies the representation error of x, at most u
x = 1.0000001
print(x - 1, abs((x - 1) - 1e-7) / 1e-7)
# 1.0000000005838672e-07 5.838672220806777e-10

# Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting, written out so that the figure can repeat it operation by operation;
# sums run left to right (Python's built-in sum() compensates rounding errors, which would change the digits)
def dot(p, q):
    s = 0.0
    for a, c in zip(p, q):
        s += a * c
    return s
def gepp(A, b):
    n = len(A); A = [row[:] for row in A]; b = b[:]
    for k in range(n):
        p = max(range(k, n), key=lambda i: abs(A[i][k]))
        A[k], A[p] = A[p], A[k]; b[k], b[p] = b[p], b[k]
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            m = A[i][k] / A[k][k]
            for j in range(k, n):
                A[i][j] = A[i][j] - m * A[k][j]
            b[i] = b[i] - m * b[k]
    x = [0.0] * n
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        s = b[i]
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            s = s - A[i][j] * x[j]
        x[i] = s / A[i][i]
    return x
def power(M, it=2000):                                       # the largest eigenvalue of a symmetric positive definite matrix
    n = len(M); v = [1.0] * n; lam = 0.0
    for _ in range(it):
        w = [dot(M[i], v) for i in range(n)]
        lam = dot(w, v) / dot(v, v)
        s = max(abs(t) for t in w); v = [t / s for t in w]
    return lam
def hilbert_inverse(n):                                      # the exact inverse of the Hilbert matrix has integer entries
    return [[(-1) ** (i + j) * (i + j - 1) * comb(n + i - 1, n - j) * comb(n + j - 1, n - i) * comb(i + j - 2, i - 1) ** 2
             for j in range(1, n + 1)] for i in range(1, n + 1)]

# the Hilbert matrix H_ij = 1/(i + j - 1): solve H x = H 1 and compare the error with kappa * u
norm = lambda v: dot(v, v) ** 0.5
for n in range(3, 14):
    H = [[1.0 / (i + j + 1) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    kappa = power(H) * power([[float(t) for t in row] for row in hilbert_inverse(n)])   # lambda_max(H) / lambda_min(H)
    b = [dot(H[i], [1.0] * n) for i in range(n)]
    xh = gepp(H, b)
    err = norm([t - 1 for t in xh]) / n ** 0.5
    r = [float(Fraction(b[i]) - sum(Fraction(H[i][j]) * Fraction(xh[j]) for j in range(n))) for i in range(n)]
    res = norm(r) / (power(H) * norm(xh))                    # exact residual of the stored data, relative: a backward error
    print(n, f'{kappa:.2e} {kappa * u:.1e} {err:.1e} {res:.1e}')
```

## The course in one paragraph

Machine learning is written in linear algebra, differentiated with calculus, trained by optimization and run in floating point, and this course built each layer on the previous one. Data are vectors and models are linear maps between them ([vectors and linear maps](/notes/math/vectors-and-linear-maps)); solving the resulting systems is elimination, a factorization $A = LU$ ([linear systems](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems)); inner products give lengths, angles and projections, and least squares is a projection ([orthogonality](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality)); eigenvalues describe what a matrix does when applied repeatedly ([eigenvalues](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues)); symmetric matrices have orthogonal eigenvectors that become the axes of quadratic forms, covariances and Hessians ([symmetric matrices](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices)); and the SVD, which exists for every matrix, gives rank, low-rank approximation, the pseudoinverse and PCA ([SVD](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition)). Derivatives are best linear approximations ([derivatives](/notes/math/derivatives-and-taylor-series)), which in many variables become gradients, Jacobians and Hessians chained by matrix products ([gradients](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians)) and written compactly for expressions in vectors and matrices ([matrix calculus](/notes/math/matrix-calculus)), while the Jacobian determinant changes variables in integrals ([multiple integrals](/notes/math/multiple-integrals)). Convexity turns a zero gradient into a certificate of global optimality ([convexity](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions)); gradient descent needs about $\kappa$ steps and Newton's method removes $\kappa$ near the minimum ([unconstrained optimization](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization)); constraints are balanced by multipliers in the KKT conditions ([KKT](/notes/math/lagrange-multipliers-and-kkt)), whose dual problem gives lower bounds and the SVM's form ([duality](/notes/math/duality)); and the condition number, the quantity that set the speed of gradient descent, also sets how many digits a computer can trust ([this note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability)). The [Probability course](/notes/probability/what-probability-is) supplies the other half of the language, random variables, expectations and the Gaussian; the [Machine Learning course](/notes/ml/what-machine-learning-is) builds on both.
