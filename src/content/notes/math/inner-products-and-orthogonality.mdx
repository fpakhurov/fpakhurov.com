The [previous note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems) ended with a system $Ax = b$ that has no solution, $b = (1, 2, 6)$ against the columns $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$, and with the question which $x$ makes $Ax$ *closest* to $b$. A vector space cannot answer it: addition and scaling say nothing about how long a vector is or whether two vectors are perpendicular. This note adds that structure. An inner product gives lengths, distances and angles; the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality guarantees that the angles make sense; and perpendicularity turns "the closest point" into a linear system, the orthogonal projection. Orthonormal bases make projections trivial to compute, and the Gram–Schmidt process builds one, which as a by-product factors a matrix as $A = QR$. The same inner product measures the similarity of two embeddings.

**Notation.** $\langle u, v\rangle$ is an inner product, $\lVert v\rVert$ the norm it defines, and $\lVert v\rVert_p$ the $\ell_p$ norm; $u^\top v = \sum_i u_i v_i$ is the dot product of $\mathbb R^n$. $\theta$ is the angle between two vectors. For a matrix $A$ with independent columns, $\hat x$ is the least-squares solution, $p = A\hat x$ the projection of $b$ and $e = b - p$ the error. $q_1, \dots, q_n$ are orthonormal vectors, $Q$ a matrix with orthonormal columns and $R$ an upper triangular matrix. $\operatorname{tr} A$, the trace, is the sum of the diagonal entries.

## Baseline: length and angle in the plane

In the plane, the length of $u = (u_1, u_2)$ is $\sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2}$ by Pythagoras, and the angle between two arrows can be measured with a protractor. The dot product connects the two. The law of cosines in the triangle with sides $u$, $v$ and $u - v$ says $\lVert u - v\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 + \lVert v\rVert^2 - 2\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert \cos\theta$, while expanding the left side in coordinates gives $\sum_i (u_i - v_i)^2 = \sum_i u_i^2 + \sum_i v_i^2 - 2\sum_i u_i v_i$. Comparing the two,

$$
u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 = \lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert \cos\theta .
$$

For $u = (3, 4)$ and $v = (4, 3)$: $u^\top v = 24$, both lengths are 5, so $\cos\theta = 24/25 = 0.96$ and $\theta \approx 16.3°$.

This works because the plane comes with a protractor. In $\mathbb R^{768}$, where embeddings live, or among polynomials, or among matrices, there is no picture to measure, and "angle" needs a definition. The approach of the previous note applies again: take the algebraic properties of the dot product as the definition, and derive the geometry from them.

## Inner products

An **inner product** on a vector space $V$ assigns a number $\langle u, v\rangle$ to every pair of vectors such that, for all $u, v, w$ and numbers $a, b$:

- it is **symmetric**: $\langle u, v\rangle = \langle v, u\rangle$;
- it is **linear** in the first argument: $\langle au + bw, v\rangle = a\langle u, v\rangle + b\langle w, v\rangle$, and so, by symmetry, in the second;
- it is **positive definite**: $\langle v, v\rangle \gt 0$ for every $v \ne 0$.

It defines a length, the **norm** $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt{\langle v, v\rangle}$, and a distance $\lVert u - v\rVert$. Examples:

- the dot product $\langle u, v\rangle = u^\top v$ on $\mathbb R^n$, the default from now on;
- a weighted inner product $\langle u, v\rangle_M = u^\top M v$, for a symmetric matrix $M$ with $v^\top M v \gt 0$ for all $v \ne 0$. With $M = \Sigma^{-1}$ for a covariance matrix $\Sigma$, its norm is the Mahalanobis distance of the [multivariate Gaussian](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#covariance-as-geometry);
- the **trace inner product** of matrices, $\langle A, B\rangle = \operatorname{tr}(A^\top B) = \sum_{i,j} a_{ij} b_{ij}$, which multiplies entries in matching positions; for instance $\langle A, I\rangle = \operatorname{tr} A$;
- for functions on $[0, 1]$, $\langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^1 f(t) g(t)\, dt$; the constant 1 and the function $t$ have $\langle 1, t\rangle = 1/2$.

Two vectors are **orthogonal**, $u \perp v$, when $\langle u, v\rangle = 0$. Expanding by linearity gives Pythagoras in any inner product space:

$$
\lVert u + v\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\langle u, v\rangle + \lVert v\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 + \lVert v\rVert^2 \quad \text{if } u \perp v .
$$

## Norms

A length can be defined without an inner product. A **norm** is any function $\lVert\cdot\rVert$ with $\lVert v\rVert \gt 0$ for $v \ne 0$, $\lVert a v\rVert = \lvert a\rvert\, \lVert v\rVert$, and the **triangle inequality** $\lVert u + v\rVert \le \lVert u\rVert + \lVert v\rVert$. Three norms on $\mathbb R^n$ are used constantly, and a fourth on matrices:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert v\rVert_1 &= \sum_i \lvert v_i\rvert, \\
\lVert v\rVert_2 &= \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i v_i^2}, \\
\lVert v\rVert_\infty &= \max_i \lvert v_i\rvert, \\
\lVert A\rVert_F &= \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(A^\top A)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $v = (3, -4)$ they give $\lVert v\rVert_1 = 7$, $\lVert v\rVert_2 = 5$ and $\lVert v\rVert_\infty = 4$; for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, $\lVert A\rVert_F = \sqrt{30} \approx 5.477$. The **Frobenius norm** is the $\ell_2$ norm of the matrix read as one long vector, and the norm of the trace inner product. The $\ell_2$ norm comes from the dot product; the general $\ell_p$ norm is $\big(\sum_i \lvert v_i\rvert^p\big)^{1/p}$ for $p \ge 1$.

The shape of a norm is the shape of its **unit ball**, the set of vectors of norm at most 1:

<UnitBalls />

The balls are nested, which says $\lVert v\rVert_\infty \le \lVert v\rVert_2 \le \lVert v\rVert_1$ for every $v$; in the other direction $\lVert v\rVert_1 \le \sqrt n\, \lVert v\rVert_2$ (Cauchy–Schwarz below, against the vector of ones) and $\lVert v\rVert_2 \le \sqrt n\, \lVert v\rVert_\infty$. These norms are therefore equivalent up to constants, as all norms in finite dimensions are, but the constants grow with $n$, and the shapes matter for what a penalty prefers: the corners of the $\ell_1$ ball sit on the axes, which is why the lasso, which constrains $\lVert w\rVert_1$, produces weights that are exactly zero ([the constrained form](/notes/ml/regularization#the-constrained-form-why-l1-gives-zeros)).

Of the three norms on $\mathbb R^n$, only $\ell_2$ comes from an inner product, and on matrices the Frobenius norm does. A norm that comes from one satisfies the **parallelogram law** $\lVert u + v\rVert^2 + \lVert u - v\rVert^2 = 2\lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\lVert v\rVert^2$, by adding the expansions of the two squares. For $u = e_1$ and $v = e_2$ the $\ell_1$ norm gives $2^2 + 2^2 = 8$ on the left and $2 + 2 = 4$ on the right. Without an inner product there is no notion of angle, and none of the projections below.

## The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

The definition of an angle by $\cos\theta = \langle u, v\rangle / (\lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert)$ makes sense only if that ratio lies between $-1$ and $1$. That is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and it holds for every inner product. The idea of the proof: the squared length of $u - tv$ can never be negative, whatever $t$ is; choosing the best $t$ turns that fact into the inequality.

$$
\boxed{\; \lvert\langle u, v\rangle\rvert \le \lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert, \quad \text{with equality exactly when one of } u, v \text{ is a multiple of the other} \;}
$$

Why: if $v = 0$ both sides are zero. Otherwise, for every number $t$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 &\le \lVert u - tv\rVert^2 \\
&= \lVert u\rVert^2 - 2t\,\langle u, v\rangle + t^2\, \lVert v\rVert^2 \\
&= \lVert u\rVert^2 - \frac{\langle u, v\rangle^2}{\lVert v\rVert^2} \qquad \text{at } t = \frac{\langle u, v\rangle}{\lVert v\rVert^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is positivity. The second expands $\langle u - tv, u - tv\rangle$ by linearity in both arguments and symmetry. The third substitutes the $t$ that minimizes the quadratic in $t$: the middle term becomes $-2\langle u, v\rangle^2/\lVert v\rVert^2$ and the last $+\langle u, v\rangle^2/\lVert v\rVert^2$. Multiplying by $\lVert v\rVert^2$ gives $\langle u, v\rangle^2 \le \lVert u\rVert^2 \lVert v\rVert^2$, and taking square roots the boxed inequality. Equality means $\lVert u - tv\rVert = 0$, that is, $u = tv$.

**Worked example.** For $u = (1, 2, 2)$ and $v = (2, 0, 1)$: $\langle u, v\rangle = 2 + 0 + 2 = 4$, $\lVert u\rVert = 3$ and $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt 5 \approx 2.236$, so $4 \le 6.708$. The angle has $\cos\theta = 4/6.708 = 0.596$, $\theta \approx 53.4°$. Over $100{,}000$ random pairs in $\mathbb R^5$ the largest ratio $\lvert\langle u, v\rangle\rvert / (\lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert)$ in the Code section is $0.9979$, never above 1.

Two consequences. The **triangle inequality** for the norm of an inner product follows in one line:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert u + v\rVert^2 &= \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\langle u, v\rangle + \lVert v\rVert^2 \\
&\le \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert + \lVert v\rVert^2 = \big(\lVert u\rVert + \lVert v\rVert\big)^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line expands the square, and the second applies Cauchy–Schwarz to the middle term. And the inequality between norms used above: $\lVert v\rVert_1 = \sum_i 1 \cdot \lvert v_i\rvert \le \sqrt n\, \lVert v\rVert_2$, by Cauchy–Schwarz for the vectors $(1, \dots, 1)$ and $(\lvert v_1\rvert, \dots, \lvert v_n\rvert)$. In probability the same inequality, for the inner product $E[XY]$ of random variables, is why a correlation lies between $-1$ and $1$.

## Cosine similarity in embeddings

Embedding models compare words, sentences and images by the cosine of the angle between their vectors:

$$
\cos(u, v) = \frac{\langle u, v\rangle}{\lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert} \in [-1, 1] .
$$

The [word-embedding note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings#reading-a-vector-space) uses it to find neighbours, and [semantic search](/notes/nlp/semantic-search#the-embedding-model) ranks passages by it. It ignores lengths, which in embeddings often reflect frequency rather than meaning. For unit vectors, cosine and distance carry the same information:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert u - v\rVert^2 &= \lVert u\rVert^2 - 2\langle u, v\rangle + \lVert v\rVert^2 \\
&= 2 - 2\cos(u, v) \qquad \text{if } \lVert u\rVert = \lVert v\rVert = 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line expands the square, and the second uses the unit lengths. So the nearest neighbours by cosine are the nearest neighbours by Euclidean distance *after* normalizing every vector, which is why retrieval systems normalize once and then use fast distance search. Without normalization the two rankings differ. In the Code section, 1000 random vectors in $\mathbb R^{50}$ whose lengths vary by a random factor (log-normal, standard deviation 0.5 on the log scale) are searched with 100 random queries: the nearest neighbour by raw Euclidean distance is the nearest by cosine for 25 of the 100 queries, and after normalization for all 100.

Two cautions. Attention compares queries and keys by the raw dot product, not the cosine, so lengths do matter there ([why a dot product](/notes/nlp/attention#why-a-dot-product)). And in high dimensions unrelated vectors are almost orthogonal: for two independent random directions in $\mathbb R^d$ the cosine has mean 0 and variance exactly $1/d$ (by symmetry, each of the $d$ squared coordinates of a random unit vector has mean $1/d$). For $d = 768$ its standard deviation is $0.036$, so a cosine of $0.2$ between two embeddings is far from chance, even though it looks small.

## Projection onto a line

The closest point to $b$ on the line through $a$ is the foot of the perpendicular. The idea: the error $e = b - \hat x a$ must be orthogonal to $a$, and that single condition fixes $\hat x$.

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
p &= \hat x\, a, \\
\hat x &= \frac{a^\top b}{a^\top a}, \\
p &= P b \ \text{ with } \ P = \frac{a a^\top}{a^\top a}
\end{aligned}
}
$$

Why:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^\top (b - \hat x a) &= 0 \\
a^\top b - \hat x\, a^\top a &= 0 \\
\hat x &= \frac{a^\top b}{a^\top a} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The three lines are the orthogonality condition, linearity, and division by $a^\top a \gt 0$. Then $p = a\hat x = a\,(a^\top b)/(a^\top a) = \big(aa^\top/(a^\top a)\big) b$, since $a^\top b$ is a number and can be moved. It is the closest point: for any other $ta$, $b - ta = e + (\hat x - t)\,a$ with $e \perp a$, so by Pythagoras $\lVert b - ta\rVert^2 = \lVert e\rVert^2 + (\hat x - t)^2 \lVert a\rVert^2 \ge \lVert e\rVert^2$.

**Worked example.** $a = (2, 1)$ and $b = (3, 4)$: $a^\top b = 6 + 4 = 10$ and $a^\top a = 5$, so $\hat x = 2$, $p = (4, 2)$ and $e = (-1, 2)$. The error is perpendicular, $a^\top e = -2 + 2 = 0$, and $\lVert b\rVert^2 = 25 = 20 + 5 = \lVert p\rVert^2 + \lVert e\rVert^2$. The projection matrix is $P = \frac15 \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $Pb = \frac15 (12 + 8,\ 6 + 4) = (4, 2)$.

## Projection onto a subspace

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> The point of a subspace closest to $b$ is the one whose error is orthogonal to the whole subspace. For the column space of $A$ that condition is $A^\top(b - A\hat x) = 0$, a square linear system: finding the closest point is solving the normal equations.</aside>

Let the subspace be the column space of $A \in \mathbb R^{m \times n}$, with independent columns $a_1, \dots, a_n$. A point of it is $A\hat x$, and the error $e = b - A\hat x$ must be orthogonal to every column, $a_j^\top e = 0$, which is $A^\top e = 0$ written as one vector equation:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
A^\top A\, \hat x &= A^\top b, \qquad p = A\hat x = Pb, \\
P &= A\,(A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top
\end{aligned}
}
$$

Why:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^\top (b - A\hat x) &= 0 \\
A^\top A\, \hat x &= A^\top b \\
\hat x &= (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top b \\
p = A\hat x &= A\,(A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top\, b .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the orthogonality condition; the second multiplies out; the third needs $A^\top A$ to be invertible, which holds because the columns are independent: if $A^\top A z = 0$, then $z^\top A^\top A z = \lVert Az\rVert^2 = 0$, so $Az = 0$ and $z = 0$. The fourth multiplies by $A$. The point $p$ is the closest one by the same Pythagoras argument as for a line: for any $y$, $b - Ay = e + A(\hat x - y)$ with $e$ orthogonal to $A(\hat x - y)$, so $\lVert b - Ay\rVert^2 = \lVert e\rVert^2 + \lVert A(\hat x - y)\rVert^2$.

The projection matrix has two characteristic properties. It is **idempotent**, $P^2 = P$: projecting twice changes nothing, as $P^2 = A(A^\top A)^{-1}(A^\top A)(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top = P$. And it is **symmetric**, $P^\top = P$, because $(A^\top A)^{-1}$ is. Its trace is $n$, the dimension of the subspace. The error $e = (I - P)b$ lies in the left null space $\ker A^\top$ of the [previous note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#four-subspaces), and $I - P$ is the projection onto it: every $b$ splits uniquely into a part in $\operatorname{col}(A)$ and a part orthogonal to it.

**Worked example.** The plane spanned by $a_1 = (1, 0, 1)$ and $a_2 = (0, 1, 1)$, and $b = (1, 2, 6)$, the system without a solution from the previous note:

$$
A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^\top b = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat x = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

So $p = 2a_1 + 3a_2 = (2, 3, 5)$ and $e = (-1, -1, 1)$, with $a_1^\top e = -1 + 1 = 0$ and $a_2^\top e = -1 + 1 = 0$. The distance from $b$ to the plane is $\lVert e\rVert = \sqrt 3$. The projection matrix is

$$
P = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{tr} P = \frac{2 + 2 + 2}{3} = 2 .
$$

## Least squares as a projection

When $Ax = b$ has no solution, the least-squares solution minimizes $\lVert Ax - b\rVert$, the distance from $b$ to the point $Ax$ of the column space. By the previous section the minimizer is the projection: $A\hat x = Pb$, and $\hat x$ solves the normal equations $A^\top A\hat x = A^\top b$. The calculus derivation in the [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations) and the geometric one here give the same equations; the [geometry section](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-geometry-a-projection) of that note draws the projection for a regression with three observations, calls $P$ the hat matrix, and reads its diagonal as the leverage of each point. In the example above the best combination of $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$ is $\hat x = (2, 3)$, and no combination comes closer to $b$ than $\sqrt 3$.

The normal equations are the right description and often the wrong computation: forming $A^\top A$ squares the condition number of the problem, as the [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#when-the-solution-is-unstable) shows with polynomial features. The QR factorization below avoids forming it.

## Orthonormal bases

Vectors $q_1, \dots, q_n$ are **orthonormal** if they are mutually orthogonal and of unit length: $q_i^\top q_j = 1$ for $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise. As the columns of a matrix $Q$, this is $Q^\top Q = I$. Orthonormal vectors are automatically independent: if $\sum_j c_j q_j = 0$, taking the inner product with $q_i$ leaves $c_i = 0$.

In an orthonormal basis, coordinates need no linear system. If $x = \sum_j c_j q_j$, the inner product with $q_i$ kills every term but one, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_i &= q_i^\top x, \\
x &= \sum_i (q_i^\top x)\, q_i .
\end{aligned}
$$

For orthonormal $q_1, \dots, q_n$ spanning a subspace of $\mathbb R^m$, the projection onto it is $p = \sum_i (q_i^\top b)\, q_i = QQ^\top b$, the formula $A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ with $A = Q$ and $Q^\top Q = I$. For example, with $q_1 = (0.8, 0.6)$ and $q_2 = (-0.6, 0.8)$, the vector $x = (2, 4)$ has coordinates $q_1^\top x = 1.6 + 2.4 = 4$ and $q_2^\top x = -1.2 + 3.2 = 2$, and indeed $4q_1 + 2q_2 = (3.2 - 1.2,\ 2.4 + 1.6) = (2, 4)$.

## Gram–Schmidt and the QR factorization \{#gram-schmidt-and-qr\}

The **Gram–Schmidt process** turns independent vectors $a_1, \dots, a_n$ into orthonormal $q_1, \dots, q_n$ with the same span at every stage. The idea: take the vectors one at a time, remove from each its projections onto the $q$'s already built, and normalize what remains:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_j &= a_j - \sum_{i \lt j} (q_i^\top a_j)\, q_i, \\
q_j &= \frac{v_j}{\lVert v_j\rVert} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The remainder $v_j$ is orthogonal to every earlier $q_k$, because $q_k^\top v_j = q_k^\top a_j - \sum_{i \lt j} (q_i^\top a_j)\, q_k^\top q_i = q_k^\top a_j - q_k^\top a_j = 0$, using $q_k^\top q_i = 0$ for $i \ne k$. It is nonzero, because $a_j$ is not in the span of $a_1, \dots, a_{j-1}$, which is the span of $q_1, \dots, q_{j-1}$.

Record the coefficients as $r_{ij} = q_i^\top a_j$ for $i \lt j$ and $r_{jj} = \lVert v_j\rVert$. Solving the definition of $v_j$ for $a_j$ shows that each column of $A$ is a combination of the first $j$ columns of $Q$:

$$
\boxed{\; A = QR, \qquad Q^\top Q = I, \qquad R = \begin{pmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} & \cdots \\ 0 & r_{22} & \cdots \\ \vdots & & \ddots \end{pmatrix} \text{ upper triangular with } r_{jj} \gt 0 \;}
$$

Why:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_j &= v_j + \sum_{i \lt j} (q_i^\top a_j)\, q_i \\
&= r_{jj}\, q_j + \sum_{i \lt j} r_{ij}\, q_i \\
&= Q \begin{pmatrix} r_{1j} & \cdots & r_{jj} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \end{pmatrix}^\top .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line rearranges the definition of $v_j$, the second uses $v_j = \lVert v_j\rVert\, q_j$ and the names of the coefficients, and the third reads the combination as $Q$ times a column whose entries below position $j$ are zero. That column is column $j$ of $R$.

**Worked example in the plane.** $a_1 = (4, 3)$ and $a_2 = (2, 4)$, the figure below.

1. $r_{11} = \lVert a_1\rVert = 5$ and $q_1 = (0.8, 0.6)$.
2. $r_{12} = q_1^\top a_2 = 1.6 + 2.4 = 4$, so the component of $a_2$ along $q_1$ is $4q_1 = (3.2, 2.4)$.
3. $v_2 = a_2 - 4q_1 = (-1.2, 1.6)$, with $q_1^\top v_2 = -0.96 + 0.96 = 0$.
4. $r_{22} = \lVert v_2\rVert = \sqrt{1.44 + 2.56} = 2$ and $q_2 = (-0.6, 0.8)$.

So $\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & -0.6 \\ 0.6 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. The second column of $R$, $(4, 2)$, is the coordinate vector of $a_2$ in the basis $q_1, q_2$ found in the previous section.

<GramSchmidtSteps />

**Least squares through QR.** With $A = QR$ the normal equations simplify. The idea: $Q^\top Q = I$ removes $Q$, and the invertible $R^\top$ cancels:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^\top A\, \hat x &= A^\top b \\
R^\top Q^\top Q R\, \hat x &= R^\top Q^\top b \\
R^\top R\, \hat x &= R^\top Q^\top b \\
R\, \hat x &= Q^\top b .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second line substitutes $A = QR$ and $(QR)^\top = R^\top Q^\top$, the third uses $Q^\top Q = I$, and the fourth multiplies by $(R^\top)^{-1}$, which exists because $R$ is triangular with a positive diagonal. The last system is triangular and is solved by back substitution, and $A^\top A$ is never formed. For the plane example, Gram–Schmidt gives $q_1 = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt 2$, then $v_2 = (0, 1, 1) - \tfrac12 (1, 0, 1) = (-\tfrac12, 1, \tfrac12)$ with $\lVert v_2\rVert = \sqrt{3/2}$, so $q_2 = (-1, 2, 1)/\sqrt 6$, and

$$
R = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt 2 & 1/\sqrt 2 \\ 0 & \sqrt{3/2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q^\top b = \begin{pmatrix} 7/\sqrt 2 \\ 9/\sqrt 6 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Back substitution gives $\hat x_2 = (9/\sqrt 6)/\sqrt{3/2} = 9/3 = 3$, and then $\sqrt 2\, \hat x_1 + 3/\sqrt 2 = 7/\sqrt 2$, so $2\hat x_1 + 3 = 7$ and $\hat x_1 = 2$, as before.

**In floating point, the formula above is not the algorithm.** Classical Gram–Schmidt, which projects the original $a_j$, loses orthogonality when the columns are nearly dependent. **Modified Gram–Schmidt** subtracts the projections one at a time from the running remainder, which is the same in exact arithmetic and much better in floating point. Library routines (`numpy.linalg.qr`, LAPACK) use **Householder reflections** instead, the orthogonal matrices of the next section, which keep $Q$ orthogonal to machine precision. For polynomial features $1, t, \dots, t^{11}$ on 50 points of $[0, 1]$, with condition number about $10^8$, the Code section measures $\lVert Q^\top Q - I\rVert_F$ as $2$ for classical Gram–Schmidt (orthogonality is lost completely), $3 \cdot 10^{-9}$ for modified Gram–Schmidt (in line with the known bound proportional to the condition number times the unit roundoff) and $2 \cdot 10^{-15}$ for Householder ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), lectures 8–10; [Higham, 2002](#references), chapter 19). Why algorithms that agree in exact arithmetic differ this much is the subject of the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability).

## Orthogonal matrices

A square matrix $Q$ with orthonormal columns is **orthogonal**: $Q^\top Q = I$, so $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$ and also $QQ^\top = I$, which says the rows are orthonormal too. The idea: an orthogonal matrix changes the coordinates but keeps every inner product, hence every length, distance and angle.

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\langle Qx, Qy\rangle &= \langle x, y\rangle, \\
\lVert Qx\rVert &= \lVert x\rVert \quad \text{for all } x, y
\end{aligned}
}
$$

Why:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle Qx, Qy\rangle &= (Qx)^\top (Qy) \\
&= x^\top Q^\top Q\, y \\
&= x^\top y .
\end{aligned}
$$

The steps are the definition of the dot product, $(Qx)^\top = x^\top Q^\top$, and $Q^\top Q = I$; the norm is the case $y = x$. Products of orthogonal matrices are orthogonal, since $(Q_1 Q_2)^\top Q_1 Q_2 = Q_2^\top Q_1^\top Q_1 Q_2 = I$.

Examples are rotations, reflections and permutations. The rotation by $30°$ moves $x = (4, 3)$ and $y = (2, 4)$ but keeps $\lVert x\rVert = 5$ and $x^\top y = 20$. A **Householder reflection** $H = I - 2vv^\top/(v^\top v)$ reflects in the hyperplane orthogonal to $v$; with $v = x - \lVert x\rVert e_1$ it sends $x$ to $\lVert x\rVert e_1$. For $x = (4, 3)$, $v = (-1, 3)$, $v^\top v = 10$ and

$$
H = I - \frac{2}{10} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -3 & 9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & -0.8 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad H \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3.2 + 1.8 \\ 2.4 - 2.4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Householder QR applies one such reflection per column to clear everything below the diagonal; the product of the reflections is $Q^\top$.

**Where machine learning uses them.** PCA rotates centred data into the orthonormal basis of its principal directions, an orthogonal change of coordinates that keeps all distances ([dimensionality reduction](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction)). Orthogonal weight matrices keep the scale of a signal through any number of linear layers, which is the motivation for **orthogonal initialization** ([Saxe, McClelland and Ganguli, 2014](#references)) and for the orthogonal regularization of [BigGAN](/notes/generative-models/gan-scaling#orthogonal-regularization). The Code section multiplies 50 random $100 \times 100$ matrices: with Gaussian entries of variance $1/100$, whose average effect on the squared length is exactly 1, the product stretches some direction by a factor of 6.9 and shrinks another by a factor below $10^{-16}$, too small for double precision to resolve: that direction of the input is lost. With orthogonal matrices every direction keeps its length exactly. The [normalization note](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#why-the-scale-is-multiplied-at-every-layer) studies the same multiplication of scales with nonlinearities in between.

## What changed

| | Lengths and angles in the plane | Inner product spaces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Length and angle | Pythagoras and a protractor | $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt{\langle v, v\rangle}$, $\cos\theta = \langle u, v\rangle / (\lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert)$ in any dimension |
| Why angles make sense | a picture | Cauchy–Schwarz |
| Other lengths | none | $\ell_1$, $\ell_\infty$, Frobenius; only $\ell_2$ and Frobenius have angles |
| Closest point | drop a perpendicular by eye | solve $A^\top A\hat x = A^\top b$; $P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ |
| Coordinates | solve a system | inner products, in an orthonormal basis |
| Least squares | normal equations | $R\hat x = Q^\top b$ after $A = QR$ |

## Limitations

- **The Euclidean inner product is a choice.** Distances and angles change when features are rescaled, so features in different units need [scaling](/notes/ml/data-features-and-leakage#scaling) first, or a weighted inner product such as the Mahalanobis one.
- **Cosine similarity discards length**, which can carry information, and its typical values depend on the dimension: in $\mathbb R^{768}$ unrelated vectors have cosines of a few hundredths, so thresholds do not transfer between models.
- **The formulas need independent columns.** With dependent columns $A^\top A$ is singular and $(A^\top A)^{-1}$ does not exist; the projection is still defined, and the [SVD note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) computes it with the pseudoinverse.
- **Exact formulas and stable algorithms differ.** Classical Gram–Schmidt and the normal equations are correct in exact arithmetic and unreliable in floating point when the columns are nearly dependent; Householder QR is the default.
- **Spaces of functions need more care.** The inner product $\int f g$ makes sense, but the existence of closest points needs completeness, which [kernel methods](/notes/ml/kernels) and [Gaussian processes](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes) rely on without this course proving it.

## What you should remember

- An inner product is symmetric, linear and positive definite; it gives the norm $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt{\langle v, v\rangle}$, distances, and Pythagoras for orthogonal vectors.
- The $\ell_1$, $\ell_2$, $\ell_\infty$ and Frobenius norms measure size differently; only $\ell_2$ (and Frobenius for matrices) comes from an inner product.
- Cauchy–Schwarz, $\lvert\langle u, v\rangle\rvert \le \lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert$, follows from $\lVert u - tv\rVert^2 \ge 0$ at the best $t$; it makes angles and the triangle inequality work.
- Cosine similarity is the inner product of normalized vectors; for unit vectors, $\lVert u - v\rVert^2 = 2 - 2\cos$.
- Projection onto a line: $\hat x = a^\top b / a^\top a$. Onto the column space: $A^\top A\hat x = A^\top b$ and $P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$, symmetric and idempotent. The error is orthogonal to the subspace.
- Least squares is the projection of $b$ onto the column space.
- In an orthonormal basis, coordinates are inner products and the projection is $QQ^\top$.
- Gram–Schmidt produces $A = QR$; least squares becomes $R\hat x = Q^\top b$. In practice, use Householder QR.
- Orthogonal matrices keep all lengths and angles: $Q^\top Q = I$ and $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$.

## Code

The examples of the note: norms, the parallelogram law, Cauchy–Schwarz, cosine against distance, the two projections, Gram–Schmidt and QR, the loss of orthogonality, orthogonal matrices and fifty layers of random matrices. Seeds are fixed; exact numbers can differ across platforms and library versions, especially for the orthogonality errors and the smallest stretch factor, and Householder QR may return columns of $Q$ with the opposite sign. The script runs in a few seconds.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
norm = np.linalg.norm

# norms of v = (3, -4), of x = (0.6, 0.8), and the Frobenius norm of a matrix
v = np.array([3., -4])
print(norm(v, 1), norm(v), norm(v, np.inf))                   # 7.0 5.0 4.0
x0 = np.array([0.6, 0.8])
print(round(norm(x0, 1), 6), round(norm(x0), 6), round(norm(x0, np.inf), 6))   # 1.4 1.0 0.8: outside, on, inside the unit balls
A = np.array([[1., 2], [3, 4]])
print(round(norm(A, 'fro'), 3), round(np.sqrt(np.trace(A.T @ A)), 3))   # 5.477 5.477

# the parallelogram law holds for the 2-norm and fails for the 1-norm
e1, e2 = np.eye(2)
for p in (2, 1):
    print(p, round(norm(e1 + e2, p) ** 2 + norm(e1 - e2, p) ** 2, 6), 2 * (norm(e1, p) ** 2 + norm(e2, p) ** 2))
# 2 4.0 4.0 | 1 8.0 4.0

# Cauchy-Schwarz and the angle between u = (1, 2, 2) and w = (2, 0, 1)
u, w = np.array([1., 2, 2]), np.array([2., 0, 1])
c = u @ w / (norm(u) * norm(w))
print(u @ w, norm(u), round(norm(w), 3), round(c, 3), round(np.degrees(np.arccos(c)), 1))   # 4.0 3.0 2.236 0.596 53.4
X, Y = rng.normal(size=(2, 100_000, 5))
print(round((np.abs((X * Y).sum(1)) / (norm(X, axis=1) * norm(Y, axis=1))).max(), 4))      # 0.9979: never above 1

# cosine similarity: for unit vectors, squared distance = 2 - 2 cos, so the neighbours agree
E = rng.normal(size=(1000, 50)) * rng.lognormal(0, 0.5, size=(1000, 1))   # vectors of varying length
q = rng.normal(size=(100, 50))
cos = (q @ E.T) / (norm(q, axis=1)[:, None] * norm(E, axis=1))
dist = norm(q[:, None, :] - E[None], axis=2)
En = E / norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True); qn = q / norm(q, axis=1, keepdims=True)
dist_n = norm(qn[:, None, :] - En[None], axis=2)
print((cos.argmax(1) == dist.argmin(1)).mean(), (cos.argmax(1) == dist_n.argmin(1)).mean(),
      np.allclose(dist_n ** 2, 2 - 2 * cos))
# 0.25 1.0 True: raw distances pick other neighbours; after normalizing they agree with cosine
U1, V1 = rng.normal(size=(2, 20_000, 768))
c768 = (U1 * V1).sum(1) / (norm(U1, axis=1) * norm(V1, axis=1))
print(round(c768.std(), 4), round(1 / np.sqrt(768), 4))      # 0.0361 0.0361: random directions are nearly orthogonal

# projection onto the line through a = (2, 1)
a, b = np.array([2., 1]), np.array([3., 4])
p = (a @ b) / (a @ a) * a
Pa = np.outer(a, a) / (a @ a)
print((a @ b) / (a @ a), p, b - p, a @ (b - p), b @ b, p @ p + (b - p) @ (b - p), (5 * Pa).tolist())
# 2.0 [4. 2.] [-1.  2.] 0.0 25.0 25.0 [[4.0, 2.0], [2.0, 1.0]]

# projection onto the plane spanned by (1, 0, 1) and (0, 1, 1)
A2 = np.column_stack([[1., 0, 1], [0, 1, 1]]); b2 = np.array([1., 2, 6])
xh = np.linalg.solve(A2.T @ A2, A2.T @ b2)
P = A2 @ np.linalg.inv(A2.T @ A2) @ A2.T
print(xh, A2 @ xh, b2 - A2 @ xh, A2.T @ (b2 - A2 @ xh))         # [2. 3.] [2. 3. 5.] [-1. -1.  1.] [0. 0.]
print((3 * P).round(6).tolist(), np.allclose(P @ P, P), np.allclose(P, P.T), round(np.trace(P), 6))
# [[2.0, -1.0, 1.0], [-1.0, 2.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0, 2.0]] True True 2.0

# Gram-Schmidt, by hand on a1 = (4, 3), a2 = (2, 4)
def gram_schmidt(A, modified=False):
    m, n = A.shape; Q = np.zeros((m, n)); R = np.zeros((n, n))
    for j in range(n):
        vj = A[:, j].copy()
        for i in range(j):
            R[i, j] = Q[:, i] @ (vj if modified else A[:, j])   # classical: project a_j; modified: the running v_j
            vj -= R[i, j] * Q[:, i]
        R[j, j] = norm(vj); Q[:, j] = vj / R[j, j]
    return Q, R
Q, R = gram_schmidt(np.array([[4., 2], [3, 4]]))
print(Q.round(6).tolist(), R.round(6).tolist())    # [[0.8, -0.6], [0.6, 0.8]] [[5.0, 4.0], [0.0, 2.0]]
print((Q.T @ [2, 4]).round(6), ((Q.T @ Q).round(6) + 0).tolist())   # [4. 2.] [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]]: coordinates of a2 = column 2 of R

# QR of the 3 x 2 matrix, and least squares through R x = Q^T b
Q3, R3 = gram_schmidt(A2)
print(R3.round(4).tolist(), (Q3 * np.sqrt(6)).round(4)[:, 1], np.linalg.solve(R3, Q3.T @ b2), Q3 @ Q3.T @ b2)
# [[1.4142, 0.7071], [0.0, 1.2247]] [-1.  2.  1.] [2. 3.] [2. 3. 5.]
Qn, Rn = np.linalg.qr(A2)                          # Householder (LAPACK); columns may differ in sign
print(np.allclose(np.abs(Rn), np.abs(R3)), np.allclose(Qn @ Rn, A2))   # True True

# loss of orthogonality: classical and modified Gram-Schmidt against Householder QR
V = np.vander(np.linspace(0, 1, 50), 12, increasing=True)            # polynomial features 1, t, ..., t^11
print(f'{np.linalg.cond(V):.0e}', *[f'{norm(Qx.T @ Qx - np.eye(12)):.0e}' for Qx in
      (gram_schmidt(V)[0], gram_schmidt(V, modified=True)[0], np.linalg.qr(V)[0])])
# 1e+08 2e+00 3e-09 2e-15   (cond(V); orthogonality error of classical GS, modified GS, Householder)

# orthogonal matrices: a rotation and a Householder reflection preserve lengths and inner products
t = np.radians(30); Rot = np.array([[np.cos(t), -np.sin(t)], [np.sin(t), np.cos(t)]])
x, y = np.array([4., 3]), np.array([2., 4])
print(np.allclose(Rot.T @ Rot, np.eye(2)), round(norm(Rot @ x), 6), round((Rot @ x) @ (Rot @ y), 6), x @ y)   # True 5.0 20.0 20.0
h = x - norm(x) * e1                                 # reflect (4, 3) onto the first axis
H = np.eye(2) - 2 * np.outer(h, h) / (h @ h)
print(H.round(6).tolist(), (H @ x).round(6) + 0, np.allclose(H @ H, np.eye(2)), round(np.linalg.det(H), 6))
# [[0.8, 0.6], [0.6, -0.8]] [5. 0.] True -1.0

# fifty linear layers of width 100: Gaussian weights (variance 1/n) against orthogonal weights;
# the largest and smallest stretch factors of the product (its largest and smallest singular values)
n, L = 100, 50
G, O = np.eye(n), np.eye(n)
for _ in range(L):
    G = rng.normal(size=(n, n)) / np.sqrt(n) @ G
    O = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(n, n)))[0] @ O
sG, sO = np.linalg.svd(G, compute_uv=False), np.linalg.svd(O, compute_uv=False)
print(f'{sG[0]:.1f} {sG[-1]:.0e} {sO[0]:.6f} {sO[-1]:.6f}')   # 6.9 2e-20 1.000000 1.000000
# (the smallest Gaussian value is below the rounding level of double precision: only its smallness is meaningful)
```
