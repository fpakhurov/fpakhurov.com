No combination of the columns $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$ equals $b = (1, 2, 6)$, as the [previous note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#when-there-is-no-exact-solution-least-squares) found. Which combination comes closest? And how similar are two word embeddings, each a vector of 768 numbers? Both questions need lengths and angles, and a vector space has neither: addition and scaling say nothing about how long a vector is or whether two vectors are perpendicular.

In the plane, lengths and angles come with the picture. Pythagoras gives the length of $u = (u_1, u_2)$,

$$
\lVert u\rVert = \sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2} ,
$$

and the law of cosines in the triangle with sides $u$, $v$ and $u - v$ relates the angle $\theta$ between $u$ and $v$ to the coordinates. Expanding the same squared length in two ways,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert u - v\rVert^2 &= \lVert u\rVert^2 + \lVert v\rVert^2 - 2\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert \cos\theta , \\
\lVert u - v\rVert^2 &= \sum_i u_i^2 + \sum_i v_i^2 - 2\sum_i u_i v_i ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and comparing the two gives

$$
u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 = \lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert \cos\theta .
$$

For $u = (3, 4)$ and $v = (4, 3)$ the left side is 24 and both lengths are 5, so $\cos\theta = 0.96$ and $\theta \approx 16.3°$. In $\mathbb R^{768}$ there is no picture to measure. The way out is the one the [first note](/notes/math/vectors-and-linear-maps) took for vectors: take the algebraic properties of this product as the definition, and derive the geometry from them.

## Inner products

An **inner product** on a vector space $V$ assigns a number $\langle u, v\rangle$ to every pair of vectors such that, for all $u, v, w$ and numbers $a, b$:

- it is **symmetric**: $\langle u, v\rangle = \langle v, u\rangle$;
- it is **linear** in the first argument: $\langle au + bw, v\rangle = a\langle u, v\rangle + b\langle w, v\rangle$, and so, by symmetry, in the second;
- it is **positive definite**: $\langle v, v\rangle \gt 0$ for every $v \ne 0$.

It defines a length, the **norm** $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt{\langle v, v\rangle}$, and a distance $\lVert u - v\rVert$. Four inner products appear in this course:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle u, v\rangle &= u^\top v = \sum_i u_i v_i , \\
\langle u, v\rangle_M &= u^\top M v , \\
\langle A, B\rangle &= \operatorname{tr}(A^\top B) = \sum_{i,j} a_{ij} b_{ij} , \\
\langle f, g\rangle &= \int_0^1 f(t)\, g(t)\, dt .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first, the **dot product** of $\mathbb R^n$, is the default from now on. The second needs a symmetric matrix $M$ with $v^\top M v \gt 0$ for every $v \ne 0$; with $M = \Sigma^{-1}$ for a covariance matrix $\Sigma$, its norm is the Mahalanobis distance of the [multivariate Gaussian](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#covariance-as-geometry). The third, the **trace inner product** of matrices, multiplies entries in matching positions; $\operatorname{tr}$, the trace, is the sum of the diagonal entries, and $\langle A, I\rangle = \operatorname{tr} A$. The fourth acts on functions on $[0, 1]$; the constant 1 and the function $t$ have $\langle 1, t\rangle = 1/2$.

Two vectors are **orthogonal**, $u \perp v$, when $\langle u, v\rangle = 0$. Expanding by linearity gives Pythagoras in any inner product space:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert u + v\rVert^2 &= \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\langle u, v\rangle + \lVert v\rVert^2 \\
&= \lVert u\rVert^2 + \lVert v\rVert^2 \quad \text{if } u \perp v .
\end{aligned}
$$

## Norms

A length can be defined without an inner product. A **norm** is any function $\lVert\cdot\rVert$ with $\lVert v\rVert \gt 0$ for $v \ne 0$, $\lVert a v\rVert = \lvert a\rvert\, \lVert v\rVert$, and the **triangle inequality** $\lVert u + v\rVert \le \lVert u\rVert + \lVert v\rVert$. Three norms on $\mathbb R^n$ are used constantly, and a fourth on matrices:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert v\rVert_1 &= \sum_i \lvert v_i\rvert, \\
\lVert v\rVert_2 &= \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i v_i^2}, \\
\lVert v\rVert_\infty &= \max_i \lvert v_i\rvert, \\
\lVert A\rVert_F &= \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(A^\top A)} .
\end{aligned}
$$

For $v = (3, -4)$ they give $\lVert v\rVert_1 = 7$, $\lVert v\rVert_2 = 5$ and $\lVert v\rVert_\infty = 4$. The **Frobenius norm** is the $\ell_2$ norm of the matrix read as one long vector, and the norm of the trace inner product; for

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

it is $\sqrt{30} \approx 5.477$. The $\ell_1$, $\ell_2$ and $\ell_\infty$ norms belong to one family, the $\ell_p$ norms for $p \ge 1$:

$$
\lVert v\rVert_p = \Big(\sum_i \lvert v_i\rvert^p\Big)^{1/p} .
$$

The shape of a norm is the shape of its **unit ball**, the set of vectors of norm at most 1:

<UnitBalls />

The balls are nested, which says $\lVert v\rVert_\infty \le \lVert v\rVert_2 \le \lVert v\rVert_1$ for every $v$. In the other direction $\lVert v\rVert_1 \le \sqrt n\, \lVert v\rVert_2$, as the next section shows, and $\lVert v\rVert_2 \le \sqrt n\, \lVert v\rVert_\infty$. So these norms are equivalent up to constants, as all norms in finite dimensions are, but the constants grow with $n$. The shapes also matter for what a penalty prefers. The corners of the $\ell_1$ ball sit on the axes, which is why the lasso, which constrains $\lVert w\rVert_1$, produces weights that are exactly zero ([the constrained form](/notes/ml/regularization#the-constrained-form-why-l1-gives-zeros)).

Of these norms, only $\ell_2$ comes from an inner product, and on matrices the Frobenius norm does. Adding the expansions of the two squares shows that a norm from an inner product satisfies the **parallelogram law**:

$$
\lVert u + v\rVert^2 + \lVert u - v\rVert^2 = 2\lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\lVert v\rVert^2 .
$$

For $u = e_1$ and $v = e_2$ the $\ell_1$ norm gives $2^2 + 2^2 = 8$ on the left and $2 + 2 = 4$ on the right, so $\ell_1$ has no inner product behind it. Without one there is no notion of angle, and none of the projections below.

## The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

An angle in any inner product space would be defined by

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\langle u, v\rangle}{\lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert} ,
$$

which makes sense only if the ratio lies between $-1$ and $1$. It always does: the **Cauchy–Schwarz inequality** says that

$$
\lvert\langle u, v\rangle\rvert \le \lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert ,
$$

with equality exactly when one of $u$, $v$ is a multiple of the other.

Why? Split $u$ into a part along $v$ and a part orthogonal to it ([Axler, 2024](#references), 6.13–6.14). If $v = 0$ both sides are zero. Otherwise put $t = \langle u, v\rangle / \lVert v\rVert^2$ and $w = u - tv$. Then $\langle w, v\rangle = \langle u, v\rangle - t\lVert v\rVert^2 = 0$, so $u = tv + w$ with $w \perp v$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert u\rVert^2 &= t^2 \lVert v\rVert^2 + \lVert w\rVert^2 && \text{Pythagoras} \\
&\ge t^2 \lVert v\rVert^2 \\
&= \frac{\langle u, v\rangle^2}{\lVert v\rVert^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying by $\lVert v\rVert^2$ and taking square roots gives the inequality. Equality holds exactly when $w = 0$, that is, when $u = tv$. The part $tv$ is the projection of $u$ onto the line of $v$, the subject of a section below; the inequality says that a projection is never longer than the vector.

For $u = (1, 2, 2)$ and $v = (2, 0, 1)$: $\langle u, v\rangle = 4$, $\lVert u\rVert = 3$ and $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt 5 \approx 2.236$, so $4 \le 6.708$, $\cos\theta = 0.596$ and $\theta \approx 53.4°$. Over $100{,}000$ random pairs in $\mathbb R^5$ the largest ratio in the Code section is $0.9979$, never above 1.

The **triangle inequality** for the norm of an inner product follows in one step:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert u + v\rVert^2 &= \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\langle u, v\rangle + \lVert v\rVert^2 \\
&\le \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert + \lVert v\rVert^2 && \text{Cauchy–Schwarz} \\
&= \big(\lVert u\rVert + \lVert v\rVert\big)^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

So does the bound between norms used above, by Cauchy–Schwarz for the vectors $(1, \dots, 1)$ and $(\lvert v_1\rvert, \dots, \lvert v_n\rvert)$:

$$
\lVert v\rVert_1 = \sum_i 1 \cdot \lvert v_i\rvert \le \sqrt n\, \lVert v\rVert_2 .
$$

In probability the same inequality, for the inner product $\mathbb E[XY]$ of random variables, is why a correlation lies between $-1$ and $1$.

## Cosine similarity in embeddings

Embedding models compare words, sentences and images by the cosine of the angle between their vectors:

$$
\cos(u, v) = \frac{\langle u, v\rangle}{\lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert} \in [-1, 1] .
$$

The [word-embedding note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings#reading-a-vector-space) uses it to find neighbours, and [semantic search](/notes/nlp/semantic-search#the-embedding-model) ranks passages by it. It ignores lengths, which in embeddings often reflect frequency rather than meaning. For unit vectors, cosine and distance carry the same information:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lVert u - v\rVert^2 &= \lVert u\rVert^2 - 2\langle u, v\rangle + \lVert v\rVert^2 \\
&= 2 - 2\cos(u, v) && \lVert u\rVert = \lVert v\rVert = 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The nearest neighbours by cosine are therefore the nearest by Euclidean distance after every vector is normalized, which is why retrieval systems normalize once and then use fast distance search. Without normalization the two rankings differ. In the Code section, 1000 random vectors in $\mathbb R^{50}$ with lengths scaled by a random log-normal factor (standard deviation 0.5 on the log scale) are searched with 100 random queries. The nearest neighbour by raw Euclidean distance is the nearest by cosine for 25 of the 100 queries, and after normalization for all 100.

Attention compares queries and keys by the raw dot product instead of the cosine, so there lengths do matter ([why a dot product](/notes/nlp/attention#why-a-dot-product)).

In high dimensions unrelated vectors are almost orthogonal. For two independent random directions in $\mathbb R^d$ the cosine has mean 0 and variance exactly $1/d$, because each of the $d$ squared coordinates of a random unit vector has mean $1/d$ by symmetry. For $d = 768$ its standard deviation is $0.036$, so a cosine of $0.2$ between two embeddings is far from chance, even though it looks small.

## Projection onto a line

Which multiple of $a$ is closest to $b$? The foot of the perpendicular: the error $e = b - \hat x a$ must be orthogonal to $a$, and that single condition fixes $\hat x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^\top (b - \hat x a) &= 0 \\
a^\top b - \hat x\, a^\top a &= 0 \\
\hat x &= \frac{a^\top b}{a^\top a} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The projection is $p = \hat x a$. Since $a^\top b$ is a number, it can be moved, and $p = Pb$ with the **projection matrix**

$$
P = \frac{a a^\top}{a^\top a} .
$$

Doubling $b$ doubles $p$; doubling $a$ does not change $p$, because only the line matters ([Strang, 2011](#references), lecture 15). It is the closest point of the line: any other point $ta$ has $b - ta = e + (\hat x - t)\,a$ with $e \perp a$, so by Pythagoras

$$
\lVert b - ta\rVert^2 = \lVert e\rVert^2 + (\hat x - t)^2 \lVert a\rVert^2 \ge \lVert e\rVert^2 .
$$

For $a = (2, 1)$ and $b = (3, 4)$: $a^\top b = 10$ and $a^\top a = 5$, so $\hat x = 2$, $p = (4, 2)$ and $e = (-1, 2)$. The error is perpendicular, $a^\top e = -2 + 2 = 0$, and $\lVert b\rVert^2 = 25 = 20 + 5 = \lVert p\rVert^2 + \lVert e\rVert^2$. The projection matrix and its action are

$$
\begin{aligned}
P &= \frac15 \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \\
Pb &= \frac15 \begin{pmatrix} 12 + 8 \\ 6 + 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

## Projection onto a subspace

Let the subspace be the column space of $A \in \mathbb R^{m \times n}$, with independent columns $a_1, \dots, a_n$. A point of it is $A\hat x$, and the error $e = b - A\hat x$ must now be orthogonal to every column, $a_j^\top e = 0$, which is $A^\top e = 0$ written as one vector equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^\top (b - A\hat x) &= 0 \\
A^\top A\, \hat x &= A^\top b \\
\hat x &= (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top b .
\end{aligned}
$$

The square system in the second line is the **normal equations**. Its matrix $A^\top A$ is invertible because the columns are independent: if $A^\top A z = 0$, then $z^\top A^\top A z = \lVert Az\rVert^2 = 0$, so $Az = 0$ and $z = 0$. The projection is $p = A\hat x = Pb$, with

$$
P = A\,(A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top .
$$

It is tempting to simplify $(A^\top A)^{-1}$ to $A^{-1}(A^\top)^{-1}$, but a rectangular $A$ has no inverse. When $A$ is square and invertible, the simplification is allowed and gives $P = I$: the column space is the whole space and already contains $b$ ([Strang, 2011](#references), lecture 15). The point $p$ is the closest one by the same Pythagoras argument as for a line: for any $y$, $b - Ay = e + A(\hat x - y)$ with $e$ orthogonal to $A(\hat x - y)$, so

$$
\lVert b - Ay\rVert^2 = \lVert e\rVert^2 + \lVert A(\hat x - y)\rVert^2 .
$$

Two properties characterize the projection matrix. It is **idempotent**: projecting twice changes nothing,

$$
P^2 = A(A^\top A)^{-1}(A^\top A)(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top = P .
$$

And it is **symmetric**, $P^\top = P$, because $(A^\top A)^{-1}$ is. Its trace is $n$, the dimension of the subspace. The error $e = (I - P)b$ lies in the left null space $\ker A^\top$ of the [previous note](/notes/math/matrices-and-linear-systems#four-subspaces), and $I - P$ is the projection onto it: every $b$ splits uniquely into a part in $\operatorname{col}(A)$ and a part orthogonal to it.

Return to the opening: the plane spanned by $a_1 = (1, 0, 1)$ and $a_2 = (0, 1, 1)$, and $b = (1, 2, 6)$. The normal equations are

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^\top A &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \\
A^\top b &= \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix}, \\
\hat x &= \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

So $p = 2a_1 + 3a_2 = (2, 3, 5)$ and $e = (-1, -1, 1)$, with $a_1^\top e = 0$ and $a_2^\top e = 0$. The distance from $b$ to the plane is $\lVert e\rVert = \sqrt 3$. The projection matrix has trace 2:

$$
P = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

## Least squares as a projection

When $Ax = b$ has no solution, the least-squares solution minimizes $\lVert Ax - b\rVert$, the distance from $b$ to the point $Ax$ of the column space. By the previous section the minimizer is the projection: $A\hat x = Pb$, and $\hat x$ solves the normal equations. The calculus derivation in the [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations) and the geometric one here give the same equations. The [geometry section](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-geometry-a-projection) of that note draws the projection for a regression with three observations, calls $P$ the hat matrix, and reads its diagonal as the leverage of each point. In the opening example the best combination is $\hat x = (2, 3)$, and no combination comes closer to $b$ than $\sqrt 3$.

The normal equations are the right description and often the wrong computation: forming $A^\top A$ squares the condition number of the problem, as the [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#when-the-solution-is-unstable) shows with polynomial features. The QR factorization below avoids forming it.

## Orthonormal bases

Vectors $q_1, \dots, q_n$ are **orthonormal** if they are mutually orthogonal and of unit length: $q_i^\top q_j = 1$ for $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise. As the columns of a matrix $Q$, this is $Q^\top Q = I$. Orthonormal vectors are automatically independent: if $\sum_j c_j q_j = 0$, the inner product with $q_i$ leaves $c_i = 0$.

In an orthonormal basis, coordinates need no linear system. If $x = \sum_j c_j q_j$, the inner product with $q_i$ removes every term but one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_i &= q_i^\top x, \\
x &= \sum_i (q_i^\top x)\, q_i .
\end{aligned}
$$

For orthonormal $q_1, \dots, q_n$ spanning a subspace of $\mathbb R^m$, the projection onto it is $p = QQ^\top b$, the formula $A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ with $A = Q$ and $Q^\top Q = I$. With $q_1 = (0.8, 0.6)$ and $q_2 = (-0.6, 0.8)$, the vector $x = (2, 4)$ has coordinates $q_1^\top x = 4$ and $q_2^\top x = 2$, and indeed $4q_1 + 2q_2 = (2, 4)$.

## Gram–Schmidt and the QR factorization \{#gram-schmidt-and-qr\}

How do we get an orthonormal basis of a given subspace? Elimination made a matrix triangular; the **Gram–Schmidt process** makes it orthonormal ([Strang, 2011](#references), lecture 17). It turns independent vectors $a_1, \dots, a_n$ into orthonormal $q_1, \dots, q_n$ with the same span at every stage: take the vectors one at a time, remove from each its projections onto the $q$'s already built, and normalize what remains:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_j &= a_j - \sum_{i \lt j} (q_i^\top a_j)\, q_i, \\
q_j &= \frac{v_j}{\lVert v_j\rVert} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The remainder $v_j$ is orthogonal to every earlier $q_k$, since $q_k^\top q_i = 0$ for $i \ne k$ leaves one term of the sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_k^\top v_j &= q_k^\top a_j - \sum_{i \lt j} (q_i^\top a_j)\, q_k^\top q_i \\
&= q_k^\top a_j - q_k^\top a_j = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

It is nonzero, because $a_j$ is not in the span of $a_1, \dots, a_{j-1}$, which is the span of $q_1, \dots, q_{j-1}$.

Record the coefficients as $r_{ij} = q_i^\top a_j$ for $i \lt j$ and $r_{jj} = \lVert v_j\rVert$. Solving the definition of $v_j$ for $a_j$ writes each column of $A$ as a combination of the first $j$ columns of $Q$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_j &= v_j + \sum_{i \lt j} (q_i^\top a_j)\, q_i \\
&= r_{jj}\, q_j + \sum_{i \lt j} r_{ij}\, q_i .
\end{aligned}
$$

The coefficients of column $j$, $(r_{1j}, \dots, r_{jj}, 0, \dots, 0)$, have zeros below position $j$. Collected as the columns of an upper triangular $R$ with a positive diagonal, they give the **QR factorization**

$$
A = QR ,
$$

where $Q^\top Q = I$.

For $a_1 = (4, 3)$ and $a_2 = (2, 4)$, the figure below:

1. $r_{11} = \lVert a_1\rVert = 5$ and $q_1 = (0.8, 0.6)$.
2. $r_{12} = q_1^\top a_2 = 4$, so the component of $a_2$ along $q_1$ is $4q_1 = (3.2, 2.4)$.
3. $v_2 = a_2 - 4q_1 = (-1.2, 1.6)$, and $q_1^\top v_2 = -0.96 + 0.96 = 0$.
4. $r_{22} = \lVert v_2\rVert = 2$ and $q_2 = (-0.6, 0.8)$.

So

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & -0.6 \\ 0.6 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The second column of $R$, $(4, 2)$, is the coordinate vector of $a_2$ in the basis $q_1, q_2$ found in the previous section.

### Least squares through QR

With $A = QR$ the normal equations lose $Q$, because $Q^\top Q = I$, and then $R^\top$, which is invertible because $R$ is triangular with a positive diagonal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^\top A\, \hat x &= A^\top b \\
R^\top Q^\top Q R\, \hat x &= R^\top Q^\top b \\
R^\top R\, \hat x &= R^\top Q^\top b \\
R\, \hat x &= Q^\top b .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last system is triangular and is solved by back substitution; $A^\top A$ is never formed. For the opening example, Gram–Schmidt gives $q_1 = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt 2$ and

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_2 &= (0, 1, 1) - \tfrac12 (1, 0, 1) = \big(-\tfrac12, 1, \tfrac12\big) , \\
q_2 &= \frac{v_2}{\lVert v_2\rVert} = \frac{(-1, 2, 1)}{\sqrt 6} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

since $\lVert v_2\rVert^2 = 3/2$. The triangular system is

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \sqrt 2 & 1/\sqrt 2 \\ 0 & \sqrt{3/2} \end{pmatrix} \hat x = \begin{pmatrix} 7/\sqrt 2 \\ 9/\sqrt 6 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Its last row gives $\hat x_2 = 3$, and then the first, multiplied by $\sqrt 2$, reads $2\hat x_1 + 3 = 7$, so $\hat x_1 = 2$, as before.

## Orthogonal matrices

A square matrix $Q$ with orthonormal columns is **orthogonal**: $Q^\top Q = I$, so $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$ and also $QQ^\top = I$, which says the rows are orthonormal too. An orthogonal matrix changes coordinates and keeps every inner product, hence every length, distance and angle:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle Qx, Qy\rangle &= (Qx)^\top (Qy) \\
&= x^\top Q^\top Q\, y \\
&= x^\top y .
\end{aligned}
$$

The norm is the case $y = x$: $\lVert Qx\rVert = \lVert x\rVert$. Products of orthogonal matrices are orthogonal, since $(Q_1 Q_2)^\top Q_1 Q_2 = Q_2^\top Q_2 = I$. In numerical work this is their main virtue: multiplying by $Q$ does not amplify errors in $x$, because it does not amplify anything ([Oseledets, 2024](#references), lecture 2).

Rotations, reflections and permutations are orthogonal. The rotation by $30°$ moves $x = (4, 3)$ and $y = (2, 4)$ but keeps $\lVert x\rVert = 5$ and $x^\top y = 20$. A **Householder reflection** reflects in the hyperplane orthogonal to a vector $v$:

$$
H = I - \frac{2\, vv^\top}{v^\top v} .
$$

With $v = x - \lVert x\rVert e_1$ it sends $x$ to $\lVert x\rVert e_1$. For $x = (4, 3)$, $v = (-1, 3)$ and $v^\top v = 10$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
H &= I - \frac{2}{10} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ -3 & 9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & -0.8 \end{pmatrix}, \\
H \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} &= \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Householder QR applies one such reflection per column to clear everything below the diagonal; the product of the reflections is $Q^\top$. Givens rotations, which act on two coordinates at a time, do the same one entry at a time and suit sparse matrices.

PCA rotates centred data into the orthonormal basis of its principal directions, an orthogonal change of coordinates that keeps all distances ([dimensionality reduction](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction)). Orthogonal weight matrices keep the scale of a signal through any number of linear layers, which motivates **orthogonal initialization** ([Saxe, McClelland and Ganguli, 2014](#references)) and the orthogonal regularization of [BigGAN](/notes/generative-models/gan-scaling#orthogonal-regularization).

The Code section multiplies 50 random $100 \times 100$ matrices. With Gaussian entries of variance $1/100$, whose average effect on the squared length is exactly 1, the product stretches some direction by a factor of 6.9 and shrinks another by a factor below $10^{-16}$, too small for double precision to resolve: that direction of the input is lost. With orthogonal matrices every direction keeps its length exactly. The [normalization note](/notes/deep-learning/normalization-and-initialization#why-the-scale-is-multiplied-at-every-layer) studies the same multiplication of scales with nonlinearities in between.

## Where it breaks

Gram–Schmidt is exact in exact arithmetic and fragile in floating point. Classical Gram–Schmidt projects the original $a_j$; when the columns are nearly dependent, $v_j$ is a small difference of large vectors, and rounding errors in it are no longer orthogonal to the earlier $q$'s. Modified Gram–Schmidt subtracts the projections one at a time from the running remainder. It is the same in exact arithmetic and much better in floating point. Library routines (`numpy.linalg.qr`, LAPACK) use Householder reflections instead.

Take the polynomial features $1, t, \dots, t^{11}$ on 50 points of $[0, 1]$, a $50 \times 12$ matrix with condition number about $10^8$, and measure how far the computed $Q$ is from orthonormal:

| Method | $\lVert Q^\top Q - I\rVert_F$ |
| --- | --- |
| classical Gram–Schmidt | $2$ |
| modified Gram–Schmidt | $3 \cdot 10^{-9}$ |
| Householder QR | $2 \cdot 10^{-15}$ |

Classical Gram–Schmidt loses orthogonality completely. The modified version loses about what the condition number times the rounding unit, $10^8 \cdot 10^{-16}$, predicts. Householder stays at rounding level. Why algorithms that agree in exact arithmetic differ this much is the subject of the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability).

The other formulas have limits of their own. With dependent columns $A^\top A$ is singular; the projection still exists, and the [SVD note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) computes it with the pseudoinverse. And the Euclidean inner product is a choice: distances and angles change when features are rescaled, so features in different units need [scaling](/notes/ml/data-features-and-leakage#scaling) first, or a weighted inner product such as the Mahalanobis one.

## What you should remember

- An inner product is symmetric, linear and positive definite; it gives lengths, distances, angles and Pythagoras.
- Cauchy–Schwarz, $\lvert\langle u, v\rangle\rvert \le \lVert u\rVert\, \lVert v\rVert$: a projection is never longer than the vector.
- For unit vectors, $\lVert u - v\rVert^2 = 2 - 2\cos(u, v)$; random directions in $\mathbb R^d$ have cosines of about $1/\sqrt d$.
- Projection: the error is orthogonal to the subspace, $A^\top A\hat x = A^\top b$, $P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$ with $P^2 = P = P^\top$.
- Gram–Schmidt gives $A = QR$, and least squares becomes $R\hat x = Q^\top b$; in practice, Householder QR.
- Orthogonal matrices keep lengths and angles: $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$.

## Code

The examples of the note: norms, the parallelogram law, Cauchy–Schwarz, cosine against distance, the two projections, Gram–Schmidt and QR, the loss of orthogonality, orthogonal matrices and fifty layers of random matrices. Seeds are fixed; exact numbers can differ across platforms and library versions, especially for the orthogonality errors and the smallest stretch factor, and Householder QR may return columns of $Q$ with the opposite sign. The script runs in a few seconds.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
norm = np.linalg.norm

# norms of v = (3, -4), of x = (0.6, 0.8), and the Frobenius norm of a matrix
v = np.array([3., -4])
print(norm(v, 1), norm(v), norm(v, np.inf))                   # 7.0 5.0 4.0
x0 = np.array([0.6, 0.8])
print(round(norm(x0, 1), 6), round(norm(x0), 6), round(norm(x0, np.inf), 6))   # 1.4 1.0 0.8: outside, on, inside the unit balls
A = np.array([[1., 2], [3, 4]])
print(round(norm(A, 'fro'), 3), round(np.sqrt(np.trace(A.T @ A)), 3))   # 5.477 5.477

# the parallelogram law holds for the 2-norm and fails for the 1-norm
e1, e2 = np.eye(2)
for p in (2, 1):
    print(p, round(norm(e1 + e2, p) ** 2 + norm(e1 - e2, p) ** 2, 6), 2 * (norm(e1, p) ** 2 + norm(e2, p) ** 2))
# 2 4.0 4.0 | 1 8.0 4.0

# Cauchy-Schwarz and the angle between u = (1, 2, 2) and w = (2, 0, 1)
u, w = np.array([1., 2, 2]), np.array([2., 0, 1])
c = u @ w / (norm(u) * norm(w))
print(u @ w, norm(u), round(norm(w), 3), round(c, 3), round(np.degrees(np.arccos(c)), 1))   # 4.0 3.0 2.236 0.596 53.4
X, Y = rng.normal(size=(2, 100_000, 5))
print(round((np.abs((X * Y).sum(1)) / (norm(X, axis=1) * norm(Y, axis=1))).max(), 4))      # 0.9979: never above 1

# cosine similarity: for unit vectors, squared distance = 2 - 2 cos, so the neighbours agree
E = rng.normal(size=(1000, 50)) * rng.lognormal(0, 0.5, size=(1000, 1))   # vectors of varying length
q = rng.normal(size=(100, 50))
cos = (q @ E.T) / (norm(q, axis=1)[:, None] * norm(E, axis=1))
dist = norm(q[:, None, :] - E[None], axis=2)
En = E / norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True); qn = q / norm(q, axis=1, keepdims=True)
dist_n = norm(qn[:, None, :] - En[None], axis=2)
print((cos.argmax(1) == dist.argmin(1)).mean(), (cos.argmax(1) == dist_n.argmin(1)).mean(),
      np.allclose(dist_n ** 2, 2 - 2 * cos))
# 0.25 1.0 True: raw distances pick other neighbours; after normalizing they agree with cosine
U1, V1 = rng.normal(size=(2, 20_000, 768))
c768 = (U1 * V1).sum(1) / (norm(U1, axis=1) * norm(V1, axis=1))
print(round(c768.std(), 4), round(1 / np.sqrt(768), 4))      # 0.0361 0.0361: random directions are nearly orthogonal

# projection onto the line through a = (2, 1)
a, b = np.array([2., 1]), np.array([3., 4])
p = (a @ b) / (a @ a) * a
Pa = np.outer(a, a) / (a @ a)
print((a @ b) / (a @ a), p, b - p, a @ (b - p), b @ b, p @ p + (b - p) @ (b - p), (5 * Pa).tolist())
# 2.0 [4. 2.] [-1.  2.] 0.0 25.0 25.0 [[4.0, 2.0], [2.0, 1.0]]
print(np.allclose(np.outer(2 * a, 2 * a) / ((2 * a) @ (2 * a)), Pa))   # True: doubling a leaves P unchanged

# projection onto the plane spanned by (1, 0, 1) and (0, 1, 1)
A2 = np.column_stack([[1., 0, 1], [0, 1, 1]]); b2 = np.array([1., 2, 6])
xh = np.linalg.solve(A2.T @ A2, A2.T @ b2)
P = A2 @ np.linalg.inv(A2.T @ A2) @ A2.T
print(xh, A2 @ xh, b2 - A2 @ xh, A2.T @ (b2 - A2 @ xh))         # [2. 3.] [2. 3. 5.] [-1. -1.  1.] [0. 0.]
print((3 * P).round(6).tolist(), np.allclose(P @ P, P), np.allclose(P, P.T), round(np.trace(P), 6))
# [[2.0, -1.0, 1.0], [-1.0, 2.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0, 2.0]] True True 2.0

# Gram-Schmidt, by hand on a1 = (4, 3), a2 = (2, 4)
def gram_schmidt(A, modified=False):
    m, n = A.shape; Q = np.zeros((m, n)); R = np.zeros((n, n))
    for j in range(n):
        vj = A[:, j].copy()
        for i in range(j):
            R[i, j] = Q[:, i] @ (vj if modified else A[:, j])   # classical: project a_j; modified: the running v_j
            vj -= R[i, j] * Q[:, i]
        R[j, j] = norm(vj); Q[:, j] = vj / R[j, j]
    return Q, R
Q, R = gram_schmidt(np.array([[4., 2], [3, 4]]))
print(Q.round(6).tolist(), R.round(6).tolist())    # [[0.8, -0.6], [0.6, 0.8]] [[5.0, 4.0], [0.0, 2.0]]
print((Q.T @ [2, 4]).round(6), ((Q.T @ Q).round(6) + 0).tolist())   # [4. 2.] [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]]: coordinates of a2 = column 2 of R

# QR of the 3 x 2 matrix, and least squares through R x = Q^T b
Q3, R3 = gram_schmidt(A2)
print(R3.round(4).tolist(), (Q3 * np.sqrt(6)).round(4)[:, 1], np.linalg.solve(R3, Q3.T @ b2), Q3 @ Q3.T @ b2)
# [[1.4142, 0.7071], [0.0, 1.2247]] [-1.  2.  1.] [2. 3.] [2. 3. 5.]
Qn, Rn = np.linalg.qr(A2)                          # Householder (LAPACK); columns may differ in sign
print(np.allclose(np.abs(Rn), np.abs(R3)), np.allclose(Qn @ Rn, A2))   # True True

# loss of orthogonality: classical and modified Gram-Schmidt against Householder QR
V = np.vander(np.linspace(0, 1, 50), 12, increasing=True)            # polynomial features 1, t, ..., t^11
print(f'{np.linalg.cond(V):.0e}', *[f'{norm(Qx.T @ Qx - np.eye(12)):.0e}' for Qx in
      (gram_schmidt(V)[0], gram_schmidt(V, modified=True)[0], np.linalg.qr(V)[0])])
# 1e+08 2e+00 3e-09 2e-15   (cond(V); orthogonality error of classical GS, modified GS, Householder)

# orthogonal matrices: a rotation and a Householder reflection preserve lengths and inner products
t = np.radians(30); Rot = np.array([[np.cos(t), -np.sin(t)], [np.sin(t), np.cos(t)]])
x, y = np.array([4., 3]), np.array([2., 4])
print(np.allclose(Rot.T @ Rot, np.eye(2)), round(norm(Rot @ x), 6), round((Rot @ x) @ (Rot @ y), 6), x @ y)   # True 5.0 20.0 20.0
h = x - norm(x) * e1                                 # reflect (4, 3) onto the first axis
H = np.eye(2) - 2 * np.outer(h, h) / (h @ h)
print(H.round(6).tolist(), (H @ x).round(6) + 0, np.allclose(H @ H, np.eye(2)), round(np.linalg.det(H), 6))
# [[0.8, 0.6], [0.6, -0.8]] [5. 0.] True -1.0

# fifty linear layers of width 100: Gaussian weights (variance 1/n) against orthogonal weights;
# the largest and smallest stretch factors of the product (its largest and smallest singular values)
n, L = 100, 50
G, O = np.eye(n), np.eye(n)
for _ in range(L):
    G = rng.normal(size=(n, n)) / np.sqrt(n) @ G
    O = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(n, n)))[0] @ O
sG, sO = np.linalg.svd(G, compute_uv=False), np.linalg.svd(O, compute_uv=False)
print(f'{sG[0]:.1f} {sG[-1]:.0e} {sO[0]:.6f} {sO[-1]:.6f}')   # 6.9 2e-20 1.000000 1.000000
# (the smallest Gaussian value is below the rounding level of double precision: only its smallness is meaningful)
```
