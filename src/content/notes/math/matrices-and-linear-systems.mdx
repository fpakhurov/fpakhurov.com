Two equations in two unknowns are solved at school by substitution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x - y &= 1 , \\
x + y &= 5 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second equation gives $y = 5 - x$; substituting it into the first, $2x - (5 - x) = 1$, so $3x = 6$, $x = 2$ and $y = 3$. Fitting a linear model, evaluating a Gaussian density, taking a Newton step and inverting a layer of a normalizing flow all ask the same question with thousands of unknowns: given an output $b$, which inputs $x$ satisfy $Ax = b$?

Substitution does not scale. With a thousand unknowns there is no obvious order in which to substitute, and no estimate of the work. It also gives no warning in advance. Replace the second equation by $4x - 2y = 3$ and substitution ends in the contradiction $2 = 3$; replace it by $4x - 2y = 2$ and it ends in $2 = 2$, true for every point of the line $y = 2x - 1$. Whether a system has no solution, one, or infinitely many is a property of the matrix and the right-hand side, and it can be computed before solving.

## Three readings of Ax = b \{#three-readings\}

The system above is $Ax = b$ with

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \\
b &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and the unknown $x = (x_1, x_2)$. Read by rows, each equation is a line in the plane of the unknowns, here $2x_1 - x_2 = 1$ and $x_1 + x_2 = 5$, and a solution is a point on both. In $\mathbb R^n$ each equation is a hyperplane, a flat set of dimension $n - 1$, and the solutions are their intersection. For a nonzero row $a^\top$, the solutions of $a^\top x = 0$ are the kernel of a map of rank 1, of dimension $n - 1$ by rank–nullity, and those of $a^\top x = b_i$ are that kernel shifted by one solution, as the [existence section](#existence-and-uniqueness) shows.

Read by columns, $Ax$ is the combination $x_1 a_1 + x_2 a_2$ of the columns $a_1 = (2, 1)$ and $a_2 = (-1, 1)$, and the question is which combination gives $b$. The answer, $2a_1 + 3a_2 = (1, 5)$, is the solution $x = (2, 3)$.

Read as a map, $x \mapsto Ax$ is linear, and the question is whether $b$ lies in its image and which inputs reach it. A solution exists exactly when $b$ is in the column space, and any two solutions differ by a vector of the kernel.

Elimination manipulates the rows; the columns and the map explain when a solution exists ([Strang, 2016](#references), section 1.3).

## Gaussian elimination

Elimination uses one operation: subtract a multiple of one equation from another. It does not change the set of solutions, because adding the same multiple back undoes it. The goal is to clear the entries below the diagonal, one column at a time. In column $k$ the diagonal entry $a_{kk}$ is the **pivot**. For each row $i$ below it, the **multiplier** $\ell_{ik} = a_{ik}/a_{kk}$, computed from the current entries, times row $k$ is subtracted from row $i$, which makes its entry in column $k$ zero.

Take three equations, written as the **augmented matrix** $[A \mid b]$, which appends $b$ to $A$ as an extra column:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x_1 + x_2 - x_3 &= 1 \\
4x_1 + 5x_2 - 3x_3 &= 5 \\
-2x_1 + 5x_2 - 2x_3 &= 2 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
[A \mid b] = \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 2 & 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 4 & 5 & -3 & 5 \\ -2 & 5 & -2 & 2 \end{array}\right) .
$$

1. In column 1 the pivot is $2$. Row 2 has the multiplier $\ell_{21} = 4/2 = 2$, and row 2 minus twice row 1 is $(0, 3, -1 \mid 3)$. Row 3 has $\ell_{31} = -2/2 = -1$, and row 3 plus row 1 is $(0, 6, -3 \mid 3)$.
2. In column 2 the pivot is now $3$. Row 3 has $\ell_{32} = 6/3 = 2$, and row 3 minus twice row 2 is $(0, 0, -1 \mid -3)$.
3. The system is now upper triangular, all its entries below the diagonal being zero, and it is solved from the bottom up by **back substitution**:

$$
\left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 2 & 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & -1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & -3 \end{array}\right) ,
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
-x_3 &= -3 &&\Rightarrow\ x_3 = 3 , \\
3x_2 - 3 &= 3 &&\Rightarrow\ x_2 = 2 , \\
2x_1 + 2 - 3 &= 1 &&\Rightarrow\ x_1 = 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The solution $x = (1, 2, 3)$ satisfies the three original equations: $2 + 2 - 3 = 1$, $4 + 10 - 9 = 5$ and $-2 + 10 - 6 = 2$. The pivots $2$, $3$ and $-1$ were all nonzero, which is what made each step possible.

## Elimination is a factorization: A = LU \{#lu-factorization\}

The multipliers $2, -1, 2$ were used once and seemingly thrown away. Kept, they give a factorization of $A$. Collect the multipliers of step $k$ in the vector $\ell_k = (0, \dots, 0, \ell_{k+1,k}, \dots, \ell_{nk})$, with zeros in positions $1$ to $k$. Subtracting $\ell_{ik}$ times row $k$ from every row $i \gt k$ is multiplication on the left by

$$
E_k = I - \ell_k e_k^\top ,
$$

because $e_k^\top M$ is row $k$ of $M$; here $I$ is the identity and $e_k$ the $k$-th standard basis vector. Adding the rows back undoes the step, $E_k^{-1} = I + \ell_k e_k^\top$: the product $(I - \ell_k e_k^\top)(I + \ell_k e_k^\top)$ equals $I - \ell_k (e_k^\top \ell_k)\, e_k^\top$, and $e_k^\top \ell_k = 0$ because $\ell_k$ has a zero in position $k$. For three unknowns, elimination and its reversal read

$$
\begin{aligned}
U &= E_2 E_1 A , \\
A &= E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1}\, U \\
&= \big(I + \ell_1 e_1^\top\big)\big(I + \ell_2 e_2^\top\big)\, U \\
&= \big(I + \ell_1 e_1^\top + \ell_2 e_2^\top\big)\, U .
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiplying out the two brackets leaves a cross term $\ell_1 (e_1^\top \ell_2)\, e_2^\top$, which vanishes because the first entry of $\ell_2$ is zero. The last bracket has $\ell_1$ in column 1 and $\ell_2$ in column 2 below a diagonal of ones. It is a lower triangular matrix $L$, with the multiplier $\ell_{ik}$ in row $i$ and column $k$, and

$$
A = LU ,
$$

where $U$ is the upper triangular matrix at the end of elimination. For any $n$ the same expansion of $E_1^{-1} \cdots E_{n-1}^{-1}$ gives $I + \ell_1 e_1^\top + \dots + \ell_{n-1} e_{n-1}^\top$: every product of two or more of the terms contains a factor $e_j^\top \ell_k$ with $j \lt k$, which is zero because $\ell_k$ has zeros in positions 1 to $k$. In the example,

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 4 & 5 & -3 \\ -2 & 5 & -2 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Row 3 of the product is $-1 \cdot (2, 1, -1) + 2 \cdot (0, 3, -1) + 1 \cdot (0, 0, -1) = (-2, 5, -2)$, the third row of $A$.

The factorization separates the matrix from the right-hand side. Solving $Ax = b$ becomes two triangular solves: first $Lc = b$ from the top down, by **forward substitution**, then $Ux = c$ from the bottom up. Here $c_1 = 1$, then $2c_1 + c_2 = 5$ gives $c_2 = 3$, and $-c_1 + 2c_2 + c_3 = 2$ gives $c_3 = -3$, the right-hand column that elimination produced. Once $A$ is factored, every new $b$ costs only the two triangular solves.

## Row exchanges and pivoting

Elimination needs a nonzero pivot. The system

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

has the solution $x = (1, 1)$, but its first pivot is zero. Exchanging the two equations fixes it. In general rows are exchanged whenever needed, and the factorization becomes $PA = LU$, where the **permutation matrix** $P$ is the identity with its rows reordered. Every invertible matrix has such a factorization. A nonzero pivot can always be found: if at step $k$ every entry of column $k$ from row $k$ down were zero, the first $k$ columns of the current matrix $EA$ would lie in the span of $e_1, \dots, e_{k-1}$ and so be dependent; some $z \ne 0$ would give $EAz = 0$, hence $Az = 0$, and $A$ would be singular. That the exchanges made along the way can be collected into one $P$ in front, with the multipliers reordered inside $L$, is shown in [Trefethen and Bau (1997)](#references), lecture 21.

A small pivot is nearly as bad as a zero one. Replace the $0$ above by $10^{-17}$. Without an exchange the multiplier is $10^{17}$. Double precision keeps about 16 significant digits ([floating point](/notes/acos/floating-point)), so the entry $1 - 10^{17}$ rounds to $-10^{17}$, and so does the right-hand side $2 - 10^{17}$. Back substitution then gives $x_2 = 1$ and $x_1 = (1 - 1)/10^{-17} = 0$, while the true solution is $(1, 1)$ to sixteen digits. The information in the second equation has been rounded away.

**Partial pivoting** prevents this. At each step it exchanges rows so that the pivot is the entry of largest absolute value in its column, which keeps every multiplier at most 1 in absolute value. Library routines (`scipy.linalg.lu`, and `numpy.linalg.solve`, which calls LAPACK) always pivot. On the worked example the first pivot becomes $4$ instead of $2$; the factors change and the solution does not. The entries of $U$ can still grow. One step replaces an entry by $a_{ij} - \ell_{ik} a_{kj}$, and with $\lvert\ell_{ik}\rvert \le 1$ that is at most twice the largest entry in absolute value, so $n - 1$ steps grow the entries by a factor of at most $2^{n-1}$. Contrived matrices reach this bound, and practical ones almost never come close ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), lecture 22; [Oseledets, 2024](#references), lecture 5); the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) shows what the size of the error depends on instead.

## What elimination costs

Step $k$ changes only the block below and to the right of the pivot: $n - k$ rows, each with $n - k$ entries, at one multiply–add per entry. The entries of column $k$ become zero by construction and are not computed. Summing over the steps, with $j = n - k$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} (n - k)^2 &= \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} j^2 \\
&= \frac{(n - 1)\, n\, (2n - 1)}{6} \approx \frac{n^3}{3}
\end{aligned}
$$

multiply–adds for the LU factorization of an $n \times n$ matrix. The $n - k$ divisions for the multipliers add only a lower-order term. Counting the multiplication and the addition as two floating-point operations gives the $\tfrac23 n^3$ flops quoted by [Boyd and Vandenberghe (2004](#references), appendix C).

For the $3 \times 3$ example the count is $2^2 + 1^2 = 5$: four updated entries in step 1 and one in step 2. For $n = 1000$ it is $332{,}833{,}500$, close to $n^3/3$. Each triangular solve costs about $n^2/2$ multiply–adds, since row $i$ uses the $i - 1$ unknowns already found and $1 + 2 + \dots + (n - 1) = n(n - 1)/2$; so with the factors at hand a new right-hand side costs $n^2$: for $n = 1000$, a million operations instead of a third of a billion.

## Rank and the four fundamental subspaces \{#four-subspaces\}

Elimination works on any rectangular matrix $A \in \mathbb R^{m \times n}$. It produces an **echelon form** $R$, in which each nonzero row starts with a pivot further right than the row above and zero rows come last. The **rank** $r$ is the number of pivots. Four subspaces belong to every $A$:

- the **column space** $\operatorname{col}(A) \subseteq \mathbb R^m$, spanned by the columns: the image of $x \mapsto Ax$;
- the **row space** $\operatorname{row}(A) = \operatorname{col}(A^\top) \subseteq \mathbb R^n$, spanned by the rows;
- the **kernel** (null space) $\ker A = \{x : Ax = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb R^n$;
- the **left null space** $\ker A^\top = \{y : A^\top y = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb R^m$, the combinations of rows that vanish, $y^\top A = 0$.

Their dimensions are all fixed by $r$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dim \operatorname{row}(A) &= \dim \operatorname{col}(A) = r , \\
\dim \ker A &= n - r , \\
\dim \ker A^\top &= m - r .
\end{aligned}
$$

That the number of independent rows equals the number of independent columns is not obvious. A $3 \times 100$ matrix has at most 3 independent rows, so it has at most 3 independent columns among its hundred. Elimination is the reason: it changes neither the row space nor the kernel, and in the echelon form both counts can be read off ([Strang, 2016](#references), section 3.5). Write the elimination as $R = EA$, with $E$ invertible because every step can be undone. Then:

1. Every row of $R$ is a combination of rows of $A$ and, since $E$ is invertible, the other way round, so $\operatorname{row}(A) = \operatorname{row}(R)$. The nonzero rows of $R$ are independent, since each has its pivot in a column where the rows below it are zero. So $\dim \operatorname{row}(A) = r$.
2. $Ax = 0$ exactly when $Rx = EAx = 0$, so a combination of columns vanishes for $A$ exactly when the same combination vanishes for $R$. In $R$ the $r$ pivot columns are independent: in a vanishing combination of them, the last pivot row is nonzero only in the last pivot column, so that coefficient is zero; the row above then clears the next coefficient, and so on up. All columns of $R$ have zeros below row $r$, so they lie in a space of dimension $r$, of which the $r$ pivot columns are a basis; every other column is a combination of them. The same holds for the columns of $A$ in the same positions. So $\dim \operatorname{col}(A) = r$.
3. Rank–nullity ([previous note](/notes/math/vectors-and-linear-maps#rank-nullity)) gives $\dim \ker A = n - r$, and, for $A^\top$, whose rank is $\dim \operatorname{col}(A^\top) = \dim \operatorname{row}(A) = r$, it gives $\dim \ker A^\top = m - r$.

Row 2 minus twice row 1, row 3 minus three times row 1, and then row 3 minus row 2 bring a $3 \times 4$ matrix to echelon form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \\
R &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

There are two pivots, in columns 1 and 3, so $r = 2$. The pivot columns of $A$, $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$, are a basis of $\operatorname{col}(A)$: column 2 is twice column 1, and column 4 is column 1 plus twice column 3. The nonzero rows of $R$, $(1, 2, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 0, 1, 2)$, are a basis of $\operatorname{row}(A)$.

The kernel has dimension $4 - 2 = 2$. The non-pivot unknowns $x_2 = s$ and $x_4 = t$ are free, and $Rx = 0$ gives $x_3 = -2t$ and $x_1 = -2s - t$, so $\ker A$ is spanned by $(-2, 1, 0, 0)$ and $(-1, 0, -2, 1)$. The left null space has dimension $3 - 2 = 1$. Elimination showed that row 3 minus row 1 minus row 2 is zero, so it is spanned by $y = (1, 1, -1)$, and indeed $y^\top A = 0$.

The subspaces come in perpendicular pairs. Every row of $A$ has zero dot product with every kernel vector, since that is what $Ax = 0$ says, and every column has zero dot product with every vector of the left null space. Here $(1, 2, 0, 1) \cdot (-1, 0, -2, 1) = 0$ and $(1, 2, 3) \cdot (1, 1, -1) = 0$. The [next note](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality) makes "perpendicular" precise and uses these pairs for projections.

## When a solution exists, and when it is unique \{#existence-and-uniqueness\}

When is $b$ reachable? The column picture gives the first answer and the left null space gives a test:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& Ax = b \text{ has a solution} \\
\iff\ & b \in \operatorname{col}(A) \\
\iff\ & y^\top b = 0 \text{ for every } y \in \ker A^\top .
\end{aligned}
$$

The test is necessary: if $b = Ax$, then for every $y \in \ker A^\top$

$$
\begin{aligned}
y^\top b &= y^\top A x \\
&= (A^\top y)^\top x \\
&= 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

It is also sufficient. Let $Y$ have a basis of $\ker A^\top$ as its $m - r$ rows. The vectors $b$ with $Yb = 0$ form a subspace of dimension $m - (m - r) = r$, by rank–nullity. It contains $\operatorname{col}(A)$, by the chain above, and $\operatorname{col}(A)$ also has dimension $r$, so the two coincide.

Once one solution $x_p$ is known, the kernel describes all of them. If $Ax_p = b$ and $Ax = b$, then $A(x - x_p) = 0$, so the solutions are exactly the vectors $x_p + z$ with $z \in \ker A$.

For the $3 \times 4$ matrix above, $Ax = b$ is solvable exactly when $b_1 + b_2 - b_3 = 0$. For $b = (1, 3, 4)$ the test passes, elimination gives $Rx = (1, 1, 0)$, and with $x_2 = x_4 = 0$ the particular solution is $x_p = (1, 0, 1, 0)$. All solutions are $x_p + s(-2, 1, 0, 0) + t(-1, 0, -2, 1)$. For $b = (1, 3, 5)$ the test gives $-1$, and the last row of the eliminated system reads $0 = 1$: no solution exists.

The rank decides which cases are possible:

| shape and rank | solutions of $Ax = b$ |
| --- | --- |
| $r = m = n$ (square, invertible) | exactly one, for every $b$ |
| $r = n \lt m$ (tall, independent columns) | none or exactly one |
| $r = m \lt n$ (wide, independent rows) | infinitely many, for every $b$ |
| $r \lt m$ and $r \lt n$ | none or infinitely many |

A solution exists for every $b$ exactly when $r = m$, so that $\ker A^\top = \{0\}$ and the test is empty. It is unique whenever it exists exactly when $r = n$, so that $\ker A = \{0\}$.

## The inverse, and why it is rarely formed

A square matrix with $r = n$ is **invertible**: there is a matrix $A^{-1}$ with $A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I$, and then $x = A^{-1} b$. Why does it exist? By the table above, each system $Ax = e_j$ has exactly one solution; the matrix $X$ with these solutions as columns has $AX = I$. It is also a left inverse: $A(XA - I) = AXA - A = 0$, and since $\ker A = \{0\}$, every column of $XA - I$ is zero. So a one-sided inverse of a square matrix is the inverse. Column $j$ of $A^{-1}$ solves $Ax = e_j$, so the inverse can be computed by solving $n$ systems with the same LU factors. Two rules follow from the definition. The inverse of a product reverses the order, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$, because $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = AA^{-1} = I$. And $(A^\top)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^\top$, by transposing $AA^{-1} = I$ with the rule $(XY)^\top = Y^\top X^\top$, which holds entry by entry, since $(XY)_{ji} = \sum_k x_{jk} y_{ki}$.

For $2 \times 2$ matrices there is a formula. Swapping the diagonal entries and negating the others gives a matrix whose product with $A$ is a multiple of $I$; the four entries of the product are $ad - bc$, $-ab + ba$, $cd - dc$ and $-cb + da$:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} = (ad - bc)\, I .
$$

So, when $ad - bc \ne 0$,

$$
\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The number $ad - bc$ is the determinant, the subject of the [next note but one](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues). For the opening system $ad - bc = 3$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{-1} &= \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \\
A^{-1} b &= \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 5 \\ -1 + 10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The formula $x = A^{-1}b$ is the right way to write a solution in a derivation and the wrong way to compute one ([Strang, 2016](#references), section 2.5; [Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), appendix C). Forming $A^{-1}$ takes about $n^3$ multiply–adds, three times the LU factorization: $n^3/3$ for the factors, about $n^3/6$ for the forward solves $Lc = e_j$, whose solutions begin with $j - 1$ zeros, and $n \cdot n^2/2$ for the back solves. Afterwards $A^{-1}b$ costs $n^2$, the same as the two triangular solves. Nothing is saved.

It is also less accurate. The Code section builds a $100 \times 100$ matrix with nearly dependent columns, whose condition number, defined in the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability), is $10^{12}$, and solves $Ax = b$ both ways. With `numpy.linalg.solve` the relative residual $\lVert b - A\hat x\rVert / (\lVert A\rVert\, \lVert \hat x\rVert)$ is about $10^{-16}$, at the level of rounding. Through the computed inverse it is $7 \cdot 10^{-7}$, and the error in $\hat x$ grows from $8 \cdot 10^{-6}$ to $9 \cdot 10^{-5}$. A solver with a small residual has solved a nearby system exactly; the inverse gives no such guarantee.

And the inverse destroys structure. The $5 \times 5$ matrix with $2$ on the diagonal and $-1$ next to it has 13 nonzero entries, and its LU factors keep that band, because each elimination step subtracts the pivot row, with two nonzeros, from the one row below it. Its inverse has all 25 entries nonzero; its first row is $\tfrac16 (5, 4, 3, 2, 1)$. For a million unknowns the band has three million entries and the inverse $10^{12}$.

One identity about inverses is worth knowing because it saves work. Suppose $A$ is $n \times n$ and easy to solve with, diagonal or already factored, and it changes by a low-rank term $UCV$, with $U$ of size $n \times k$, an invertible $C$ of size $k \times k$ and $V$ of size $k \times n$. The **Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula**, or matrix inversion lemma, gives the new inverse through a $k \times k$ system:

$$
(A + UCV)^{-1} = A^{-1} - A^{-1}U\big(C^{-1} + VA^{-1}U\big)^{-1}VA^{-1} .
$$

It holds whenever $A$, $C$ and $S = C^{-1} + VA^{-1}U$ are invertible. Multiplying the right side by $A + UCV$ gives $I$, and a one-sided inverse of a square matrix is the inverse. Boyd and Vandenberghe derive it instead by writing $(A + UCV)x = b$ as a block system in $x$ and $y = CVx$ and eliminating $x$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: the product is the identity</summary>

Expand the product, collect the terms that end in $S^{-1}VA^{-1}$, and use $I + CVA^{-1}U = C\,S$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(A + UCV)\big(A^{-1} - A^{-1}U S^{-1} V A^{-1}\big) \\
&= I + UCVA^{-1} - U\big(I + CVA^{-1}U\big) S^{-1} VA^{-1} \\
&= I + UCVA^{-1} - UC\, S\, S^{-1} VA^{-1} \\
&= I .
\end{aligned}
$$

</details>

For a diagonal $A$ the formula needs $A^{-1}U$ ($nk$ operations), $VA^{-1}U$ ($nk^2$) and one $k \times k$ solve ($k^3$). With $n = 2000$ and $k = 5$ it solves the system in $O(nk^2)$ operations instead of $O(n^3)$, and $k = 1$ is the Sherman–Morrison update after a rank-one change. The Bayes course uses the formula to move between two sizes: [Bayesian linear regression](/notes/bayesian-methods/bayesian-linear-regression#tools-matrix-derivatives-and-gaussian-identities) inverts a $d \times d$ matrix built from $d$ features, a [Gaussian process](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#bayesian-linear-regression-is-a-gp-with-a-linear-kernel) an $N \times N$ matrix over $N$ data points, and the formula turns one inverse into the other, so the computation can be done in the smaller space.

## When there is no exact solution: least squares

Data rarely satisfy a tall system exactly. With $a_1 = (1, 0, 1)$, $a_2 = (0, 1, 1)$ and $b = (1, 2, 6)$, the left null space of $A = [a_1\ a_2]$ is spanned by $y = (1, 1, -1)$, and $y^\top b = -3 \ne 0$: no $x$ gives $Ax = b$. The useful question becomes which $x$ makes $Ax$ closest to $b$. Minimizing $\lVert Ax - b\rVert^2$ leads to the **normal equations** $A^\top A\, \hat x = A^\top b$, a square system that elimination solves; here they give $\hat x = (2, 3)$.

Their derivation by calculus and their statistics are in the [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations) of the Machine Learning course, and their geometry, with this same example, in the [next note](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality#least-squares-as-a-projection). Forming $A^\top A$ is itself a numerical risk, and the next note solves the problem through the QR factorization instead.

## Where machine learning solves linear systems

Least squares and ridge regression solve $(X^\top X + \lambda I)\, w = X^\top y$, a symmetric system usually handled by the [Cholesky factorization](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-cholesky-factorization), the variant of LU for symmetric positive definite matrices ([ridge](/notes/ml/regularization#ridge-a-penalty-on-the-size-of-the-weights)). The density of a [multivariate Gaussian](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#the-density) contains $(x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)$, and a [Gaussian process prediction](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#regression-the-posterior-in-closed-form) contains $K^{-1} y$; both are computed by solving with a factored matrix. Each step of Newton's method solves $Hd = -g$ with the Hessian $H$ and the gradient $g$ ([unconstrained optimization](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization)). Glow learns its invertible $1 \times 1$ convolutions directly in the factored form $W = PLU$, so that the inverse and the determinant cost little ([invertible convolutions](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures#c-invertible-convolutions-glow)).

## Where it breaks

Dense elimination stops being an option long before the matrices of practice run out. For $n = 10^5$ unknowns, the matrix alone takes $8n^2$ bytes, 80 GB in double precision, and the factorization $n^3/3 \approx 3 \cdot 10^{14}$ multiply–adds.

Large matrices are usually sparse, and elimination can destroy the sparsity. Take the $1000 \times 1000$ **arrow matrix**: the diagonal, plus a full first row and first column, 2,998 nonzeros in all. Eliminating the first unknown first subtracts a multiple of the full first row from every other row, and the factor fills in completely. Its Cholesky factor has 500,500 nonzeros, the whole lower triangle. Number the unknowns in reverse order, so that the full row and column come last, and the same matrix has a factor with 1,999 nonzeros. The order of elimination decides the cost ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), appendix C). Sparse solvers choose the order by heuristics, and the largest systems are solved by [iterative methods](/notes/math/iterative-methods), which only multiply by the matrix.

Exactness is the other limit. With measured data, a dependent set of columns becomes independent after the smallest perturbation, so "is $b$ in the column space?" needs a tolerance; the [SVD note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) gives the numerical rank. And for an ill-conditioned matrix even the best solver returns an $\hat x$ that solves a nearby system exactly but may be far from the true $x$; the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) measures the gap.

## What you should remember

- $Ax = b$ reads three ways: an intersection of hyperplanes, a combination of columns equal to $b$, and a point in the image of a map.
- Elimination is the factorization $A = LU$, or $PA = LU$ with row exchanges: $n^3/3$ multiply–adds once, then $n^2$ per right-hand side.
- Partial pivoting takes the largest pivot in the column and keeps every multiplier at most 1.
- The row and column spaces have dimension $r$, the kernel $n - r$, the left null space $m - r$.
- A solution exists exactly when $y^\top b = 0$ for all $y \in \ker A^\top$; all solutions are $x_p + \ker A$.
- Write $A^{-1}b$, compute with the factors; Woodbury inverts a low-rank change through a $k \times k$ system.

## Code

The examples of the note: elimination by hand with the multipliers kept, the library LU with pivoting, the cost count, the four subspaces, the solvability test, a small pivot, solving with and without the inverse, the Woodbury formula and the arrow matrix. Seeds are fixed; exact numbers can differ slightly across platforms and library versions, especially in the inverse experiment. The script runs in about a second.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import lu, lu_factor, lu_solve, null_space, solve_triangular

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
rank = np.linalg.matrix_rank

# the 2 x 2 system: row picture and column picture give the same x
M = np.array([[2., -1], [1, 1]]); bm = np.array([1., 5])
xm = np.linalg.solve(M, bm)
print(xm, xm[0] * M[:, 0] + xm[1] * M[:, 1])     # [2. 3.] [1. 5.]

# Gaussian elimination without row exchanges, keeping the multipliers
def lu_nopivot(A):
    U = A.astype(float).copy(); n = len(U); L = np.eye(n)
    for k in range(n - 1):
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            L[i, k] = U[i, k] / U[k, k]          # the multiplier
            U[i, k:] -= L[i, k] * U[k, k:]       # row i minus multiplier times the pivot row
    return L, U

A = np.array([[2., 1, -1], [4, 5, -3], [-2, 5, -2]]); b = np.array([1., 5, 2])
L, U = lu_nopivot(A)
print(L.tolist(), U.tolist(), np.allclose(L @ U, A))
# [[1.0, 0.0, 0.0], [2.0, 1.0, 0.0], [-1.0, 2.0, 1.0]] [[2.0, 1.0, -1.0], [0.0, 3.0, -1.0], [0.0, 0.0, -1.0]] True
c = solve_triangular(L, b, lower=True)           # forward substitution: Lc = b
x = solve_triangular(U, c)                       # back substitution: Ux = c
print(c, x)                                      # [ 1.  3. -3.] [1. 2. 3.]

# library LU uses partial pivoting: PA = LU with the largest available pivot; same solution
P, Lp, Up = lu(A)                                # scipy returns A = P Lp Up
print(np.allclose(P @ Lp @ Up, A), Up[0, 0], np.abs(Lp).max(), lu_solve(lu_factor(A), b))
# True 4.0 1.0 [1. 2. 3.]

# a tiny pivot: without row exchanges the answer is wrong, with partial pivoting it is right
As = np.array([[1e-17, 1], [1, 1]]); bs = np.array([1., 2])
Ls, Us = lu_nopivot(As)
print(solve_triangular(Us, solve_triangular(Ls, bs, lower=True)), np.linalg.solve(As, bs))   # [0. 1.] [1. 1.]

# multiply-adds of elimination on an n x n matrix: sum of (n - k)^2, about n^3 / 3
for n in (3, 10, 100, 1000):
    print(n, sum((n - k) ** 2 for k in range(1, n)), round(n ** 3 / 3))
# 3 5 9 | 10 285 333 | 100 328350 333333 | 1000 332833500 333333333

# the four subspaces of a 3 x 4 matrix of rank 2
A4 = np.array([[1., 2, 0, 1], [2, 4, 1, 4], [3, 6, 1, 5]])
print(rank(A4), null_space(A4).shape[1], null_space(A4.T).shape[1])   # 2 2 1: dimensions r, n - r, m - r
for v in ([-2, 1, 0, 0], [-1, 0, -2, 1]):
    print(A4 @ v)                                # [0. 0. 0.] twice: a basis of the kernel
print(np.array([1, 1, -1]) @ A4)                 # [0. 0. 0. 0.]: the left null space
# solvable exactly when b is in the column space, i.e. b1 + b2 - b3 = 0
for bb in ([1., 3, 4], [1., 3, 5]):
    print(bb, rank(np.column_stack([A4, bb])), np.array([1, 1, -1]) @ bb)
# [1.0, 3, 4] 2 0.0 | [1.0, 3, 5] 3 -1.0
xp = np.array([1., 0, 1, 0])
print(A4 @ xp, A4 @ (xp + 2 * np.array([-2, 1, 0, 0]) - np.array([-1, 0, -2, 1])))   # [1. 3. 4.] [1. 3. 4.]

# the inverse of a 2 x 2 matrix, and the inverse of a sparse matrix is dense
print(np.linalg.inv(M) * 3, np.linalg.inv(M) @ bm)    # [[ 1.  1.] [-1.  2.]] [2. 3.]
T = 2 * np.eye(5) - np.eye(5, k=1) - np.eye(5, k=-1)
Lt, Ut = lu_nopivot(T)
print(np.count_nonzero(T), np.count_nonzero(np.abs(Lt) + np.abs(Ut) > 1e-12),
      np.count_nonzero(np.abs(np.linalg.inv(T)) > 1e-12), (6 * np.linalg.inv(T)).round(6)[0])
# 13 13 25 [5. 4. 3. 2. 1.]: the factors keep the band (L and U together), the inverse fills in

# solving through the inverse: a much larger residual (n = 100, condition number 1e12)
n = 100
Q1, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(n, n))); Q2, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(n, n)))
Ak = Q1 @ np.diag(np.logspace(0, -12, n)) @ Q2.T
xt = rng.normal(size=n); bk = Ak @ xt
for name, xh in (('solve', np.linalg.solve(Ak, bk)), ('inv', np.linalg.inv(Ak) @ bk)):
    print(name, f'{np.linalg.norm(bk - Ak @ xh) / (np.linalg.norm(Ak, 2) * np.linalg.norm(xh)):.0e}',
          f'{np.linalg.norm(xh - xt) / np.linalg.norm(xt):.0e}')
# solve 1e-16 8e-06 | inv 7e-07 9e-05   (relative residual, relative error)

# Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury: diagonal plus rank 5, n = 2000, solved through a 5 x 5 system
n, k = 2000, 5
d = rng.uniform(1, 2, n); Uw = rng.normal(size=(n, k)); Cw = np.eye(k); bw = rng.normal(size=n)
Ab, AU = bw / d, Uw / d[:, None]                 # A^-1 b and A^-1 U for the diagonal A
xw = Ab - AU @ np.linalg.solve(np.linalg.inv(Cw) + Uw.T @ AU, Uw.T @ Ab)
print(np.allclose(xw, np.linalg.solve(np.diag(d) + Uw @ Cw @ Uw.T, bw)))   # True

# the arrow matrix: eliminating the full row first fills the factor in; reversing the order does not
n = 1000
Ar = n * np.eye(n); Ar[0, :] = 1; Ar[:, 0] = 1; Ar[0, 0] = n
nnz = lambda F: np.count_nonzero(np.abs(F) > 1e-14)
print(nnz(Ar), nnz(np.linalg.cholesky(Ar)), nnz(np.linalg.cholesky(Ar[::-1, ::-1])))   # 2998 500500 1999

# no exact solution: b = (1, 2, 6) is not a combination of (1, 0, 1) and (0, 1, 1)
A2 = np.column_stack([[1., 0, 1], [0, 1, 1]]); b2 = np.array([1., 2, 6])
print(rank(A2), rank(np.column_stack([A2, b2])), np.array([1, 1, -1]) @ b2, np.linalg.lstsq(A2, b2, rcond=None)[0])
# 2 3 -3.0 [2. 3.]
```
