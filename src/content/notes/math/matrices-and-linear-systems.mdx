The [previous note](/notes/math/vectors-and-linear-maps) turned a linear map into a matrix: given $x$, the product $Ax$ says where it goes. Most uses of linear algebra ask the opposite question. Given the output $b$, which inputs $x$ satisfy $Ax = b$? Fitting a model, computing a Gaussian density, taking a Newton step and inverting a layer of a normalizing flow all come down to it. This note solves the question three times over: by an algorithm, Gaussian elimination, which also factors the matrix into two triangular ones; by a theorem, which uses the rank and the kernel of the previous note to say exactly when a solution exists and how many there are; and by a warning, that the inverse matrix, the formula $x = A^{-1}b$, is almost never the way to compute $x$.

**Notation.** $A \in \mathbb R^{m \times n}$ has entries $a_{ij}$, columns $a_1, \dots, a_n$ and rank $r$; $x \in \mathbb R^n$ is the unknown and $b \in \mathbb R^m$ the right-hand side. The **augmented matrix** $[A \mid b]$ appends $b$ as an extra column. $I$ is the identity matrix and $e_k$ the $k$-th standard basis vector. A matrix is **upper triangular** if all its entries below the diagonal are zero and **lower triangular** if all those above are; $L$ is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal and $U$ upper triangular. $A^\top$ is the transpose; for vectors, $y^\top b = \sum_i y_i b_i$. The four subspaces $\operatorname{col}(A)$, $\operatorname{row}(A)$, $\ker A$ and $\ker A^\top$ are defined below.

## Baseline: substitution

Two equations in two unknowns are solved at school by substitution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x - y &= 1 \\
x + y &= 5 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second equation gives $y = 5 - x$; substituting into the first, $2x - (5 - x) = 1$, so $3x = 6$, $x = 2$ and $y = 3$.

Substitution works, but it leaves the important questions unanswered. It does not scale: with a thousand unknowns there is no obvious order in which to substitute, and no estimate of the work. And it gives no warning in advance. Replace the second equation by $4x - 2y = 3$ and substitution ends in the contradiction $2 = 3$; replace it by $4x - 2y = 2$ and it ends in $2 = 2$, true for every point of the line $y = 2x - 1$: infinitely many solutions. Which of the three cases occurs is a property of the matrix and the right-hand side, and the sections below compute it.

## Three readings of Ax = b \{#three-readings\}

The system above is $Ax = b$ with

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

It can be read in three ways.

- **The row picture.** Each equation is a line in the plane of the unknowns, here $2x_1 - x_2 = 1$ and $x_1 + x_2 = 5$, and a solution is a point on all of them. In $\mathbb R^n$ each equation is a hyperplane, a flat set of dimension $n - 1$, and the solutions are their intersection.
- **The column picture.** $Ax$ is the combination $x_1 a_1 + x_2 a_2$ of the columns, so the question is which combination of $a_1 = (2, 1)$ and $a_2 = (-1, 1)$ gives $b$. The answer $2a_1 + 3a_2 = (4 - 3,\ 2 + 3) = (1, 5)$ is the solution $x = (2, 3)$.
- **The map.** $x \mapsto Ax$ is a linear map, and the question is whether $b$ lies in its image and which inputs reach it. A solution exists exactly when $b$ is in the column space, and any two solutions differ by a vector of the kernel.

<ColumnPicture />

The row picture is the one that elimination manipulates; the column picture and the map are the ones that explain when a solution exists.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Gaussian elimination replaces $Ax = b$ by a triangular system $Ux = c$ with the same solutions, which is solved from the bottom up. The work it does is recorded as the factorization $A = LU$. Whether a solution exists depends only on whether $b$ is in the column space of $A$; how many there are depends only on the kernel.</aside>

## Gaussian elimination

Elimination uses one operation: subtract a multiple of one equation from another. This does not change the set of solutions, because it can be undone by adding the same multiple back. The plan is to clear the entries below the diagonal one column at a time. In column $k$, the entry $a_{kk}$ on the diagonal is the **pivot**; for each row $i$ below it, the **multiplier** $\ell_{ik} = a_{ik}/a_{kk}$ (the current entries, after the earlier steps) times row $k$ is subtracted from row $i$, which makes the entry in column $k$ of row $i$ zero.

**Worked example.** Solve

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x_1 + x_2 - x_3 &= 1 \\
4x_1 + 5x_2 - 3x_3 &= 5 \\
-2x_1 + 5x_2 - 2x_3 &= 2 ,
\end{aligned}
\qquad
[A \mid b] = \left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 2 & 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 4 & 5 & -3 & 5 \\ -2 & 5 & -2 & 2 \end{array}\right) .
$$

1. **Column 1.** The pivot is $2$. The multiplier for row 2 is $\ell_{21} = 4/2 = 2$: row 2 minus 2 times row 1 is $(4 - 4,\ 5 - 2,\ -3 + 2 \mid 5 - 2) = (0, 3, -1 \mid 3)$. The multiplier for row 3 is $\ell_{31} = -2/2 = -1$: row 3 minus $(-1)$ times row 1 is $(-2 + 2,\ 5 + 1,\ -2 - 1 \mid 2 + 1) = (0, 6, -3 \mid 3)$.
2. **Column 2.** The pivot is now $3$, in row 2. The multiplier for row 3 is $\ell_{32} = 6/3 = 2$: row 3 minus 2 times row 2 is $(0,\ 6 - 6,\ -3 + 2 \mid 3 - 6) = (0, 0, -1 \mid -3)$.
3. **Back substitution.** The system is now triangular:

$$
\left(\begin{array}{rrr|r} 2 & 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & -1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & -3 \end{array}\right) :
\qquad
\begin{aligned}
-x_3 &= -3 &&\Rightarrow\ x_3 = 3 , \\
3x_2 - 3 &= 3 &&\Rightarrow\ x_2 = 2 , \\
2x_1 + 2 - 3 &= 1 &&\Rightarrow\ x_1 = 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The solution is $x = (1, 2, 3)$; substituting it into the three original equations gives $2 + 2 - 3 = 1$, $4 + 10 - 9 = 5$ and $-2 + 10 - 6 = 2$. The pivots are $2$, $3$ and $-1$, all nonzero, which is what made each step possible.

## Elimination is a factorization: A = LU \{#lu-factorization\}

The multipliers $2, -1, 2$ were used once and seemingly thrown away. They are worth keeping. The idea: each elimination step is multiplication by a simple matrix, the steps can be undone in reverse order, and undoing them puts the multipliers, unchanged, into a lower triangular matrix.

$$
\boxed{\; A = LU, \qquad L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & & \\ \ell_{21} & 1 & \\ \ell_{31} & \ell_{32} & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \text{the triangular matrix at the end of elimination} \;}
$$

In general, $L$ has the multiplier $\ell_{ik}$ in row $i$ and column $k$ below a diagonal of ones.

Why: write $\ell_k = (0, \dots, 0, \ell_{k+1,k}, \dots, \ell_{nk})$ for the multipliers of step $k$, with zeros in positions $1$ to $k$. Subtracting $\ell_{ik}$ times row $k$ from every row $i \gt k$ is multiplication on the left by $E_k = I - \ell_k e_k^\top$, since $e_k^\top M$ is row $k$ of $M$. For three unknowns,

$$
\begin{aligned}
U &= E_2 E_1 A \\
A &= E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1}\, U \\
&= \big(I + \ell_1 e_1^\top\big)\big(I + \ell_2 e_2^\top\big)\, U \\
&= \big(I + \ell_1 e_1^\top + \ell_2 e_2^\top + \ell_1 (e_1^\top \ell_2)\, e_2^\top\big)\, U \\
&= \big(I + \ell_1 e_1^\top + \ell_2 e_2^\top\big)\, U = LU .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is elimination. The second undoes the two steps in reverse order. The third uses $E_k^{-1} = I + \ell_k e_k^\top$, which holds because $(I - \ell_k e_k^\top)(I + \ell_k e_k^\top) = I - \ell_k (e_k^\top \ell_k)\, e_k^\top = I$, and $e_k^\top \ell_k = 0$ since $\ell_k$ has a zero in position $k$. The fourth multiplies out. The fifth drops the cross term, because $e_1^\top \ell_2$ is the first entry of $\ell_2$, which is zero. The matrix $I + \ell_1 e_1^\top + \ell_2 e_2^\top$ has $\ell_1$ in column 1 and $\ell_2$ in column 2 below the diagonal: it is $L$. The same argument works for any $n$.

In the example, $\ell_1 = (0, 2, -1)$ and $\ell_2 = (0, 0, 2)$, so

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 4 & 5 & -3 \\ -2 & 5 & -2 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Row 3 of the product is $-1 \cdot (2, 1, -1) + 2 \cdot (0, 3, -1) + 1 \cdot (0, 0, -1) = (-2, 5, -2)$, the third row of $A$.

The factorization separates the matrix from the right-hand side. Solving $Ax = b$ becomes two triangular solves: first $Lc = b$ from the top down (**forward substitution**), then $Ux = c$ from the bottom up. Here $c_1 = 1$, $2c_1 + c_2 = 5$ gives $c_2 = 3$, and $-c_1 + 2c_2 + c_3 = 2$ gives $c_3 = -3$: exactly the right-hand column that elimination produced. Once $A$ is factored, every new $b$ costs only the two triangular solves.

## Row exchanges and pivoting

Elimination needs a nonzero pivot. The following system has the solution $x = (1, 1)$, but its first pivot is zero:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Exchanging the two equations fixes it. In general, rows are exchanged whenever needed, and the factorization becomes $PA = LU$, where the **permutation matrix** $P$ is the identity with its rows reordered. Every invertible matrix has such a factorization.

A small pivot is nearly as bad as a zero one, for a numerical reason. Replace the $0$ above by $10^{-17}$. Elimination without an exchange has the multiplier $10^{17}$; in double precision, which keeps about 16 significant digits ([floating point](/notes/acos/floating-point)), the entry $1 - 10^{17}$ rounds to $-10^{17}$ and the right-hand side $2 - 10^{17}$ to $-10^{17}$. Back substitution then gives $x_2 = 1$ and $x_1 = (1 - 1)/10^{-17} = 0$, while the true solution is $(1, 1)$ to sixteen digits. The information in the second equation has been rounded away. **Partial pivoting** prevents this: at each step it exchanges rows so that the pivot is the entry of largest absolute value in its column, which keeps every multiplier at most 1 in absolute value. Library routines (`scipy.linalg.lu`, `numpy.linalg.solve`, which calls LAPACK) always pivot. On the worked example the first pivot becomes $4$ instead of $2$; the factors change and the solution does not. When partial pivoting can still fail, and why it almost never does, is part of the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) ([Trefethen and Bau, 1997](#references), lectures 21–22).

## What elimination costs

The idea: step $k$ updates every entry of the rows below the pivot that lies to the right of the pivot column, and nothing else.

$$
\boxed{\; \text{multiply–adds of LU on an } n \times n \text{ matrix} = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} (n - k)^2 = \frac{(n - 1)\, n\, (2n - 1)}{6} \approx \frac{n^3}{3} \;}
$$

Why: at step $k$ there are $n - k$ rows below the pivot, and in each of them the $n - k$ entries to the right of column $k$ change by one multiply–add each (the entry in column $k$ becomes zero by construction and is not computed). So

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} (n - k)(n - k) &= \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} j^2 \\
&= \frac{(n - 1)\, n\, (2n - 1)}{6} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first step substitutes $j = n - k$, and the second is the formula for the sum of squares. The $n - k$ divisions for the multipliers add only a lower-order term. For the $3 \times 3$ example the count is $2^2 + 1^2 = 5$: four updated entries in step 1 and one in step 2, and $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 / 6 = 5$. For $n = 1000$ it is $332{,}833{,}500$, about $n^3/3 = 3.3 \cdot 10^8$. Each triangular solve costs about $n^2/2$ multiply–adds, so with the factors at hand a new right-hand side costs $n^2$: for $n = 1000$, a million operations instead of three hundred million.

## Rank and the four fundamental subspaces \{#four-subspaces\}

Elimination works on any rectangular matrix. It produces an **echelon form** $R$: each nonzero row starts with a pivot further right than the row above, and zero rows come last. The **rank** $r$ is the number of pivots. Four subspaces belong to every $A \in \mathbb R^{m \times n}$:

- the **column space** $\operatorname{col}(A) \subseteq \mathbb R^m$, spanned by the columns: the image of $x \mapsto Ax$;
- the **row space** $\operatorname{row}(A) = \operatorname{col}(A^\top) \subseteq \mathbb R^n$, spanned by the rows;
- the **kernel** (null space) $\ker A = \{x : Ax = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb R^n$;
- the **left null space** $\ker A^\top = \{y : A^\top y = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb R^m$, the combinations of rows that vanish, $y^\top A = 0$.

Their dimensions are all fixed by $r$:

$$
\boxed{\; \dim \operatorname{row}(A) = \dim \operatorname{col}(A) = r, \qquad \dim \ker A = n - r, \qquad \dim \ker A^\top = m - r \;}
$$

The first equality, that the number of independent rows equals the number of independent columns, is not obvious: a $3 \times 100$ matrix has at most 3 independent rows, so it has at most 3 independent columns among its hundred. The idea of the proof: elimination changes neither the row space nor the kernel, and in the echelon form both counts are visibly $r$. Write the elimination as $R = EA$, where $E$ is invertible because every step can be undone. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dim \operatorname{row}(A) &= \dim \operatorname{row}(R) = r , \\
\dim \operatorname{col}(A) &= \#\{\text{pivot columns of } R\} = r , \\
\dim \ker A &= n - \operatorname{rank} A = n - r , \\
\dim \ker A^\top &= m - \operatorname{rank} A^\top = m - r .
\end{aligned}
$$

First line: every row of $R$ is a combination of rows of $A$ and, since $E$ is invertible, every row of $A$ is a combination of rows of $R$, so the two row spaces coincide; the nonzero rows of $R$ are independent, because each has a pivot in a column where all rows below it are zero, so they form a basis. Second line: $Ax = 0$ exactly when $Rx = EAx = 0$, so a combination of columns $\sum_j x_j a_j$ vanishes for $A$ exactly when the same combination vanishes for $R$; in $R$ the pivot columns are independent and every other column is a combination of them, so the same holds for the columns of $A$ in the same positions. Third line: rank–nullity ([the previous note](/notes/math/vectors-and-linear-maps#rank-nullity)). Fourth line: rank–nullity for $A^\top$, whose rank is also $r$ by the first two lines.

**Worked example.** Row 2 minus 2 times row 1, row 3 minus 3 times row 1, and then row 3 minus row 2 bring a $3 \times 4$ matrix to echelon form:

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}
\ \longrightarrow\
R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

There are two pivots, in columns 1 and 3, so $r = 2$:

- $\operatorname{col}(A)$ has the basis $(1, 2, 3)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, the pivot columns of $A$. The others are combinations of them: column 2 is twice column 1, and column 4 is column 1 plus twice column 3.
- $\operatorname{row}(A)$ has the basis $(1, 2, 0, 1)$, $(0, 0, 1, 2)$, the nonzero rows of $R$.
- $\ker A$ has dimension $4 - 2 = 2$. The non-pivot unknowns $x_2 = s$ and $x_4 = t$ are free, and $Rx = 0$ gives $x_3 = -2t$ and $x_1 = -2s - t$, so $\ker A$ is spanned by $(-2, 1, 0, 0)$ and $(-1, 0, -2, 1)$.
- $\ker A^\top$ has dimension $3 - 2 = 1$. The elimination showed that row 3 minus row 1 minus row 2 is zero, so it is spanned by $y = (1, 1, -1)$: indeed $y^\top A = (1 + 2 - 3,\ 2 + 4 - 6,\ 0 + 1 - 1,\ 1 + 4 - 5) = 0$.

The subspaces come in perpendicular pairs. Every row of $A$ has zero dot product with every kernel vector, since $Ax = 0$ says exactly that, and every column has zero dot product with every vector of the left null space. Here $(1, 2, 0, 1) \cdot (-1, 0, -2, 1) = -1 + 1 = 0$, and $(1, 2, 3) \cdot (1, 1, -1) = 0$. The [next note](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality) makes "perpendicular" precise and uses these pairs for projections.

## When a solution exists, and when it is unique \{#existence-and-uniqueness\}

The idea: a solution exists when $b$ is reachable, and the left null space gives a test for reachability; the kernel describes all the solutions once one is known.

$$
\boxed{\;
\begin{gathered}
Ax = b \text{ has a solution} \iff b \in \operatorname{col}(A) \iff y^\top b = 0 \text{ for every } y \in \ker A^\top , \\
\text{and then the set of solutions is } \{x_p + z : z \in \ker A\} \text{ for any one solution } x_p
\end{gathered}
\;}
$$

Why: the first equivalence is the column picture, since $Ax$ is a combination of the columns. For the second, if $b = Ax$ then for every $y \in \ker A^\top$

$$
\begin{aligned}
y^\top b &= y^\top A x \\
&= (A^\top y)^\top x \\
&= 0^\top x = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The steps use $b = Ax$, then $(A^\top y)^\top = y^\top A$, then $A^\top y = 0$. Conversely, let $y_1, \dots, y_{m-r}$ be a basis of $\ker A^\top$ and $Y$ the matrix with these rows. The vectors $b$ with $Yb = 0$ form $\ker Y$, of dimension $m - (m - r) = r$ by rank–nullity. It contains $\operatorname{col}(A)$, by the chain above, and $\operatorname{col}(A)$ also has dimension $r$, so the two coincide. For the set of solutions: if $Ax_p = b$ and $Ax = b$, then $A(x - x_p) = 0$, so $x = x_p + z$ with $z \in \ker A$; and every such $x$ solves the system.

**Worked example.** With the $3 \times 4$ matrix above, $Ax = b$ is solvable exactly when $b_1 + b_2 - b_3 = 0$. For $b = (1, 3, 4)$: $1 + 3 - 4 = 0$, and elimination gives $R x = (1, 1, 0)$, so with $x_2 = x_4 = 0$ the particular solution is $x_p = (1, 0, 1, 0)$; all solutions are $x_p + s(-2, 1, 0, 0) + t(-1, 0, -2, 1)$. For $b = (1, 3, 5)$: $1 + 3 - 5 = -1 \ne 0$, and the last row of the eliminated system reads $0 = 1$. No solution exists.

The rank decides which cases are possible:

| shape and rank | solutions of $Ax = b$ |
| --- | --- |
| $r = m = n$ (square, invertible) | exactly one, for every $b$ |
| $r = n \lt m$ (tall, independent columns) | none or exactly one |
| $r = m \lt n$ (wide, independent rows) | infinitely many, for every $b$ |
| $r \lt m$ and $r \lt n$ | none or infinitely many |

A solution exists for every $b$ exactly when $r = m$ (then $\ker A^\top = \{0\}$ and the test is empty), and it is unique whenever it exists exactly when $r = n$ (then $\ker A = \{0\}$).

## The inverse, and why it is rarely formed

A square matrix with $r = n$ is **invertible**: there is a matrix $A^{-1}$ with $A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I$, and then $x = A^{-1} b$. Column $j$ of $A^{-1}$ solves $Ax = e_j$, so the inverse can be computed by solving $n$ systems with the same LU factors. Two rules follow from the definition: $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1}$, because $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I$, and $(A^\top)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^\top$, by transposing $AA^{-1} = I$.

For $2 \times 2$ matrices there is a formula. The idea: swapping the diagonal entries and negating the others gives a matrix whose product with $A$ is a multiple of $I$.

$$
\boxed{\; \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}, \qquad ad - bc \ne 0 \;}
$$

Why:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}
&= \begin{pmatrix} ad - bc & -ab + ba \\ cd - dc & -cb + da \end{pmatrix} \\
&= (ad - bc)\, I .
\end{aligned}
$$

The product is computed by the row-times-column rule; dividing by $ad - bc$ gives $I$. The number $ad - bc$ is the determinant, the subject of the [next note but one](/notes/math/determinants-and-eigenvalues). For the system of the baseline, $ad - bc = 2 \cdot 1 - (-1) \cdot 1 = 3$ and

$$
A^{-1} = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^{-1} b = \frac13 \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 5 \\ -1 + 10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The formula $x = A^{-1}b$ is the right way to write a solution in a derivation and the wrong way to compute one, for three reasons ([Higham, 2002](#references), chapter 14):

- **It costs more and saves nothing.** Forming $A^{-1}$ takes about $n^3$ multiply–adds, three times the $n^3/3$ of the LU factorization, and afterwards $A^{-1}b$ costs $n^2$, the same as the two triangular solves.
- **It is less accurate.** The Code section builds a $100 \times 100$ matrix whose columns are nearly dependent (its condition number, defined in the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability), is $10^{12}$) and solves $Ax = b$ both ways. With `numpy.linalg.solve` the relative residual $\lVert b - A\hat x\rVert / (\lVert A\rVert\, \lVert \hat x\rVert)$ is about $10^{-16}$, at the level of rounding; through the computed inverse it is $7 \cdot 10^{-7}$, and the error in $\hat x$ is also larger, $9 \cdot 10^{-5}$ against $8 \cdot 10^{-6}$. A solver with a small residual has solved a nearby system exactly; the inverse gives no such guarantee.
- **It destroys structure.** The $5 \times 5$ matrix with $2$ on the diagonal and $-1$ next to it has 13 nonzero entries, and its LU factors keep that band: $L$ and $U$ have nonzeros only on two diagonals each. Its inverse has all 25 entries nonzero; its first row is $\tfrac16 (5, 4, 3, 2, 1)$. For a million unknowns, the band has three million entries and the inverse $10^{12}$.

## When there is no exact solution: least squares

Data rarely satisfy a tall system exactly. With $a_1 = (1, 0, 1)$, $a_2 = (0, 1, 1)$ and $b = (1, 2, 6)$, the left null space of $A = [a_1\ a_2]$ is spanned by $y = (1, 1, -1)$, and $y^\top b = 1 + 2 - 6 = -3 \ne 0$: no $x$ gives $Ax = b$. The useful question becomes which $x$ makes $Ax$ closest to $b$. Minimizing $\lVert Ax - b\rVert^2$ leads to the **normal equations** $A^\top A\, \hat x = A^\top b$, a square system that elimination solves; here they give $\hat x = (2, 3)$. Their derivation by calculus, their statistics and their failure modes are in the [least-squares note](/notes/ml/least-squares#the-normal-equations) of the Machine Learning course; their geometry, with this same example, is in the [next note](/notes/math/inner-products-and-orthogonality#least-squares-as-a-projection). Forming $A^\top A$ is itself a numerical risk, and the next note solves the problem through the QR factorization instead.

## Where machine learning solves linear systems

- **Fitting linear models.** Least squares and ridge regression solve $(X^\top X + \lambda I)\, w = X^\top y$, a symmetric system usually handled by its own variant of LU, the [Cholesky factorization](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-cholesky-factorization) of symmetric positive definite matrices ([ridge](/notes/ml/regularization#ridge-a-penalty-on-the-size-of-the-weights)).
- **Gaussian densities and Gaussian processes.** The density of a [multivariate Gaussian](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#the-density) contains $(x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)$, and a Gaussian process prediction contains $K^{-1} y$. Both are computed as solutions of linear systems with a factored matrix, never with an inverse ([the GP posterior](/notes/bayesian-methods/gaussian-processes#regression-the-posterior-in-closed-form)).
- **Newton's method.** Each step solves $H d = -g$ with the Hessian $H$ and the gradient $g$ ([unconstrained optimization](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization)).
- **Invertible layers.** Glow learns its invertible $1 \times 1$ convolutions directly in factored form, $W = PLU$, so that both the inverse and the determinant cost little ([invertible convolutions](/notes/generative-models/flow-architectures#c-invertible-convolutions-glow)).

## What changed

| | Substitution | Elimination and the four subspaces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Procedure | ad hoc, case by case | one rule: clear each column below its pivot |
| Cost | unknown | $n^3/3$ multiply–adds once, then $n^2$ per right-hand side |
| What is kept | the answer | the factors $A = LU$ (or $PA = LU$) |
| Does a solution exist? | found out at the end | $b \in \operatorname{col}(A)$, tested by $\ker A^\top$ |
| How many? | found out at the end | one, or $x_p + \ker A$, from the rank |
| The inverse | a formula | for derivations only; solve with the factors |

## Limitations

- **Elimination needs pivoting.** Without row exchanges it stops at a zero pivot and loses accuracy at a small one. Partial pivoting is the default, and its rare failures are discussed in the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability).
- **Rank is fragile in floating point.** With measured data, a dependent set of columns becomes independent after the smallest perturbation, so tests such as "is $b$ in the column space?" need a tolerance. The [SVD note](/notes/math/singular-value-decomposition) gives the robust version, the numerical rank.
- **A small residual is not a small error.** For an ill-conditioned matrix, even the best solver returns an $\hat x$ that solves a nearby system exactly but may be far from the true $x$; the size of that gap is the condition number.
- **Dense elimination does not scale.** At $n^3/3$ multiply–adds, a dense system with $10^5$ unknowns needs about $3 \cdot 10^{14}$ of them. Large sparse systems are solved by sparse factorizations or iterative methods, which this course does not cover.
- **Exact systems are rare in data.** Tall systems built from measurements are almost never consistent; the question becomes least squares.

## What you should remember

- $Ax = b$ reads three ways: the intersection of hyperplanes (rows), a combination of columns equal to $b$, and a point in the image of a map.
- Gaussian elimination subtracts multiples of the pivot row to make the matrix upper triangular, then solves from the bottom up.
- The multipliers form $L$, and elimination is the factorization $A = LU$, or $PA = LU$ with row exchanges; partial pivoting keeps the multipliers at most 1 in absolute value.
- LU costs about $n^3/3$ multiply–adds; each further right-hand side costs $n^2$.
- Rank $r$ is the number of pivots; the row space and the column space both have dimension $r$, the kernel $n - r$, the left null space $m - r$.
- A solution exists exactly when $b \in \operatorname{col}(A)$, equivalently $y^\top b = 0$ for all $y \in \ker A^\top$; all solutions are $x_p + \ker A$.
- Write $A^{-1}b$ in derivations; compute with a factorization. The inverse costs three times as much, is less accurate and is dense.

## Code

The examples of the note: elimination by hand with the multipliers kept, the library LU with pivoting, the cost count, the four subspaces of the $3 \times 4$ example, the solvability test, a small pivot, and solving with and without the inverse. Seeds are fixed; exact numbers can differ slightly across platforms and library versions, especially the last two lines of the inverse experiment. The script runs in about a second.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import lu, lu_factor, lu_solve, null_space, solve_triangular

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
rank = np.linalg.matrix_rank

# the 2 x 2 system: row picture and column picture give the same x
M = np.array([[2., -1], [1, 1]]); bm = np.array([1., 5])
xm = np.linalg.solve(M, bm)
print(xm, xm[0] * M[:, 0] + xm[1] * M[:, 1])     # [2. 3.] [1. 5.]

# Gaussian elimination without row exchanges, keeping the multipliers
def lu_nopivot(A):
    U = A.astype(float).copy(); n = len(U); L = np.eye(n)
    for k in range(n - 1):
        for i in range(k + 1, n):
            L[i, k] = U[i, k] / U[k, k]          # the multiplier
            U[i, k:] -= L[i, k] * U[k, k:]       # row i minus multiplier times the pivot row
    return L, U

A = np.array([[2., 1, -1], [4, 5, -3], [-2, 5, -2]]); b = np.array([1., 5, 2])
L, U = lu_nopivot(A)
print(L.tolist(), U.tolist(), np.allclose(L @ U, A))
# [[1.0, 0.0, 0.0], [2.0, 1.0, 0.0], [-1.0, 2.0, 1.0]] [[2.0, 1.0, -1.0], [0.0, 3.0, -1.0], [0.0, 0.0, -1.0]] True
c = solve_triangular(L, b, lower=True)           # forward substitution: Lc = b
x = solve_triangular(U, c)                       # back substitution: Ux = c
print(c, x)                                      # [ 1.  3. -3.] [1. 2. 3.]

# library LU uses partial pivoting: PA = LU with the largest available pivot; same solution
P, Lp, Up = lu(A)                                # scipy returns A = P Lp Up
print(np.allclose(P @ Lp @ Up, A), Up[0, 0], np.abs(Lp).max(), lu_solve(lu_factor(A), b))
# True 4.0 1.0 [1. 2. 3.]

# a tiny pivot: without row exchanges the answer is wrong, with partial pivoting it is right
As = np.array([[1e-17, 1], [1, 1]]); bs = np.array([1., 2])
Ls, Us = lu_nopivot(As)
print(solve_triangular(Us, solve_triangular(Ls, bs, lower=True)), np.linalg.solve(As, bs))   # [0. 1.] [1. 1.]

# multiply-adds of elimination on an n x n matrix: sum of (n - k)^2, about n^3 / 3
for n in (3, 10, 100, 1000):
    print(n, sum((n - k) ** 2 for k in range(1, n)), round(n ** 3 / 3))
# 3 5 9 | 10 285 333 | 100 328350 333333 | 1000 332833500 333333333

# the four subspaces of a 3 x 4 matrix of rank 2
A4 = np.array([[1., 2, 0, 1], [2, 4, 1, 4], [3, 6, 1, 5]])
print(rank(A4), null_space(A4).shape[1], null_space(A4.T).shape[1])   # 2 2 1: dimensions r, n - r, m - r
for v in ([-2, 1, 0, 0], [-1, 0, -2, 1]):
    print(A4 @ v)                                # [0. 0. 0.] twice: a basis of the kernel
print(np.array([1, 1, -1]) @ A4)                 # [0. 0. 0. 0.]: the left null space
# solvable exactly when b is in the column space, i.e. b1 + b2 - b3 = 0
for bb in ([1., 3, 4], [1., 3, 5]):
    print(bb, rank(np.column_stack([A4, bb])), np.array([1, 1, -1]) @ bb)
# [1.0, 3, 4] 2 0.0 | [1.0, 3, 5] 3 -1.0
xp = np.array([1., 0, 1, 0])
print(A4 @ xp, A4 @ (xp + 2 * np.array([-2, 1, 0, 0]) - np.array([-1, 0, -2, 1])))   # [1. 3. 4.] [1. 3. 4.]

# the inverse of a 2 x 2 matrix, and the inverse of a sparse matrix is dense
print(np.linalg.inv(M) * 3, np.linalg.inv(M) @ bm)    # [[ 1.  1.] [-1.  2.]] [2. 3.]
T = 2 * np.eye(5) - np.eye(5, k=1) - np.eye(5, k=-1)
Lt, Ut = lu_nopivot(T)
print(np.count_nonzero(T), np.count_nonzero(np.abs(Lt) + np.abs(Ut) > 1e-12),
      np.count_nonzero(np.abs(np.linalg.inv(T)) > 1e-12), (6 * np.linalg.inv(T)).round(6)[0])
# 13 13 25 [5. 4. 3. 2. 1.]: the factors keep the band (L and U together), the inverse fills in

# solving through the inverse: a much larger residual (n = 100, condition number 1e12)
n = 100
Q1, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(n, n))); Q2, _ = np.linalg.qr(rng.normal(size=(n, n)))
Ak = Q1 @ np.diag(np.logspace(0, -12, n)) @ Q2.T
xt = rng.normal(size=n); bk = Ak @ xt
for name, xh in (('solve', np.linalg.solve(Ak, bk)), ('inv', np.linalg.inv(Ak) @ bk)):
    print(name, f'{np.linalg.norm(bk - Ak @ xh) / (np.linalg.norm(Ak, 2) * np.linalg.norm(xh)):.0e}',
          f'{np.linalg.norm(xh - xt) / np.linalg.norm(xt):.0e}')
# solve 1e-16 8e-06 | inv 7e-07 9e-05   (relative residual, relative error)

# no exact solution: b = (1, 2, 6) is not a combination of (1, 0, 1) and (0, 1, 1)
A2 = np.column_stack([[1., 0, 1], [0, 1, 1]]); b2 = np.array([1., 2, 6])
print(rank(A2), rank(np.column_stack([A2, b2])), np.array([1, 1, -1]) @ b2, np.linalg.lstsq(A2, b2, rcond=None)[0])
# 2 3 -3.0 [2. 3.]
```
