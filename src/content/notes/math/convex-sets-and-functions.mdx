The calculus section ended with a complete toolkit for differentiating: gradients and Hessians of functions of vectors, the chain rule for matrices, and in the [previous note](/notes/math/multiple-integrals) the change of variables for integrals. Training a model uses that toolkit to find a minimum of a loss. The gradient finds points where the slope vanishes, and the Hessian says whether such a point is a local minimum. Neither says whether it is the lowest point overall: both describe the function only near the point. This note introduces the property that turns local information into global information, **convexity**. It opens the Optimization section, whose next three notes build algorithms and constrained problems on it.

**Notation.** Vectors are lowercase letters, $x, y \in \mathbb R^n$, and matrices capital letters. $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$ is a function with gradient $\nabla f(x)$ and Hessian $\nabla^2 f(x)$, as in the [gradients note](/notes/math/gradients-jacobians-hessians). A number $\theta \in [0, 1]$ weights two points; $\theta x + (1 - \theta)y$ runs over the segment from $y$ to $x$. For a symmetric matrix, $A \succeq 0$ means positive semidefinite ($v^\top A v \ge 0$ for all $v$) and $A \succeq B$ means $A - B \succeq 0$; $\lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_{\max}$ are its extreme eigenvalues, as in the [symmetric matrices note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices). $\lVert \cdot \rVert$ is the Euclidean norm.

## Baseline: a zero gradient is only a candidate

Take the function of one variable

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= x^4 - 2x^2 + \tfrac12 x , \\
f'(x) &= 4x^3 - 4x + \tfrac12 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Its derivative vanishes at three points, $x = -1.057$, $0.127$ and $0.930$, where $f$ takes the values $-1.515$, $0.032$ and $-0.517$ and the second derivative $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 4$ is $9.42$, $-3.81$ and $6.39$. Two of them are local minima. Gradient descent with step $0.05$ started at $x = 1.5$ stops at $0.930$; started at $-1.5$, it stops at $-1.057$. At both points the derivative is zero and the second derivative is positive, so a test that looks only at the point cannot tell the global minimum from the other one. Neither can the algorithm: it reports convergence in both runs.

For the loss of a neural network, with millions of variables, there is no list of critical points to compare. The way out is to restrict the class of functions so that the local test becomes a global one. That class is the convex functions, and the restriction costs less than it seems: least squares, logistic regression, the support vector machine and the lasso all have convex losses.

## Convex sets

A set $C \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ is **convex** if it contains the segment between any two of its points:

$$
x, y \in C,\ \theta \in [0, 1] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta x + (1 - \theta)y \in C .
$$

<ConvexSets />

Examples that appear throughout machine learning:

- **Hyperplanes and half-spaces**, $\{x : a^\top x = b\}$ and $\{x : a^\top x \le b\}$. If $a^\top x \le b$ and $a^\top y \le b$, then $a^\top(\theta x + (1 - \theta)y) = \theta\,a^\top x + (1 - \theta)\,a^\top y \le \theta b + (1 - \theta) b = b$. A linear classifier splits the space into two half-spaces.
- **Norm balls**, $\{x : \lVert x - c\rVert \le r\}$. By the triangle inequality, $\lVert \theta(x - c) + (1 - \theta)(y - c)\rVert \le \theta r + (1 - \theta) r = r$. The same argument works for every norm, so the L1 ball of the lasso is convex too.
- **The probability simplex**, $\Delta = \{p : p_i \ge 0,\ \sum_i p_i = 1\}$, the set of distributions on $n$ outcomes and the range of the softmax.
- **The positive semidefinite matrices**: if $v^\top A v \ge 0$ and $v^\top B v \ge 0$ for every $v$, then so is $\theta\,v^\top A v + (1 - \theta)\,v^\top B v$. Covariance matrices and kernel matrices live in this set.

Non-examples: a ring $\{1 \le \lVert x\rVert \le 2\}$, whose chords can pass through the hole, and the union of two disjoint disks.

**Intersections preserve convexity.** If $x$ and $y$ lie in every $C_i$ and every $C_i$ is convex, the segment between them lies in every $C_i$, hence in the intersection. A finite intersection of half-spaces, $\{x : A x \le b\}$, is called a **polyhedron**; the feasible sets of linear programs and of the SVM are polyhedra. Unions do not preserve convexity, as the two disks show.

**The convex hull.** A **convex combination** of points $x_1, \dots, x_k$ is $\sum_i \theta_i x_i$ with $\theta_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_i \theta_i = 1$, a weighted average. The **convex hull** $\operatorname{conv}\{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$ is the set of all convex combinations. It is convex, it contains the points, and it is the smallest such set: a convex set that contains the points contains every convex combination of them, by induction on $k$ from the definition. For finitely many points in the plane it is the polygon a rubber band would form around them.

**Worked example.** Is $(1, 1)$ in the convex hull of $(0, 0)$, $(3, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$? Solve $\theta_1 (0, 0) + \theta_2 (3, 0) + \theta_3 (0, 3) = (1, 1)$ with $\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3 = 1$: $\theta_2 = \theta_3 = \tfrac13$ and $\theta_1 = \tfrac13$, all non-negative, so yes. For $(2, 2)$ the same equations give $\theta_2 = \theta_3 = \tfrac23$ and $\theta_1 = -\tfrac13$. The weights are unique here, because three points not on a line form a triangle, and one of them is negative, so $(2, 2)$ is outside. The probability simplex is the convex hull of the standard basis vectors, and the [duality note](/notes/math/duality) shows that the hard-margin SVM looks for the two nearest points of the convex hulls of its two classes.

## Convex functions

A function $f$ defined on a convex set is **convex** if every chord lies on or above its graph:

$$
f\big(\theta x + (1 - \theta)y\big) \le \theta f(x) + (1 - \theta) f(y) \qquad \text{for all } x, y,\ \theta \in [0, 1] .
$$

It is **strictly convex** if the inequality is strict whenever $x \ne y$ and $0 \lt \theta \lt 1$, and **concave** if $-f$ is convex. Equivalently, the region above the graph, the **epigraph** $\{(x, t) : t \ge f(x)\}$, is a convex set: the two definitions translate into each other through the points $(x, f(x))$ and $(y, f(y))$. By induction the inequality extends from two points to any convex combination, $f(\sum_i \theta_i x_i) \le \sum_i \theta_i f(x_i)$, which is **Jensen's inequality**; with the weights read as probabilities it says $f(E[X]) \le E[f(X)]$, the form that bounds the log-likelihood in the [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from), where the logarithm is concave and the inequality flips.

**Worked example.** For $f(x) = x^2$, $x = 0$, $y = 2$ and $\theta = \tfrac12$, the function at the midpoint is $f(1) = 1$ and the chord at the midpoint is $\tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 \cdot 4 = 2$. In general the gap is $\theta f(x) + (1 - \theta) f(y) - f(\theta x + (1 - \theta) y) = \theta(1 - \theta)(x - y)^2$, here $\tfrac14 \cdot 4 = 1$: non-negative for every pair, so $x^2$ is convex, and positive whenever $x \ne y$, so it is strictly convex. Every norm is convex by the triangle inequality, as for the balls above.

Checking chords pair by pair is impractical in many dimensions. For differentiable functions two tests use the derivatives instead.

## The first-order condition: the tangent lies below

The idea: for a convex function the chord from $x$ towards $y$ lies above the graph, and as the chord shrinks to a point it turns into the tangent. So the tangent at any point lies below the whole graph. For a differentiable $f$ on an open convex set,

$$
\boxed{\; f \text{ is convex} \iff f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) \quad \text{for all } x, y . \;}
$$

<TangentBelow />

Why convexity gives the tangent bound. Fix $x$ and $y$ and take $0 \lt \theta \le 1$; the point $x + \theta(y - x) = \theta y + (1 - \theta)x$ lies on the segment:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f\big(x + \theta(y - x)\big) &\le \theta f(y) + (1 - \theta) f(x) && \text{convexity with weights } \theta,\ 1 - \theta \\
f(y) &\ge f(x) + \frac{f\big(x + \theta(y - x)\big) - f(x)}{\theta} && \text{subtract } f(x) \text{, divide by } \theta \gt 0 \\
&\to f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) \quad (\theta \to 0) && \text{the quotient is a directional derivative.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line is the chain rule for $t \mapsto f(x + t(y - x))$ at $t = 0$, whose derivative is $\nabla f(x)^\top(y - x)$. The inequality holds for every $\theta$, so it survives the limit. The converse is as short: put $z = \theta x + (1 - \theta)y$, write the tangent bound at $z$ once towards $x$ and once towards $y$, multiply the two inequalities by $\theta$ and $1 - \theta$ and add them. The gradient terms cancel, because $\theta(x - z) + (1 - \theta)(y - z) = 0$, and what remains is $\theta f(x) + (1 - \theta) f(y) \ge f(z)$.

**Worked example.** $f(x) = e^x$ at $x = 0$ has tangent $1 + y$, so the bound says $e^y \ge 1 + y$ for every $y$: at $y = 1$, $2.718 \ge 2$, at $y = -2$, $0.135 \ge -1$. The baseline function fails the test. At its local minimum $x = 0.930$ the tangent is the horizontal line at height $-0.517$, and at $x = -1.057$ the graph is $0.998$ below it.

The bound is the reason convexity matters. Put $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ into it: $f(y) \ge f(x^*)$ for every $y$. **For a differentiable convex function, a point with zero gradient is a global minimum.** The local test has become a global one.

## The second-order condition: a positive semidefinite Hessian

The idea: a function is convex exactly when it is convex along every line, and along a line its second derivative is a quadratic form of the Hessian. For a twice differentiable $f$ on an open convex set,

$$
\boxed{\; f \text{ is convex} \iff \nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0 \quad \text{for all } x . \;}
$$

Why. Restrict $f$ to the line through $x$ in direction $v$, $g(t) = f(x + t v)$. Chords of $g$ are chords of $f$, so $f$ is convex if and only if every such $g$ is. For $g$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g'(t) &= \nabla f(x + t v)^\top v && \text{chain rule} \\
g''(t) &= v^\top \nabla^2 f(x + t v)\, v && \text{chain rule again, on each component of } \nabla f \\
g \text{ convex} &\iff g'' \ge 0 \text{ everywhere} && \text{one variable: } g' \text{ nondecreasing, the tangent test above.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line is the one-variable fact: $g'' \ge 0$ means that $g'$ never decreases, and then $g(s) - g(t) - g'(t)(s - t) = \int_t^s \big(g'(r) - g'(t)\big)\,dr \ge 0$ on either side of $t$, which is the tangent bound; conversely, a convex $g$ has $g'$ nondecreasing, by the tangent bound written at $s$ and at $t$ and added. Requiring $g'' \ge 0$ for every point and every direction $v$ is exactly $\nabla^2 f \succeq 0$ everywhere. If $\nabla^2 f(x) \succ 0$ (positive definite) everywhere, $f$ is strictly convex; the converse fails, since $x^4$ is strictly convex with $f''(0) = 0$.

**Worked example.** The quadratic $f(x) = \tfrac12 x^\top A x + b^\top x$ with symmetric $A$ has the constant Hessian $A$, so it is convex exactly when $A \succeq 0$. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ the eigenvalues are 1 and 3, both positive: convex. For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ they are 3 and $-1$, and the direction $v = (1, -1)$ with $v^\top A v = -2$ exposes it: with $b = 0$, $f(\pm v) = -1$ while the midpoint has $f(0) = 0$, above the chord.

**Worked example: log-sum-exp.** The function $\operatorname{lse}(z) = \log\sum_i e^{z_i}$, which turns logits into the normalizer of the softmax, has gradient $p = \operatorname{softmax}(z)$ and Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(p) - p p^\top$. For any $v$,

$$
v^\top \big(\operatorname{diag}(p) - p p^\top\big)v = \sum_i p_i v_i^2 - \Big(\sum_i p_i v_i\Big)^2 \ge 0 ,
$$

because the right side is the variance of a random variable that takes the value $v_i$ with probability $p_i$. For $z = (1, 2, 3)$, $p = (0.090, 0.245, 0.665)$, the Hessian has eigenvalues $0$, $0.119$ and $0.371$, and for $v = (1, 0, -1)$ both sides equal $0.424$. The zero eigenvalue belongs to $v = (1, 1, 1)$: adding a constant to every logit changes nothing, so lse is convex but not strictly.

## Operations that preserve convexity

Most losses are not checked from scratch. They are built from convex pieces by operations that keep convexity, each one a line of algebra from the chord definition:

- **Non-negative weighted sums.** If $f_1, \dots, f_k$ are convex and $w_i \ge 0$, then $\sum_i w_i f_i$ is convex: add the chord inequalities with weights $w_i$. An average loss over training examples plus a convex penalty is convex.
- **Composition with an affine map.** If $f$ is convex, so is $x \mapsto f(A x + b)$, because $A(\theta x + (1 - \theta)y) + b = \theta(A x + b) + (1 - \theta)(A y + b)$. A convex loss of a linear model's prediction is convex in the weights.
- **Pointwise maximum.** If $f_1, \dots, f_k$ are convex, so is $\max_i f_i$: each $f_i$ at the mixed point is at most the chord of $f_i$, which is at most the chord of the maximum. The epigraph of the maximum is the intersection of the epigraphs. The hinge $\max(0, 1 - m)$ is the maximum of two affine functions.
- **Composition with a convex nondecreasing function.** If $g$ is convex and $h: \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is convex and nondecreasing, then $h(g(x))$ is convex: $h(g(\theta x + (1 - \theta)y)) \le h(\theta g(x) + (1 - \theta) g(y)) \le \theta h(g(x)) + (1 - \theta) h(g(y))$, the first step because $h$ is nondecreasing, the second because $h$ is convex. So $e^{g(x)}$ is convex, and so is $\lVert A x - b\rVert^2$: the argument needs $h$ nondecreasing only on the values of $g$, and $h(t) = t^2$ is nondecreasing for $t \ge 0$, the values a norm takes.

A product of convex functions, a difference, or a composition in the wrong order need not be convex: $x$ and $-x$ are convex but their product $-x^2$ is not, and $(ab - 1)^2$, the squared error of the product of two weights, is the counterexample of the last section.

## Why every local minimum is global

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> On a convex function, the chord from a point towards any lower point would pass below the graph near the first point. So no point can be a local minimum without being a global one, and for differentiable convex functions a zero gradient certifies the global minimum.</aside>

A point $x^*$ is a **local minimum** if $f(x^*) \le f(x)$ for all $x$ in some ball $\lVert x - x^*\rVert \le r$ with $r > 0$. The claim, for a convex $f$ on a convex set $C$:

$$
\boxed{\; x^* \text{ is a local minimum of a convex } f \text{ on } C \ \Longrightarrow\ f(x^*) \le f(y) \text{ for every } y \in C . \;}
$$

Why. Suppose some $y \in C$ had $f(y) \lt f(x^*)$. Walk from $x^*$ a small fraction $\theta$ of the way towards $y$, to $z = (1 - \theta)x^* + \theta y$, with $\theta$ so small that $\lVert z - x^*\rVert = \theta\lVert y - x^*\rVert \le r$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z) &\le (1 - \theta) f(x^*) + \theta f(y) && \text{convexity; } z \in C \text{ because } C \text{ is convex} \\
&\lt (1 - \theta) f(x^*) + \theta f(x^*) && f(y) \lt f(x^*) \text{ and } \theta \gt 0 \\
&= f(x^*) && \text{collect terms.}
\end{aligned}
$$

So $z$ is inside the ball and strictly lower than $x^*$, which contradicts the local minimality of $x^*$. Hence no such $y$ exists. Two consequences follow from the same chord. The set of minimizers is convex: if $x$ and $y$ both attain the minimum value $p$, the chord gives $f \le p$ on the segment, so the whole segment attains it. And a strictly convex function has at most one minimizer: two distinct ones would make the midpoint strictly lower than both.

**Worked example.** Least squares with two identical feature columns has the convex loss $(w_1 + w_2 - 1)^2$ for a target of 1 and a feature equal to 1: its minimizers are the whole line $w_1 + w_2 = 1$, a convex set, and gradient descent stops somewhere on it, always at the minimum value 0. Adding the penalty $\lambda(w_1^2 + w_2^2)$ makes the loss strictly convex and leaves one minimizer, $w_1 = w_2 = 1/(2 + \lambda)$, which is $(0.476, 0.476)$ for $\lambda = 0.1$. Convexity does not promise that a minimum exists: $e^x$ is convex with no minimizer. Existence needs more, such as the strong convexity of the next section or a closed and bounded feasible set.

## Strong convexity and smoothness

Convexity says the function lies above its tangents. Two quantitative versions say by how much at least and at most, and they set the speed of every algorithm in the [next note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization).

A differentiable $f$ is **$\mu$-strongly convex**, with $\mu \gt 0$, if it lies above its tangents by at least a parabola:

$$
f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) + \frac\mu2 \lVert y - x\rVert^2 .
$$

Equivalently, $f(x) - \frac\mu2\lVert x\rVert^2$ is convex, and for twice differentiable $f$, $\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq \mu I$: every eigenvalue of the Hessian is at least $\mu$, everywhere. The first equivalence is the tangent test applied to $f - \frac\mu2\lVert\cdot\rVert^2$, whose gradient is $\nabla f(x) - \mu x$; the second is the Hessian test applied to it.

A differentiable $f$ is **$L$-smooth** if its gradient is $L$-Lipschitz, $\lVert\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\rVert \le L\lVert x - y\rVert$; for twice differentiable $f$ this is $-LI \preceq \nabla^2 f(x) \preceq LI$. The idea of its main consequence: the gradient cannot change faster than $L$ per unit of distance, so the function cannot rise above its tangent faster than a parabola of curvature $L$:

$$
\boxed{\; f(y) \le f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) + \frac L2 \lVert y - x\rVert^2 . \;}
$$

Why. Write the gap to the tangent as an integral along the segment, $x_t = x + t(y - x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(y) - f(x) - \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) &= \int_0^1 \big(\nabla f(x_t) - \nabla f(x)\big)^\top (y - x)\, dt && \text{fundamental theorem of calculus for } t \mapsto f(x_t) \\
&\le \int_0^1 \lVert\nabla f(x_t) - \nabla f(x)\rVert\, \lVert y - x\rVert\, dt && \text{Cauchy–Schwarz} \\
&\le \int_0^1 L\, t\, \lVert y - x\rVert^2\, dt && \text{Lipschitz, with } \lVert x_t - x\rVert = t\lVert y - x\rVert \\
&= \frac L2 \lVert y - x\rVert^2 && \textstyle\int_0^1 t\, dt = \frac12 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line uses that $\frac{d}{dt} f(x_t) = \nabla f(x_t)^\top(y - x)$, and the constant $\nabla f(x)^\top(y - x)$ integrates to itself. A function that is both strongly convex and smooth is squeezed between two parabolas around every tangent, of curvatures $\mu$ and $L$. Their ratio

$$
\kappa = \frac{L}{\mu} \ge 1
$$

is the **condition number** of $f$. For a quadratic it is $\lambda_{\max}(A)/\lambda_{\min}(A)$, the condition number of the matrix that the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) studies from the numerical side.

**Worked example.** For $f(x) = \tfrac12x^\top A x$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $\mu = 1$, $L = 3$ and $\kappa = 3$. From $x = 0$ to $y = (1, 0)$: the tangent is 0, $f(y) = 1$, and the two parabolas give $0.5 \le 1 \le 1.5$.

**How far from the minimum?** Strong convexity turns a small gradient into a guarantee. The idea: the lower parabola at $x$ cannot dip below $f(x) - \lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2/(2\mu)$, and the minimum value $f^* = f(x^*)$ lies on or above it:

$$
\boxed{\; f(x) - f^* \le \frac{1}{2\mu}\lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2 . \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
f^* = f(x^*) &\ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(x^* - x) + \frac\mu2\lVert x^* - x\rVert^2 && \text{strong convexity at } x \text{, towards } x^* \\
&\ge \min_{y} \Big( f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) + \frac\mu2\lVert y - x\rVert^2 \Big) && \text{a value is at least the minimum} \\
&= f(x) - \frac{1}{2\mu}\lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2 && \text{the minimizer is } y - x = -\nabla f(x)/\mu .
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line sets the gradient of the parabola, $\nabla f(x) + \mu(y - x)$, to zero and substitutes. This is the **Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality**, and the next note turns it into the linear convergence rate of gradient descent. For the quadratic above at $x = (1, 1)$, $f = 3$, $\nabla f = (3, 3)$, and the bound is $18/2 = 9 \ge 3$.

**Where $\mu$ and $L$ come from in machine learning.** The ridge penalty $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ adds $2\lambda I$ to any Hessian, so a convex loss plus ridge is $2\lambda$-strongly convex: the [regularization note](/notes/ml/regularization) uses the penalty for statistics, and it also fixes the geometry. The logistic loss has Hessian $\frac1n X^\top S X$ with $S = \operatorname{diag}(p_i(1 - p_i))$, and $p(1 - p) \le \tfrac14$, so it is $L$-smooth with $L = \lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)/(4n)$. Its smallest Hessian eigenvalue tends to 0 far from the origin, where the predictions saturate, so without a penalty it is strictly convex but not strongly convex.

## Which machine learning losses are convex

The tests above settle the standard losses of linear models, all in the weights $w$ for fixed data:

| Loss | Why it is convex | Strongly convex? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| least squares $\frac1n\lVert X w - y\rVert^2$ | Hessian $\frac2n X^\top X \succeq 0$ ([least squares](/notes/ml/least-squares)) | if $X$ has full column rank |
| logistic $\frac1n\sum_i \log(1 + e^{-y_i w^\top x_i})$ | convex function of an affine map; Hessian $\frac1n X^\top S X$ ([logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs)) | no, only with a penalty |
| softmax cross-entropy | lse of an affine map minus a linear term | no: shifting all logits is flat |
| hinge $\frac1n\sum_i \max(0, 1 - y_i w^\top x_i)$ | maximum of affine functions ([SVM](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines)) | no; not differentiable either |
| ridge $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$, lasso $\lambda\lVert w\rVert_1$ | a squared norm, a norm | ridge yes ($2\lambda$), lasso no |

**What is not convex.** A neural network is a composition of linear maps with nonlinear activations, and the loss as a function of all the weights is not convex. The smallest example is a "network" with one hidden unit and no activation, $\hat y = a\,b\,x$, fitted to one example $x = y = 1$:

$$
f(a, b) = (ab - 1)^2 , \qquad \nabla^2 f(a, b) = \begin{pmatrix} 2b^2 & 4ab - 2 \\ 4ab - 2 & 2a^2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Both $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$ fit exactly, with loss 0, but their midpoint $(0, 0)$ has loss 1, above the chord. The Hessian there has eigenvalues $2$ and $-2$: the origin is a saddle point with zero gradient. The same sign symmetry, and the permutation symmetry of hidden units, holds in every network, as the [deep learning course](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#many-equivalent-minima-so-not-convex) works out. Matrix factorization, $k$-means and mixture models are non-convex for the same reason: a model with interchangeable parts has several equivalent minimizers and worse points between them. For these, the guarantees of the next note shrink to "the gradient becomes small", and the starting point matters.

## What changed

| | A general differentiable function | A convex function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A zero gradient | a candidate: minimum, maximum or saddle | a global minimum |
| Local minima | possibly many, with different values | all global; the minimizers form a convex set |
| Tangent at a point | describes $f$ only nearby | lies below $f$ everywhere |
| Hessian | any symmetric matrix | positive semidefinite everywhere |
| With $\mu$ and $L$ | no guarantee | $f(x) - f^* \le \lVert\nabla f\rVert^2/(2\mu)$; condition number $\kappa = L/\mu$ |
| Typical ML losses | neural networks, factorizations, mixtures | least squares, logistic, softmax, hinge, ridge, lasso |

## Limitations

- **Convexity says nothing about speed.** A convex function can be very flat or very badly conditioned; $\mu$ and $L$ measure that, and the next note shows that the number of gradient steps grows with $\kappa$.
- **A minimum need not exist.** Convex functions such as $e^x$ or the logistic loss on separable data have an infimum that no point attains.
- **Non-differentiable convex functions** such as the hinge and the L1 norm need subgradients instead of gradients; the tangent bound becomes a family of supporting lines.
- **Deep learning is not convex.** The theory here describes linear models and the convex sub-problems inside other methods (the M-step of EM, a single layer with the rest fixed), not the training of a network.

## What you should remember

- A set is convex if it contains every chord; intersections of convex sets are convex; the convex hull is the set of convex combinations.
- A function is convex if every chord lies above its graph; equivalently, every tangent lies below it, or, when it is twice differentiable, its Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere.
- Non-negative sums, affine substitutions, pointwise maxima and convex nondecreasing outer functions preserve convexity.
- For convex functions every local minimum is global, and a zero gradient is a certificate of global optimality.
- $\mu$-strong convexity and $L$-smoothness bound $f$ between parabolas of curvatures $\mu$ and $L$ around every tangent; $\kappa = L/\mu$; $f(x) - f^* \le \lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2/(2\mu)$.
- Least squares, logistic and softmax losses, the hinge and the usual penalties are convex; neural networks, factorizations and mixtures are not.

## Code

The baseline, the chord test on three convex losses and one non-convex one, the tangent and Hessian tests, the parabola sandwich and the Polyak–Łojasiewicz bound, with NumPy. Exact digits can differ slightly across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# the baseline: f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2 + x/2 has two local minima; gradient descent finds the one near its start
f = lambda x: x**4 - 2 * x**2 + 0.5 * x
df = lambda x: 4 * x**3 - 4 * x + 0.5
crit = np.sort(np.roots([4, 0, -4, 0.5]).real)
print(crit.round(3), f(crit).round(3), (12 * crit**2 - 4).round(2))   # stationary points, values, f''
for x in (1.5, -1.5):
    x0 = x
    for _ in range(2000):
        x -= 0.05 * df(x)
    print(x0, round(x, 3), round(f(x), 3))
# [-1.057  0.127  0.93 ] [-1.515  0.032 -0.517] [ 9.42 -3.81  6.39]
# 1.5 0.93 -0.517 | -1.5 -1.057 -1.515

# the chord test f(t u + (1 - t) v) <= t f(u) + (1 - t) f(v) on 10,000 random pairs
X = rng.normal(size=(50, 3)); y = np.sign(rng.normal(size=50))
losses = {
    'least squares': lambda w: np.mean((X @ w - y) ** 2),
    'logistic': lambda w: np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, -y * (X @ w))),
    'hinge': lambda w: np.mean(np.maximum(0, 1 - y * (X @ w))),
    '(ab - 1)^2': lambda w: (w[0] * w[1] - 1) ** 2,
}
for name, L in losses.items():
    bad = 0
    for _ in range(10_000):
        u, v, t = rng.normal(size=3) * 2, rng.normal(size=3) * 2, rng.uniform()
        bad += L(t * u + (1 - t) * v) > t * L(u) + (1 - t) * L(v) + 1e-12
    print(name, bad)
# least squares 0 | logistic 0 | hinge 0 | (ab - 1)^2 1779

# (ab - 1)^2: two minimizers, a worse midpoint, and an indefinite Hessian there
g = losses['(ab - 1)^2']
print(g(np.array([1., 1, 0])), g(np.array([-1., -1, 0])), g(np.zeros(3)), np.linalg.eigvalsh([[0, -2], [-2, 0]]))
# 0.0 0.0 1.0 [-2.  2.]

# the tangent lies below: e^y >= 1 + y; at the local minimum of the baseline the flat tangent does not
print(np.exp(1.0) - 2, f(crit[0]) - f(crit[2]))
# 0.7182818284590451 -0.9980051329904565

# the Hessian of log-sum-exp is diag(p) - p p^T, a covariance matrix
z = np.array([1., 2, 3]); p = np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum()
H = np.diag(p) - np.outer(p, p); v = np.array([1., 0, -1])
print(p.round(4), np.linalg.eigvalsh(H).round(4), round(v @ H @ v, 4), round(p @ v**2 - (p @ v) ** 2, 4))
# [0.09   0.2447 0.6652] [-0.      0.1186  0.3709] 0.4244 0.4244

# mu and L of a quadratic, and the sandwich mu/2 |d|^2 <= f(x + d) - f(x) - grad^T d <= L/2 |d|^2
A = np.array([[2., 1], [1, 2]]); mu, L = np.linalg.eigvalsh(A)
q = lambda x: 0.5 * x @ A @ x
ok = 0
for _ in range(10_000):
    x, d = rng.normal(size=2), rng.normal(size=2)
    gap = q(x + d) - q(x) - (A @ x) @ d
    ok += mu / 2 * d @ d - 1e-12 <= gap <= L / 2 * d @ d + 1e-12
print(mu, L, L / mu, ok)
# 1.0 3.0 3.0 10000

# the Polyak-Lojasiewicz bound f(w) - f* <= |grad f(w)|^2 / (2 mu) for logistic loss + (lam/2)|w|^2, mu = lam
lam = 0.1
F = lambda w: np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, -y * (X @ w))) + lam / 2 * w @ w
G = lambda w: -X.T @ (y / (1 + np.exp(y * (X @ w)))) / len(y) + lam * w
w = np.zeros(3)
for _ in range(20):                                   # Newton's method finds the minimizer to machine precision
    p_ = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w)))
    w -= np.linalg.solve((X.T * (p_ * (1 - p_))) @ X / len(y) + lam * np.eye(3), G(w))
Fs = F(w)
ratios = [(F(u) - Fs) / (G(u) @ G(u) / (2 * lam)) for u in rng.normal(size=(1000, 3)) * 3]
print(round(max(ratios), 3))                           # at most 1: the bound holds at every sampled point
# 0.9

# the convex hull of (0,0), (3,0), (0,3): weights of (1,1) and of (2,2)
P = np.array([[0., 3, 0], [0, 0, 3], [1, 1, 1]])      # columns: the points, with a row of ones for sum = 1
print(np.linalg.solve(P, [1, 1, 1]).round(3), np.linalg.solve(P, [2, 2, 1]).round(3))
# [0.333 0.333 0.333] [-0.333  0.667  0.667]
```

Convexity says that the bottom of the bowl can be recognized. It does not say how to get there, or how many steps it takes. The [next note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization) states the optimality conditions for a general smooth function, proves how fast gradient descent converges on smooth and on strongly convex functions, with the condition number $\kappa$ setting the speed, and derives Newton's method from the quadratic model, which removes $\kappa$ at the price of solving a linear system per step.
