Run gradient descent with step $0.05$ on the function

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &= x^4 - 2x^2 + \tfrac12 x , \\
f'(x) &= 4x^3 - 4x + \tfrac12 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Started at $x = 1.5$, it stops at $0.930$ with $f = -0.517$. Started at $-1.5$, it stops at $-1.057$ with $f = -1.515$. Both runs report convergence, and at both end points the derivative is zero and the second derivative is positive:

| Stationary point $x$ | $-1.057$ | $0.127$ | $0.930$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $f(x)$ | $-1.515$ | $0.032$ | $-0.517$ |
| $f''(x) = 12x^2 - 4$ | $9.42$ | $-3.81$ | $6.39$ |

A test that looks only at the point, as the gradient and the Hessian of the calculus section do, cannot tell the global minimum from the other one. For a function of one variable we can list the stationary points and compare them; for the loss of a model with millions of weights we cannot.

The way out is to restrict the class of functions until the local test becomes a global one. That class is the convex functions, and the restriction costs less than it seems: least squares, logistic regression, the support vector machine and the lasso all have convex losses.

## Convex sets

A set $C \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ is **convex** if it contains the segment between any two of its points. For $x$ and $y$ in $C$, the segment is the set of points $\theta x + (1 - \theta)y$ with $\theta \in [0, 1]$:

$$
x, y \in C,\ \theta \in [0, 1] \ \Longrightarrow\ \theta x + (1 - \theta)y \in C .
$$

<ConvexSets />

A **half-space** $\{x : a^\top x \le b\}$ is convex, because the constraint is linear. If $a^\top x \le b$ and $a^\top y \le b$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^\top\big(\theta x + (1 - \theta)y\big) &= \theta\, a^\top x + (1 - \theta)\, a^\top y \\
&\le \theta b + (1 - \theta) b = b .
\end{aligned}
$$

A linear classifier splits the space into two half-spaces. Other convex sets met throughout machine learning:

- norm balls $\{x : \lVert x - c\rVert \le r\}$, for every norm, so the L1 ball of the lasso too: by the triangle inequality and homogeneity, $\lVert\theta(x - c) + (1 - \theta)(y - c)\rVert \le \theta\lVert x - c\rVert + (1 - \theta)\lVert y - c\rVert \le r$;
- the probability simplex $\{p : p_i \ge 0,\ \sum_i p_i = 1\}$, the set of distributions on $n$ outcomes and the range of the softmax: a weighted average of non-negative vectors is non-negative, and its entries sum to $\theta + (1 - \theta) = 1$;
- the positive semidefinite matrices, since $v^\top A v \ge 0$ and $v^\top B v \ge 0$ give $\theta\, v^\top A v + (1 - \theta)\, v^\top B v \ge 0$; covariance and kernel matrices live here.

A ring $\{1 \le \lVert x\rVert \le 2\}$ is not convex: it contains $(1.5, 0)$ and $(-1.5, 0)$ but not their midpoint, the origin. Neither is the union of two disjoint discs, for the same reason with one point in each disc.

New convex sets come from old ones. If $x$ and $y$ lie in every $C_i$ of a family of convex sets, the segment between them does too, so any intersection of convex sets is convex. A finite intersection of half-spaces, $\{x : Ax \le b\}$, is a **polyhedron**; the feasible sets of linear programs and of the SVM are polyhedra. The image of a convex set under an affine map $x \mapsto Ax + b$ is convex, and so is its preimage ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, EE364a](#references), lecture 2). Both follow from one identity: the map sends $\theta x + (1 - \theta)y$ to $\theta(Ax + b) + (1 - \theta)(Ay + b)$, the same combination of the images. An ellipsoid, for example, is the image of a ball. Unions do not preserve convexity, as the two discs show.

A **convex combination** of points $x_1, \dots, x_k$ is a weighted average $\sum_i \theta_i x_i$ with $\theta_i \ge 0$ and $\sum_i \theta_i = 1$. The **convex hull** $\operatorname{conv}\{x_1, \dots, x_k\}$ is the set of all convex combinations. It is the smallest convex set containing the points: a convex set that contains them contains every convex combination, by induction on $k$. For $\theta_k \lt 1$ the induction step writes

$$
\sum_{i=1}^k \theta_i x_i = (1 - \theta_k)\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \frac{\theta_i}{1 - \theta_k}\, x_i + \theta_k x_k ,
$$

a two-point combination of $x_k$ and a combination of $k - 1$ points, whose weights again sum to 1. For finitely many points in the plane it is the polygon a rubber band would form around them.

Is $(1, 1)$ in the convex hull of $(0, 0)$, $(3, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$? The weights must satisfy $3\theta_2 = 1$, $3\theta_3 = 1$ and $\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3 = 1$, so all three are $\tfrac13$, non-negative, and the answer is yes. For $(2, 2)$ the same equations give $\theta_1 = -\tfrac13$. The three equations have exactly one solution, since the matrix with columns $(0, 0, 1)$, $(3, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 3, 1)$ has determinant 9, so no non-negative weights exist and $(2, 2)$ is outside. The probability simplex is the convex hull of the standard basis vectors, and the [duality note](/notes/math/duality) shows that the hard-margin SVM looks for the two nearest points of the convex hulls of its two classes.

## Convex functions

A function $f$ on a convex set is **convex** if every chord lies on or above its graph:

$$
f\big(\theta x + (1 - \theta)y\big) \le \theta f(x) + (1 - \theta) f(y)
$$

for all $x$, $y$ and $\theta \in [0, 1]$. It is **strictly convex** if the inequality is strict whenever $x \ne y$ and $0 \lt \theta \lt 1$, and **concave** if $-f$ is convex. Equivalently, the region above the graph, the **epigraph** $\{(x, t) : t \ge f(x)\}$, is a convex set. If $t \ge f(x)$ and $s \ge f(y)$, the chord inequality gives $\theta t + (1 - \theta)s \ge f(\theta x + (1 - \theta)y)$; conversely, the points $(x, f(x))$ and $(y, f(y))$ lie in the epigraph, and convexity of the epigraph is the chord inequality. The sublevel sets $\{x : f(x) \le \beta\}$ of a convex function are convex as well: if $f(x) \le \beta$ and $f(y) \le \beta$, the chord bounds $f$ on the segment by $\beta$. Convex sublevel sets do not make a function convex: $\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}$ has intervals as sublevel sets, yet its chord from 0 to 1 passes below the graph at $\tfrac14$, where $\tfrac14 \lt \tfrac12$.

By induction the inequality extends from two points to any convex combination, $f(\sum_i \theta_i x_i) \le \sum_i \theta_i f(x_i)$, which is **Jensen's inequality**: the step splits off the last point as for the convex hull, applies the two-point inequality, then the inequality for $k - 1$ points. With the weights read as probabilities it says $f(\mathbb E[X]) \le \mathbb E[f(X)]$ for an $X$ with finitely many values. The same holds for integrals and general expectations whenever they exist ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), Section 3.1.8). The [VAE note](/notes/generative-models/vae#where-the-elbo-comes-from) bounds a log-likelihood with it; the logarithm is concave, so there the inequality flips.

For $f(x) = x^2$ the gap between chord and graph is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\theta x^2 + (1 - \theta) y^2 - \big(\theta x + (1 - \theta) y\big)^2 \\
&= \theta(1 - \theta)x^2 + \theta(1 - \theta)y^2 - 2\theta(1 - \theta)xy \\
&= \theta(1 - \theta)(x - y)^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The middle line uses $\theta - \theta^2 = \theta(1 - \theta)$ and $(1 - \theta) - (1 - \theta)^2 = \theta(1 - \theta)$.

It is never negative, so $x^2$ is convex, and it is positive whenever $x \ne y$ and $0 \lt \theta \lt 1$, so $x^2$ is strictly convex. For $x = 0$, $y = 2$ and $\theta = \tfrac12$ the chord is at $2$ and the graph at $1$. Every norm is convex, by the triangle inequality and homogeneity, as for the balls above.

Checking chords pair by pair is impractical in many dimensions. For differentiable functions two tests use the derivatives instead.

## The first-order condition: the tangent lies below

For a convex function the chord from $x$ towards $y$ lies above the graph. As the chord shrinks to a point it turns into the tangent, so the tangent at any point lies below the whole graph. For a differentiable $f$ on an open convex set:

$$
\boxed{\; f \text{ is convex} \iff f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x) \ \text{ for all } x, y \;}
$$

<TangentBelow />

Fix $x$ and $y$ and take $0 \lt \theta \le 1$. Convexity with the weights $\theta$ and $1 - \theta$ bounds $f$ at the point $x + \theta(y - x)$ of the segment; subtracting $f(x)$ and dividing by $\theta$ gives a difference quotient:

$$
\frac{f\big(x + \theta(y - x)\big) - f(x)}{\theta} \le f(y) - f(x) .
$$

As $\theta \to 0$ the left side tends to the directional derivative $\nabla f(x)^\top (y - x)$, by the chain rule for $t \mapsto f(x + t(y - x))$ at $t = 0$, and the inequality survives the limit. The converse is as short. Put $z = \theta x + (1 - \theta)y$, write the tangent bound at $z$ once towards $x$ and once towards $y$, multiply the two inequalities by $\theta$ and $1 - \theta$, and add. The gradient terms cancel, because $\theta(x - z) + (1 - \theta)(y - z) = 0$, and what remains is $\theta f(x) + (1 - \theta) f(y) \ge f(z)$.

At $x = 0$, $e^x$ has the tangent $1 + y$, so the bound says $e^y \ge 1 + y$ for every $y$: at $y = 1$, $2.718 \ge 2$. The opening function fails the test. At its local minimum $x = 0.930$ the tangent is the horizontal line at height $-0.517$, and at $x = -1.057$ the graph is $0.998$ below it.

The bound is why convexity matters. Put $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ into it, and it reads $f(y) \ge f(x^*)$ for every $y$. For a differentiable convex function, a point with zero gradient is a global minimum.

## The second-order condition: a positive semidefinite Hessian

A function is convex exactly when it is convex along every line, because chords along a line are chords of $f$. Restrict $f$ to the line through $x$ in direction $v$, $g(t) = f(x + tv)$. Its derivatives come from the chain rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g'(t) &= \nabla f(x + tv)^\top v , \\
g''(t) &= v^\top \nabla^2 f(x + tv)\, v .
\end{aligned}
$$

In one variable, $g$ is convex exactly when $g'' \ge 0$. If $g'' \ge 0$, then $g'$ never decreases, and the gap to the tangent at $t$, $g(s) - g(t) - g'(t)(s - t) = \int_t^s \big(g'(r) - g'(t)\big)\,dr$, is non-negative on both sides of $t$: the tangent bound. Conversely, the tangent bound written at $s$ and at $t$ and added gives $\big(g'(s) - g'(t)\big)(s - t) \ge 0$, so $g'$ is nondecreasing, and its derivative $g''$, a limit of non-negative difference quotients, is $\ge 0$. Requiring $g'' \ge 0$ at every point and in every direction $v$ is exactly the condition on the Hessian. For a twice differentiable $f$ on an open convex set:

$$
f \text{ is convex} \iff \nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0 \ \text{ for all } x .
$$

Here $A \succeq 0$ means that the symmetric matrix $A$ is positive semidefinite, $v^\top Av \ge 0$ for all $v$, as in the [symmetric matrices note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices). If $\nabla^2 f(x) \succ 0$ (positive definite) everywhere, $f$ is strictly convex: then $g'' \gt 0$, $g'$ is strictly increasing, the gap integral above is positive for $s \ne t$, and the converse argument of the tangent test, run with strict inequalities, gives strict chords. The converse fails: $x^4$ has $f''(0) = 0$, yet its derivative $4x^3$ is strictly increasing, so the same argument makes it strictly convex.

The quadratic $f(x) = \tfrac12 x^\top A x + b^\top x$ with symmetric $A$ has the constant Hessian $A$, so it is convex exactly when $A \succeq 0$. For $A$ with rows $(2, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$ the eigenvalues are 1 and 3: convex. For rows $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$ they are 3 and $-1$, and the direction $v = (1, -1)$, with $v^\top A v = -2$, exposes it. With $b = 0$, $f(\pm v) = -1$, while the midpoint has $f(0) = 0$, above the chord.

The log-sum-exp $\operatorname{lse}(z) = \log\sum_i e^{z_i}$, the normalizer of the softmax, has gradient $p = \operatorname{softmax}(z)$ and Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top$, the Jacobian of $p$; both are derived in the [matrix calculus note](/notes/math/matrix-calculus#the-softmax-cross-entropy). For any $v$,

$$
v^\top \big(\operatorname{diag}(p) - p p^\top\big)v = \sum_i p_i v_i^2 - \Big(\sum_i p_i v_i\Big)^2 \ge 0 ,
$$

because the right side is the variance of a random variable that takes the value $v_i$ with probability $p_i$. For $z = (1, 2, 3)$, $p = (0.090, 0.245, 0.665)$, and the Hessian has eigenvalues $0$, $0.119$ and $0.371$; for $v = (1, 0, -1)$ both sides equal $0.424$. The zero eigenvalue belongs to $v = (1, 1, 1)$: adding a constant to every logit changes nothing, so lse is convex but not strictly.

## Operations that preserve convexity

Most losses are not checked from scratch. They are built from convex pieces by operations that keep convexity, each a line of algebra from the chord definition ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, EE364a](#references), lecture 3):

- a non-negative weighted sum $\sum_i w_i f_i$ of convex functions is convex: add their chord inequalities with the weights $w_i$. An average loss over training examples plus a convex penalty is convex;
- a composition $f(Ax + b)$ with an affine map is convex, because the affine map sends a segment to a segment. A convex loss of a linear model's prediction is convex in the weights;
- a pointwise maximum $\max_i f_i$ of convex functions is convex: its epigraph is the intersection of their epigraphs. The hinge $\max(0, 1 - m)$ is the maximum of two affine functions, and for symmetric $X$, $\lambda_{\max}(X) = \max_{\lVert v\rVert = 1} v^\top X v$ (the [Rayleigh quotient](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-rayleigh-quotient-the-extreme-directions)), a maximum of functions linear in $X$, is convex in $X$. The epigraph argument works for any family, finite or not.

A convex nondecreasing function of a convex function is convex. If $g$ is convex and $h: \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ is convex and nondecreasing, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
h\big(g(\theta x + (1 - \theta)y)\big) &\le h\big(\theta g(x) + (1 - \theta) g(y)\big) \\
&\le \theta\, h(g(x)) + (1 - \theta)\, h(g(y)) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first step uses that $h$ is nondecreasing, the second that $h$ is convex. So $e^{g(x)}$ is convex whenever $g$ is.

A product, a difference, or a composition in the wrong order need not be convex. The functions $x$ and $-x$ are convex, but their product $-x^2$ is not, and $(ab - 1)^2$, the squared error of the product of two weights, is the counterexample of the section on losses below.

## Why every local minimum is global

A point $x^*$ is a **local minimum** if $f(x^*) \le f(x)$ for all $x$ in some ball $\lVert x - x^*\rVert \le r$ with $r > 0$. For a convex $f$ on a convex set $C$, a local minimum is a global one: $f(x^*) \le f(y)$ for every $y \in C$.

Suppose some $y \in C$ had $f(y) \lt f(x^*)$. Walk a small fraction $\theta$ of the way from $x^*$ towards $y$, to $z = (1 - \theta)x^* + \theta y$. Since $\lVert z - x^*\rVert = \theta\lVert y - x^*\rVert$, any $\theta \in (0, 1]$ with $\theta \le r/\lVert y - x^*\rVert$ puts $z$ inside the ball. The point $z$ is in $C$ because $C$ is convex, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(z) &\le (1 - \theta) f(x^*) + \theta f(y) && \text{convexity} \\
&\lt (1 - \theta) f(x^*) + \theta f(x^*) = f(x^*) .
\end{aligned}
$$

So $z$ is inside the ball and strictly lower than $x^*$, which contradicts local minimality. The same chord gives two more facts. The set of minimizers is convex: if $x$ and $y$ both attain the minimum value, the chord keeps $f$ at or below it on the segment. And a strictly convex function has at most one minimizer, since two distinct ones would make the midpoint strictly lower than both.

Least squares with two identical feature columns shows both. For a target of 1 and a feature equal to 1, the loss $(w_1 + w_2 - 1)^2$ is convex, and its minimizers form the whole line $w_1 + w_2 = 1$, a convex set; gradient descent stops somewhere on it, always at the minimum value 0. Its gradient $2(w_1 + w_2 - 1)(1, 1)$ never changes $w_1 - w_2$, and with the step $\tfrac14$ the sum $s = w_1 + w_2$ moves to $s - \tfrac14 \cdot 4(s - 1) = 1$ in one step. The penalty $\lambda(w_1^2 + w_2^2)$ adds $2\lambda I$ to the Hessian, which has rows $(2, 2)$ and $(2, 2)$, and so makes it positive definite and the loss strictly convex. Setting both partial derivatives $2(w_1 + w_2 - 1) + 2\lambda w_i$ to zero forces $w_1 = w_2 = w$ with $2(2w - 1) + 2\lambda w = 0$, so the one minimizer is $w_1 = w_2 = 1/(2 + \lambda)$, which is $0.476$ for $\lambda = 0.1$.

## Strong convexity and smoothness

Convexity says the function lies above its tangents. Two quantitative versions say by how much, at least and at most, and they set the speed of every algorithm in the [next note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization).

A differentiable $f$ is **$\mu$-strongly convex**, with $\mu \gt 0$, if it lies above each tangent by at least a parabola:

$$
f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) + \frac\mu2 \lVert y - x\rVert^2 .
$$

Equivalently, $h(x) = f(x) - \frac\mu2\lVert x\rVert^2$ is convex. Its gradient is $\nabla f(x) - \mu x$, and its tangent test, after moving the $\mu$ terms to the right, adds $\frac\mu2\big(\lVert y\rVert^2 - \lVert x\rVert^2 - 2x^\top(y - x)\big) = \frac\mu2\lVert y - x\rVert^2$ to the tangent of $f$. For twice differentiable $f$ the Hessian test for $h$ reads $\nabla^2 f(x) - \mu I \succeq 0$ everywhere: every eigenvalue of the Hessian is at least $\mu$.

A differentiable $f$ is **$L$-smooth** if its gradient is $L$-Lipschitz, $\lVert\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\rVert \le L\lVert x - y\rVert$; for twice continuously differentiable $f$ this means $-LI \preceq \nabla^2 f(x) \preceq LI$. One way, $\nabla^2 f(x)v$ is the limit of $\big(\nabla f(x + tv) - \nabla f(x)\big)/t$, whose norm is at most $L\lVert v\rVert$, so every eigenvalue lies in $[-L, L]$. The other way, $\nabla f(y) - \nabla f(x) = \int_0^1 \nabla^2 f(x + t(y - x))(y - x)\, dt$, and a symmetric matrix with eigenvalues in $[-L, L]$ stretches no vector by more than $L$. The gradient cannot change faster than $L$ per unit of distance, so the function cannot rise above its tangent faster than a parabola of curvature $L$:

$$
f(y) \le f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) + \frac L2 \lVert y - x\rVert^2 .
$$

To prove it, let $d = y - x$, $x_t = x + td$, and write $\Delta$ for the gap $f(y) - f(x) - \nabla f(x)^\top d$ between the function and its tangent:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta &= \int_0^1 \big(\nabla f(x_t) - \nabla f(x)\big)^\top d\, dt \\
&\le \int_0^1 \lVert\nabla f(x_t) - \nabla f(x)\rVert\, \lVert d\rVert\, dt \\
&\le \int_0^1 L\, t\, \lVert d\rVert^2\, dt = \frac L2 \lVert d\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

1. The fundamental theorem of calculus for $t \mapsto f(x_t)$, whose derivative is $\nabla f(x_t)^\top d$; the constant $\nabla f(x)^\top d$ integrates to itself.
2. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
3. The Lipschitz gradient, with $\lVert x_t - x\rVert = t\lVert d\rVert$.

This is the route of [Bubeck (2015)](#references), Lemma 3.4. A function that is both strongly convex and smooth is squeezed between two parabolas around every tangent, of curvatures $\mu$ and $L$. Their ratio $\kappa = L/\mu \ge 1$ is the **condition number** of $f$. For a quadratic it is $\lambda_{\max}(A)/\lambda_{\min}(A)$, the condition number of the matrix that the [conditioning note](/notes/math/conditioning-and-stability) studies from the numerical side.

For $f(x) = \tfrac12x^\top A x$ with $A$ of rows $(2, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$, $\mu = 1$, $L = 3$ and $\kappa = 3$. From $x = 0$ to $y = (1, 0)$ the tangent is 0, $f(y) = 1$, and the two parabolas give $0.5 \le 1 \le 1.5$.

### How far from the minimum?

Strong convexity turns a small gradient into a guarantee. Write the lower parabola at $x$ towards the minimizer $x^*$, with $f^* = f(x^*)$. A value is at least the minimum over all points, and the parabola $f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) + \frac\mu2\lVert y - x\rVert^2$ is smallest where its gradient $\nabla f(x) + \mu(y - x)$ vanishes, at $y - x = -\nabla f(x)/\mu$. There it equals $f(x) - \lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2/\mu + \lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2/(2\mu)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f^* &\ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(x^* - x) + \frac\mu2\lVert x^* - x\rVert^2 \\
&\ge \min_{y} \Big( f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) + \frac\mu2\lVert y - x\rVert^2 \Big) \\
&= f(x) - \frac{1}{2\mu}\lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Rearranged, $f(x) - f^* \le \lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2/(2\mu)$. This is the **Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality**, and the next note turns it into the linear convergence rate of gradient descent. For the quadratic above at $x = (1, 1)$, $f = 3$ and $\nabla f = (3, 3)$, and the bound is $18/2 = 9 \ge 3$. The inequality can hold without convexity: [Merkulov (2024)](#references) shows $x^2 + 3\sin^2 x$, whose second derivative $2 + 6\cos 2x$ dips to $-4$. [Karimi, Nutini and Schmidt (2016)](#references) state that it satisfies the inequality with $\mu = 1/32$ (Section 2.2) and build linear-rate proofs on the inequality alone (Theorem 1).

The ridge penalty $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ adds $2\lambda I$ to any Hessian, so a convex loss plus ridge is $2\lambda$-strongly convex. The [regularization note](/notes/ml/regularization) uses the penalty for statistics; it also fixes the geometry. The logistic loss has Hessian $\frac1n X^\top S X$ with $S = \operatorname{diag}(p_i(1 - p_i))$, from the [matrix calculus note](/notes/math/matrix-calculus#the-check-against-logistic-regression). Since $p(1 - p) = \tfrac14 - (p - \tfrac12)^2 \le \tfrac14$, we get $v^\top X^\top S X v \le \tfrac14\lVert Xv\rVert^2 \le \tfrac14\lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)\lVert v\rVert^2$, so the loss is $L$-smooth with $L = \lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)/(4n)$. Far from the origin the predictions saturate: along $w = t\,w_0$ every $p_i(1 - p_i)$ with $w_0^\top x_i \ne 0$ tends to 0 as $t \to \infty$, so the smallest Hessian eigenvalue tends to 0, and without a penalty no $\mu \gt 0$ works everywhere.

## Which machine learning losses are convex

The tests above settle the standard losses of linear models, all in the weights $w$ for fixed data:

| Loss | Why it is convex | Strongly convex? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| least squares $\frac1n\lVert X w - y\rVert^2$ | Hessian $\frac2n X^\top X \succeq 0$ ([least squares](/notes/ml/least-squares)) | if $X$ has full column rank: $v^\top X^\top X v = \lVert Xv\rVert^2 \gt 0$ for $v \ne 0$ |
| logistic $\frac1n\sum_i \log(1 + e^{-y_i w^\top x_i})$ | convex function of an affine map ([logistic regression](/notes/ml/logistic-regression#the-gradient-residuals-times-inputs)) | no, only with a penalty |
| softmax cross-entropy | lse of an affine map minus a linear term | no: shifting all logits is flat |
| hinge $\frac1n\sum_i \max(0, 1 - y_i w^\top x_i)$ | maximum of affine functions ([SVM](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines)) | no; not differentiable either |
| ridge $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$, lasso $\lambda\lVert w\rVert_1$ | a squared norm, a norm | ridge yes ($2\lambda$), lasso no |

A neural network composes linear maps with nonlinear activations, and its loss as a function of all the weights is not convex. The smallest example is a "network" with one hidden unit and no activation, $\hat y = a\,b\,x$, fitted to one example $x = y = 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(a, b) &= (ab - 1)^2 , \\
\nabla^2 f(a, b) &= \begin{pmatrix} 2b^2 & 4ab - 2 \\ 4ab - 2 & 2a^2 \end{pmatrix} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Both $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$ fit exactly, with loss 0, but their midpoint $(0, 0)$ has loss 1, above the chord. The Hessian there has eigenvalues $2$ and $-2$: the origin is a saddle point with zero gradient. The same sign symmetry, and the permutation symmetry of hidden units, holds in every network, as the [deep learning course](/notes/deep-learning/optimizing-deep-networks#many-equivalent-minima-so-not-convex) works out. Matrix factorization, $k$-means and mixture models are non-convex for the same reason: a model with interchangeable parts has several equivalent minimizers and worse points between them. For these, the guarantees of the next note shrink to "the gradient becomes small", and the starting point matters.

## Where it breaks

Convexity does not promise that a minimum exists. Logistic regression on one point $x = 1$ with label $y = 1$ has the loss $f(w) = \log(1 + e^{-w})$, convex and decreasing towards 0, a value no $w$ attains. Gradient descent with step 1 never stops moving:

| Steps | $w$ | $f(w)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | $2.26$ | $9.9 \cdot 10^{-2}$ |
| 100 | $4.59$ | $1.0 \cdot 10^{-2}$ |
| 1,000 | $6.91$ | $1.0 \cdot 10^{-3}$ |
| 10,000 | $9.21$ | $1.0 \cdot 10^{-4}$ |

The weight grows like $\log k$ and the loss falls like $1/k$, as the fold derives. The same happens on any linearly separable data set: if $y_i w^\top x_i \gt 0$ for every $i$, each term $\log(1 + e^{-t\,y_i w^\top x_i})$ decreases as $t$ grows, so scaling up a separating $w$ always lowers the loss, which stays positive. A ridge penalty restores a minimizer. The penalized loss is at least $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$, so outside a large enough ball it exceeds its value at $w = 0$; on that closed ball a continuous function attains its minimum.

<details>
<summary>Derivation: why the weight grows like log k</summary>

The step is $w_{k+1} = w_k + 1/(1 + e^{w_k})$. Put $u_k = e^{w_k}$, so $u_0 = 1$ and $u_{k+1} = u_k\, e^{1/(1 + u_k)}$. The weight only grows, so $u_k \ge 1$ and the exponent $\tau = 1/(1 + u_k)$ lies in $(0, \tfrac12]$. From $1 + \tau \le e^\tau \le 1 + \tau e^\tau$, the tangent bound and the mean value theorem:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{k+1} - u_k &\ge \frac{u_k}{1 + u_k} \ge \frac12 , \\
u_{k+1} - u_k &\le \frac{u_k}{1 + u_k}\, e^{1/2} \le e^{1/2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

So $1 + k/2 \le u_k \le 1 + 1.65\,k$: $w_k = \log u_k$ is $\log k$ up to a bounded term, and the loss $\log(1 + 1/u_k)$, which lies between $1/(2u_k)$ and $1/u_k$ because $x - x^2/2 \le \log(1 + x) \le x$ and $x = 1/u_k \le 1$, falls like $1/k$.

</details>

Convexity also says nothing about speed: a convex function can be very flat or badly conditioned, and the next note shows that the number of gradient steps grows with $\kappa$. And the hinge and the L1 norm are convex but not differentiable at their kinks. There the tangent bound becomes a family of supporting lines, the subgradients, and gradient methods need their non-smooth variants.

## What you should remember

- Convex set: contains every chord. Convex function: every chord lies above the graph.
- Equivalent tests: the tangent lies below, $f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x)$; or $\nabla^2 f \succeq 0$ everywhere.
- Sums, affine substitutions, maxima and convex nondecreasing outer functions keep convexity.
- For a convex $f$, every local minimum is global, and $\nabla f = 0$ certifies it.
- $\mu I \preceq \nabla^2 f \preceq LI$, $\kappa = L/\mu$, and $f(x) - f^* \le \lVert\nabla f(x)\rVert^2/(2\mu)$.
- Linear models with standard losses are convex; neural networks, factorizations and mixtures are not.

## Code

The opening function, the chord test on three convex losses and one non-convex one, the tangent and Hessian tests, the parabola sandwich, the Polyak–Łojasiewicz bound, and logistic regression without a minimizer, with NumPy. Exact digits can differ slightly across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# the opening function f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2 + x/2 has two local minima; gradient descent finds the one near its start
f = lambda x: x**4 - 2 * x**2 + 0.5 * x
df = lambda x: 4 * x**3 - 4 * x + 0.5
crit = np.sort(np.roots([4, 0, -4, 0.5]).real)
print(crit.round(3), f(crit).round(3), (12 * crit**2 - 4).round(2))   # stationary points, values, f''
for x in (1.5, -1.5):
    x0 = x
    for _ in range(2000):
        x -= 0.05 * df(x)
    print(x0, round(x, 3), round(f(x), 3))
# [-1.057  0.127  0.93 ] [-1.515  0.032 -0.517] [ 9.42 -3.81  6.39]
# 1.5 0.93 -0.517 | -1.5 -1.057 -1.515

# the chord test f(t u + (1 - t) v) <= t f(u) + (1 - t) f(v) on 10,000 random pairs
X = rng.normal(size=(50, 3)); y = np.sign(rng.normal(size=50))
losses = {
    'least squares': lambda w: np.mean((X @ w - y) ** 2),
    'logistic': lambda w: np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, -y * (X @ w))),
    'hinge': lambda w: np.mean(np.maximum(0, 1 - y * (X @ w))),
    '(ab - 1)^2': lambda w: (w[0] * w[1] - 1) ** 2,
}
for name, L in losses.items():
    bad = 0
    for _ in range(10_000):
        u, v, t = rng.normal(size=3) * 2, rng.normal(size=3) * 2, rng.uniform()
        bad += L(t * u + (1 - t) * v) > t * L(u) + (1 - t) * L(v) + 1e-12
    print(name, bad)
# least squares 0 | logistic 0 | hinge 0 | (ab - 1)^2 1779

# (ab - 1)^2: two minimizers, a worse midpoint, and an indefinite Hessian there
g = losses['(ab - 1)^2']
print(g(np.array([1., 1, 0])), g(np.array([-1., -1, 0])), g(np.zeros(3)), np.linalg.eigvalsh([[0, -2], [-2, 0]]))
# 0.0 0.0 1.0 [-2.  2.]

# the tangent lies below: e^y >= 1 + y; at the local minimum of the opening function the flat tangent does not
print(np.exp(1.0) - 2, f(crit[0]) - f(crit[2]))
# 0.7182818284590451 -0.9980051329904565

# the Hessian of log-sum-exp is diag(p) - p p^T, a covariance matrix
z = np.array([1., 2, 3]); p = np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum()
H = np.diag(p) - np.outer(p, p); v = np.array([1., 0, -1])
print(p.round(4), np.linalg.eigvalsh(H).round(4), round(v @ H @ v, 4), round(p @ v**2 - (p @ v) ** 2, 4))
# [0.09   0.2447 0.6652] [-0.      0.1186  0.3709] 0.4244 0.4244

# mu and L of a quadratic, and the sandwich mu/2 |d|^2 <= f(x + d) - f(x) - grad^T d <= L/2 |d|^2
A = np.array([[2., 1], [1, 2]]); mu, L = np.linalg.eigvalsh(A)
q = lambda x: 0.5 * x @ A @ x
ok = 0
for _ in range(10_000):
    x, d = rng.normal(size=2), rng.normal(size=2)
    gap = q(x + d) - q(x) - (A @ x) @ d
    ok += mu / 2 * d @ d - 1e-12 <= gap <= L / 2 * d @ d + 1e-12
print(mu, L, L / mu, ok)
# 1.0 3.0 3.0 10000

# the Polyak-Lojasiewicz bound f(w) - f* <= |grad f(w)|^2 / (2 mu) for logistic loss + (lam/2)|w|^2, mu = lam
lam = 0.1
F = lambda w: np.mean(np.logaddexp(0, -y * (X @ w))) + lam / 2 * w @ w
G = lambda w: -X.T @ (y / (1 + np.exp(y * (X @ w)))) / len(y) + lam * w
w = np.zeros(3)
for _ in range(20):                                   # Newton's method finds the minimizer to machine precision
    p_ = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ w)))
    w -= np.linalg.solve((X.T * (p_ * (1 - p_))) @ X / len(y) + lam * np.eye(3), G(w))
Fs = F(w)
ratios = [(F(u) - Fs) / (G(u) @ G(u) / (2 * lam)) for u in rng.normal(size=(1000, 3)) * 3]
print(round(max(ratios), 3))                           # at most 1: the bound holds at every sampled point
# 0.9

# a non-convex function with the PL property: x^2 + 3 sin^2 x
t = np.linspace(-20, 20, 400_001); t = t[np.abs(t) > 1e-3]
print(round((2 + 6 * np.cos(2 * t)).min(), 3), round(((2 * t + 3 * np.sin(2 * t)) ** 2 / (2 * (t**2 + 3 * np.sin(t) ** 2))).min(), 3))
# -4.0 0.176: f'' dips to -4, yet f'^2 >= 2 * 0.176 * (f - f*) on the whole range

# the convex hull of (0,0), (3,0), (0,3): weights of (1,1) and of (2,2)
P = np.array([[0., 3, 0], [0, 0, 3], [1, 1, 1]])      # columns: the points, with a row of ones for sum = 1
print(np.linalg.solve(P, [1, 1, 1]).round(3), np.linalg.solve(P, [2, 2, 1]).round(3))
# [0.333 0.333 0.333] [-0.333  0.667  0.667]

# no minimizer: logistic loss log(1 + e^-w) on one separable point, gradient descent with step 1
w = 0.0
for k in range(1, 10_001):
    w += 1 / (1 + np.exp(w))
    if k in (10, 100, 1000, 10_000):
        print(k, round(w, 2), f'{np.log1p(np.exp(-w)):.1e}', end=' | ')
print()
# 10 2.26 9.9e-02 | 100 4.59 1.0e-02 | 1000 6.91 1.0e-03 | 10000 9.21 1.0e-04
```
