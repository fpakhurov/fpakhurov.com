Minimize $f(x) = x_1^2 + 2x_2^2$ over the points of the line $x_1 + x_2 = 1$. Here the constraint can be solved for one variable. Substituting $x_1 = 1 - x_2$ leaves a function of $x_2$ alone:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi(x_2) &= (1 - x_2)^2 + 2x_2^2 , \\
\varphi'(x_2) &= -2(1 - x_2) + 4x_2 = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

So $x_2 = \tfrac13$, $x_1 = \tfrac23$ and $f = \tfrac23$.

Elimination rarely goes this smoothly. On the unit circle $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 1$ it gives two branches $x_1 = \pm\sqrt{1 - x_2^2}$ with infinite slopes at their ends. With the inequalities $x_i \ge 0$ of a probability vector it turns into a case split over which coordinates are zero, $2^n$ cases in $n$ dimensions. The SVM has one inequality per training point. These problems need a method that keeps all the variables and treats every constraint the same way.

It starts from an observation about the minimum itself. Over all of $\mathbb R^n$, as in the [previous note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization), a minimum has a zero gradient. Under a constraint it need not: the function may keep decreasing, but only in directions the constraint forbids.

## Equality constraints: the gradient is parallel to the normal

<LagrangeGeometry />

At a constrained minimum, no direction that stays on the constraint surface can decrease $f$ to first order. So the gradient of $f$ is perpendicular to the surface, and so is the gradient of the constraint. Two vectors perpendicular to the same surface of dimension $n - 1$ are parallel. For one constraint $h(x) = 0$ with $\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0$, a local minimum $x^*$ therefore satisfies

$$
\boxed{\;\nabla f(x^*) + \nu\,\nabla h(x^*) = 0\;}
$$

for some number $\nu$, together with $h(x^*) = 0$.

To prove it, take any smooth curve $x(t)$ on the surface with $x(0) = x^*$ and velocity $x'(0) = v$. The chain rule at $t = 0$ gives two facts:

$$
\begin{aligned}
h(x(t)) = 0 \ &\Longrightarrow\ \nabla h(x^*)^\top v = 0 , \\
f(x(t)) \ge f(x^*) \ &\Longrightarrow\ \nabla f(x^*)^\top v = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second holds because $t = 0$ is a minimum of the one-variable function $f(x(t))$, so its derivative vanishes. Where $\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0$, the implicit function theorem makes the surface smooth near $x^*$, and every $v$ orthogonal to $\nabla h(x^*)$ is the velocity of some curve on it. So $\nabla f(x^*)$ is orthogonal to every vector that $\nabla h(x^*)$ is orthogonal to, and the only such vectors are multiples of $\nabla h(x^*)$: $\nabla f(x^*) = -\nu\,\nabla h(x^*)$.

With several constraints $h_1, \dots, h_p$, the tangent directions are those with $\nabla h_j^\top v = 0$ for every $j$, and the same argument gives

$$
\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_{j=1}^p \nu_j \nabla h_j(x^*) = 0 ,
$$

provided the gradients $\nabla h_j(x^*)$ are linearly independent. This proviso is a **constraint qualification**; without it the multipliers may not exist.

For the opening problem, $\nabla f = (2x_1, 4x_2)$ and $\nabla h = (1, 1)$. At $x^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)$, $\nabla f = (\tfrac43, \tfrac43)$, parallel to $(1, 1)$, with $\nu = -\tfrac43$. At the feasible point $(0, 1)$, instead, $\nabla f = (0, 4)$ is not parallel to $(1, 1)$. Its component along the line, in the unit direction $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$, is $-4/\sqrt2 = -2.83$, so moving along the line towards larger $x_1$ lowers $f$. In the figure, the level set of $f$ through $x^*$ touches the line; through $(0, 1)$ it crosses it.

## The Lagrangian

The conditions are the stationarity conditions of one function. The **Lagrangian** adds each constraint to the objective with its multiplier ([Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, 2020](#references), Section 7.2):

$$
\mathcal L(x, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_{j=1}^p \nu_j h_j(x) .
$$

Its gradient in $x$ is $\nabla f + \sum_j \nu_j\nabla h_j$, and its gradient in $\nu$ is the vector of constraint values $h_j(x)$. Setting both to zero gives the conditions above: a constrained problem in $n$ variables becomes $n + p$ equations in $n + p$ unknowns, with no constraints left. For the opening problem they are $2x_1 + \nu = 0$, $4x_2 + \nu = 0$ and $x_1 + x_2 = 1$, a linear system with the solution $(\tfrac23, \tfrac13, -\tfrac43)$.

The point $(x^*, \nu^*)$ is only a stationary point of $\mathcal L$: the Lagrangian is linear in $\nu$ and unbounded in it, so it has no minimum. Like a zero gradient, the conditions give candidates. The section on failures below shows how to sort them, and the [duality note](/notes/math/duality) when they give the answer.

### The nearest point on a plane

The shortest path from a point to a plane runs along the plane's normal. For the plane $a^\top x = b$ and a point $p$, minimize $\lVert x - p\rVert^2$ with the Lagrangian $\lVert x - p\rVert^2 + \nu(a^\top x - b)$. Stationarity in $x$, then the constraint, give

$$
\begin{aligned}
2(x - p) + \nu\,a = 0 \ &\Longrightarrow\ x = p - \tfrac\nu2\,a , \\
a^\top p - \tfrac\nu2\lVert a\rVert^2 = b \ &\Longrightarrow\ \nu = \frac{2(a^\top p - b)}{\lVert a\rVert^2} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Putting $\nu$ back gives the nearest point and its distance:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^* &= p - \frac{a^\top p - b}{\lVert a\rVert^2}\,a , \\
\lVert x^* - p\rVert &= \frac{\lvert a^\top p - b\rvert}{\lVert a\rVert} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The objective is convex and the constraint affine, so by the sufficiency result below this stationary point is the global minimum. The distance formula is the one the [SVM note](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines#which-separating-line) uses for the margin of a hyperplane.

For $a = (1, 2, 2)$, $b = 3$ and $p = (1, 1, 1)$: $a^\top p = 5$, $\lVert a\rVert^2 = 9$ and $\nu = \tfrac49$. The nearest point is $x^* = p - \tfrac29 a = (\tfrac79, \tfrac59, \tfrac59)$, which satisfies $\tfrac79 + \tfrac{10}9 + \tfrac{10}9 = 3$, at distance $\tfrac23$.

### Maximum entropy

Among all distributions with a given mean, which one spreads its probability most evenly? Take values $x_1, \dots, x_k$ and maximize the entropy $H(p) = -\sum_i p_i\log p_i$ subject to $\sum_i p_i = 1$ and $\sum_i p_i x_i = m$. Equivalently, minimize $\sum_i p_i \log p_i$ with a multiplier $\alpha$ for the normalization and $\beta$ for the mean. The derivative of the Lagrangian in $p_i$ is $\log p_i + 1 + \alpha + \beta x_i$, and setting it to zero gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_i &= e^{-1 - \alpha}\, e^{-\beta x_i} , \\
p_i &= \frac{e^{-\beta x_i}}{\sum_j e^{-\beta x_j}} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

where the normalization fixes $e^{-1-\alpha}$, and $\beta$ is chosen so that the mean is $m$. The answer is always an exponential of the constrained quantity ([Jaynes, 1957](#references)).

Every $p_i$ is positive, so the omitted constraints $p_i \ge 0$ hold automatically, and $\sum_i p_i\log p_i$ is convex, so the stationary point is the maximum of the entropy. Without the mean constraint, $\beta = 0$ and the answer is the uniform distribution.

A die with faces $1, \dots, 6$ constrained to the mean $4.5$ gives:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| $\beta$ | $-0.371$ |
| $p_1, \dots, p_6$ | $0.054, 0.079, 0.114, 0.165, 0.240, 0.347$ |
| entropy | $1.614$ nats |
| entropy of the fair die, $\log 6$ | $1.792$ nats |

The probabilities grow geometrically, by the factor $e^{0.371} = 1.45$ per face, the least committal way to raise the mean.

The formula is the softmax of the scores $-\beta x_i$. The softmax is the maximum-entropy distribution with prescribed expected scores, and the [exponential families](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#the-exponential-family) of statistics arise the same way. Replace the entropy by minus a KL divergence to a reference distribution, and move the reward from a constraint into the objective, and the same steps give the optimum of [KL-regularized reinforcement learning](/notes/agents/rl-for-language-models#the-kl-regularized-objective-and-its-optimum): the reference reweighted by an exponential of the reward. Entropy itself is the subject of the [entropy note](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#entropy-expected-surprise).

### What the multiplier measures

The multiplier is the price of the constraint. Move the constraint to $h(x) = u$ and let the solution $x^*(u)$ depend smoothly on $u$. The optimal value $f^*(u)$ then changes at the rate

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d f^*}{du} &= \nabla f(x^*)^\top\frac{d x^*}{du} \\
&= -\nu^*\,\nabla h(x^*)^\top\frac{d x^*}{du} \\
&= -\nu^*\,\frac{d}{du}h\big(x^*(u)\big) = -\nu^* .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the chain rule, the second the stationarity $\nabla f = -\nu\nabla h$, and the third the chain rule backwards: $h(x^*(u)) = u$ has derivative 1.

For the plane, $f^*(b) = (a^\top p - b)^2/\lVert a\rVert^2$, whose derivative at $b = 3$ is $-2 \cdot 2/9 = -\tfrac49 = -\nu$; here $h(x) = a^\top x - b$, so moving $b$ is moving $u$. For the opening problem, $f^*(s) = \tfrac23 s^2$ on the line $x_1 + x_2 = s$, with derivative $\tfrac43 = -\nu$ at $s = 1$. In economics the multiplier is called a shadow price ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), §5.6). In the SVM it measures how much the optimal objective would fall if the margin required of one training point were relaxed a little.

## Inequality constraints: active or inactive

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> An inequality either binds, and then acts like an equality whose multiplier must be non-negative, or it does not, and then its multiplier is zero. Complementary slackness, $\lambda_i g_i(x^*) = 0$, writes both cases as one equation.</aside>

<KktActiveInactive />

An inequality constraint $g(x) \le 0$ is **active** at $x$ if $g(x) = 0$ and **inactive** if $g(x) \lt 0$. At a local minimum $x^*$ there are two cases.

If the constraint is inactive, every small move keeps the point feasible, so $x^*$ is an unconstrained local minimum and $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$. The constraint plays no role; give it the multiplier $\lambda = 0$.

If it is active, the constraint locally acts like the equality $g = 0$, so $\nabla f + \lambda\nabla g = 0$ for some $\lambda$, and now the sign matters: $\lambda \ge 0$. Were $\lambda$ negative, the descent direction $-\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ would point into the feasible region, where $g$ decreases, and a small step along it would lower $f$ without leaving the feasible set. With $\lambda \ge 0$ the descent direction points outward, against the wall.

In one variable: minimize $(x - 2)^2$ subject to $x \le 1$, that is $g(x) = x - 1 \le 0$. The unconstrained minimum $x = 2$ is infeasible, so the constraint is active, $x^* = 1$, and $f'(1) + \lambda g'(1) = -2 + \lambda = 0$ gives $\lambda = 2 \ge 0$. With the looser constraint $x \le 3$, the unconstrained minimum is feasible, the constraint is inactive and $\lambda = 0$. The multiplier is again a price: moving the wall from 1 to $1 + u$ lowers the optimal value $(u - 1)^2$ at the rate $2 = \lambda$.

Both panels of the figure minimize $\lVert x - c\rVert^2$ over the triangle $x_1 + x_2 \le 2$, $x_1 \ge 0$, $x_2 \ge 0$, written as $g_1 = x_1 + x_2 - 2$, $g_2 = -x_1$ and $g_3 = -x_2$, all $\le 0$. For $c = (0.8, 0.6)$ inside the triangle the answer is $c$ itself, no constraint is active and every multiplier is zero. For $c = (2, 1)$ outside it the answer is $x^* = (1.5, 0.5)$ on the slanted side, with $\nabla f(x^*) = 2(x^* - c) = (-1, -1)$ and $\nabla g_1 = (1, 1)$, so $\lambda_1 = 1$; $g_2$ and $g_3$ are inactive and $\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = 0$.

## The KKT conditions

The general problem has $m$ inequalities and $p$ equalities, all with differentiable functions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{minimize}\quad & f(x) \\
\text{subject to}\quad & g_i(x) \le 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, m , \\
& h_j(x) = 0, \quad j = 1, \dots, p .
\end{aligned}
$$

A point that satisfies all constraints is **feasible**. Each inequality gets a multiplier $\lambda_i$ and each equality a multiplier $\nu_j$. Combining the Lagrange condition for the equalities with the two cases for each inequality, a point $x^*$ with multipliers $\lambda, \nu$ satisfies the **Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions** if

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\nabla f(x^*) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i\nabla g_i(x^*) + \sum_j \nu_j\nabla h_j(x^*) = 0 , \\
&g_i(x^*) \le 0, \quad h_j(x^*) = 0 , \\
&\lambda_i \ge 0 , \\
&\lambda_i\, g_i(x^*) = 0 .
\end{aligned}
}
$$

The four lines are called **stationarity**, **primal feasibility**, **dual feasibility** and **complementary slackness**.

Complementary slackness encodes the case split. The product $\lambda_i g_i$ of a non-negative and a non-positive number is zero only if one factor is zero. Either the constraint is active ($g_i = 0$) and its multiplier may be positive, or it is inactive and its multiplier is zero. A positive multiplier certifies that the constraint is tight; a slack constraint has no price. In the SVM, the training points with positive multipliers are exactly the [support vectors](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines#support-vectors-what-the-dual-variables-tell-you), which lie on or inside the margin. Both factors can vanish at once, for a constraint that touches the solution without pushing on it.

At a local minimum the KKT conditions hold if a constraint qualification holds there: for example, if the gradients of the active constraints are linearly independent (**LICQ**), or, for convex problems, if some point satisfies all inequalities strictly (**Slater's condition**, the subject of the [duality note](/notes/math/duality)). Affine constraints need no qualification at all ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 6).

For a **convex problem**, with $f$ and every $g_i$ convex and every $h_j$ affine, the conditions are also sufficient: any point that satisfies them is a global minimum. For every feasible $x$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &\ge f(x) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x) \\
&= \mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu) \\
&\ge \mathcal L(x^*, \lambda, \nu) \\
&= f(x^*) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line holds because each $\lambda_i g_i(x) \le 0$ and each $h_j(x) = 0$. The third holds because $\mathcal L(\cdot, \lambda, \nu)$, a non-negative combination of convex functions plus affine terms, is convex and stationary at $x^*$, so it lies above its tangent there ([convexity note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-first-order-condition-the-tangent-lies-below)). The last uses complementary slackness and $h_j(x^*) = 0$ ([Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004](#references), §5.5.3). In the figure, the multipliers $\lambda = (1, 0, 0)$ and the point $(1.5, 0.5)$ satisfy all four conditions, so the point is the global minimum.

## Projection onto a box and onto the simplex

Many algorithms need the nearest point of a convex set $C$ to a given $v$, the **projection**

$$
\Pi_C(v) = \arg\min_{x \in C}\ \tfrac12\lVert x - v\rVert^2 .
$$

Projected gradient descent alternates a gradient step with a projection, $x \leftarrow \Pi_C(x - \eta\nabla f(x))$, and so solves constrained problems with the machinery of the previous note. For two sets used constantly in machine learning, the KKT conditions give the projection in closed form.

**The box** $\{x : l_i \le x_i \le u_i\}$. The problem separates into coordinates. For one coordinate, with multipliers $\lambda_i^u$ for $x_i - u_i \le 0$ and $\lambda_i^l$ for $l_i - x_i \le 0$, stationarity reads $x_i - v_i + \lambda_i^u - \lambda_i^l = 0$. If $v_i \gt u_i$, the upper bound must be active, since otherwise $x_i = v_i + \lambda_i^l \ge v_i$ would be infeasible; then $x_i = u_i$ and $\lambda_i^u = v_i - u_i \gt 0$. The lower bound is symmetric. So the projection is clipping,

$$
\Pi_{\text{box}}(v)_i = \min\big(\max(v_i, l_i), u_i\big) ,
$$

with multipliers equal to the amounts clipped away. For $v = (1.7, -0.4, 0.3)$ and the unit box, $x^* = (1, 0, 0.3)$, with $\lambda_1^u = 0.7$, $\lambda_2^l = 0.4$ and all other multipliers zero. The soft-margin SVM dual is a problem over a box, $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$, and the [duality note](/notes/math/duality#the-duality-gap-as-a-stopping-criterion) solves it by projected gradient ascent with exactly this clipping.

**The probability simplex** $\Delta = \{x : x_i \ge 0, \sum_i x_i = 1\}$. Take the multiplier $\tau$ for $\sum_i x_i = 1$ and $\lambda_i$ for $-x_i \le 0$. Stationarity of $\tfrac12\lVert x - v\rVert^2 + \tau(\sum_i x_i - 1) - \sum_i\lambda_i x_i$ is $x_i - v_i + \tau - \lambda_i = 0$, and the two cases of complementary slackness give

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_i \gt 0 \ &\Longrightarrow\ \lambda_i = 0,\ x_i = v_i - \tau , \\
x_i = 0 \ &\Longrightarrow\ \lambda_i = \tau - v_i \ge 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The second case happens exactly when $v_i \le \tau$, so both cases are one formula:

$$
\Pi_\Delta(v)_i = \max(v_i - \tau, 0) , \quad \text{with } \textstyle\sum_i \max(v_i - \tau, 0) = 1 .
$$

Subtract the same threshold from every coordinate and cut at zero. The left side of the condition on $\tau$ decreases continuously from large values to 0 as $\tau$ grows, so exactly one $\tau$ works. Sort $v$ in decreasing order, $u_1 \ge u_2 \ge \dots$; for $r = 1, 2, \dots$ compute the candidate $\tau_r = \frac1r\big(\sum_{s \le r} u_s - 1\big)$, and take the largest $r$ with $u_r \gt \tau_r$. This costs $O(n\log n)$ operations ([Duchi et al., 2008](#references)).

For $v = (0.5, 1.2, -0.3)$ the sorted values are $1.2, 0.5, -0.3$:

| $r$ | $\tau_r$ | $u_r - \tau_r$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.2$ | $1.0 \gt 0$ |
| 2 | $0.35$ | $0.15 \gt 0$ |
| 3 | $0.133$ | $-0.433 \lt 0$ |

So $\tau = 0.35$, $\Pi_\Delta(v) = (0.15, 0.85, 0)$ and $\lambda_3 = 0.35 + 0.3 = 0.65$. Applied to the logits of a classifier, this projection is **sparsemax** ([Martins and Astudillo, 2016](#references)), an alternative to the softmax that gives exactly zero probability to low-scoring classes. The softmax was the maximum-entropy answer above; sparsemax is the nearest-point answer.

## The direction of maximum variance is an eigenvector

The [symmetric matrices note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-rayleigh-quotient-the-extreme-directions) bounds the Rayleigh quotient using the spectral theorem. The Lagrange conditions reach the same answer without it and show why only eigenvectors can be candidates. For a covariance matrix $\Sigma$, the variance of the data projected on a unit vector $u$ is $u^\top\Sigma u$, and PCA maximizes it subject to $u^\top u = 1$. With the Lagrangian $u^\top\Sigma u - \nu(u^\top u - 1)$ and $\nabla(u^\top\Sigma u) = 2\Sigma u$ for symmetric $\Sigma$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\Sigma u - 2\nu u &= 0 , \\
\Sigma u &= \nu u , \\
u^\top\Sigma u &= \nu\,u^\top u = \nu .
\end{aligned}
$$

Every stationary point is a unit eigenvector, the objective there is its eigenvalue, and comparing the candidates picks the largest eigenvalue as the maximum and the smallest as the minimum. The multiplier is the variance captured.

For $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the covariance of the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#covariance-as-geometry), the eigenvalues are 2.8 and 0.2. The direction $u = (0.832, 0.555)$ captures variance 2.8 of the total 3, and a search over 100,001 unit vectors finds the same maximum. The [dimensionality reduction note](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#pca-as-variance-maximization) builds PCA on it.

## Where it breaks: candidates that are not minima

Without convexity the conditions list stationary points, and some of them are neither minima nor maxima. In three dimensions the maximum-variance problem shows it. Take $\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(3, 2, 1)$ with eigenvectors $q_1, q_2, q_3$, the coordinate axes. All three are KKT points, with $\nu = 3, 2, 1$. At $u = q_2$ the variance is 2, yet turning $u$ by $0.1$ radians towards $q_1$ raises it to $2.01$, and turning it towards $q_3$ lowers it to $1.99$. The middle eigenvector is a saddle of the variance on the sphere.

A **second-order test** sorts the candidates. For equality constraints, look at the Hessian of the Lagrangian in $x$ along the tangent directions $v$, those with $\nabla h_j(x^*)^\top v = 0$. If $v^\top \nabla^2_{xx}\mathcal L\, v \gt 0$ for every nonzero tangent $v$, the KKT point is a strict local minimum; if it is negative for every such $v$, a strict local maximum ([Merkulov, 2024](#references), lecture 6). The Hessian of $f$ alone is the wrong matrix, because the curvature of the constraint surface enters through the multiplier terms.

Here $\nabla^2_{uu}\mathcal L = 2(\Sigma - \nu I)$, and at $u = q_2$, $\nu = 2$, it gives $q_1^\top(2\Sigma - 4I)q_1 = 2$ and $q_3^\top(2\Sigma - 4I)q_3 = -2$: mixed signs, a saddle. At $q_1$ both tangent directions give negative values, a strict maximum. For inequality constraints the same test runs over the critical cone: directions tangent to the active constraints with positive multipliers that do not violate the active constraints whose multipliers are zero.

The first-order conditions can also miss the answer. Minimize $x$ subject to $x^2 \le 0$. The only feasible point, $x = 0$, is the solution, but there $\nabla g = 0$, and $1 + \lambda \cdot 0 = 0$ has no solution. The constraint qualification fails: the constraint's gradient vanishes, so no multiple of it can balance $\nabla f$.

Even when everything is regular, the KKT system is hard to solve in general, because nobody knows in advance which constraints are active. Guessing the active set is the combinatorial core of constrained optimization; for the box and the simplex above it is trivial, but a projection onto a general polyhedron is already a quadratic program.

## What you should remember

- With equality constraints, $\nabla f + \sum_j\nu_j\nabla h_j = 0$ at a minimum: the Lagrangian is stationary.
- The multiplier is the price of the constraint: $df^*/du = -\nu$ for $h = u$.
- KKT: stationarity, feasibility, $\lambda_i \ge 0$, $\lambda_i g_i = 0$; necessary under a constraint qualification, sufficient for convex problems.
- Projection on a box clips; on the simplex it is $\max(v_i - \tau, 0)$.
- Maximum entropy with a fixed mean gives $p_i \propto e^{-\beta x_i}$; maximum variance on the sphere gives the top eigenvector.
- Without convexity, check the Hessian of the Lagrangian on tangent directions.

## Code

The opening problem as a linear system, the plane, maximum entropy, the two KKT cases of the figure, the box and simplex projections checked against SciPy's SLSQP solver, the maximum-variance direction, and the saddle among its KKT points. Exact digits can differ slightly across platforms and library versions.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq, minimize

# min x1^2 + 2 x2^2 s.t. x1 + x2 = 1: the stationarity equations of the Lagrangian are linear
K = np.array([[2., 0, 1], [0, 4, 1], [1, 1, 0]])          # 2 x1 + nu = 0, 4 x2 + nu = 0, x1 + x2 = 1
x1, x2, nu = np.linalg.solve(K, [0, 0, 1])
print(round(x1, 4), round(x2, 4), round(nu, 4), round(x1**2 + 2 * x2**2, 4))
# 0.6667 0.3333 -1.3333 0.6667
fstar = lambda s: np.linalg.solve(K, [0, 0, s])[:2] @ np.diag([1., 2]) @ np.linalg.solve(K, [0, 0, s])[:2]
print(round((fstar(1 + 1e-6) - fstar(1 - 1e-6)) / 2e-6, 4))   # d f*/ds = -nu
# 1.3333

# the nearest point on the plane a.x = b to p
a, b, p = np.array([1., 2, 2]), 3.0, np.array([1., 1, 1])
nu = 2 * (a @ p - b) / (a @ a); x = p - nu / 2 * a
print(x.round(4), round(a @ x, 10), round(np.linalg.norm(x - p), 4), round(nu, 4))
pst = lambda b: (a @ p - b) ** 2 / (a @ a)                 # the optimal value as a function of b
print(round((pst(b + 1e-6) - pst(b - 1e-6)) / 2e-6, 4))
# [0.7778 0.5556 0.5556] 3.0 0.6667 0.4444
# -0.4444

# maximum entropy on {1, ..., 6} with mean 4.5: p_i proportional to exp(-beta i)
k = np.arange(1, 7)
mean = lambda beta: k @ np.exp(-beta * k) / np.exp(-beta * k).sum()
beta = brentq(lambda t: mean(t) - 4.5, -5, 5)
pm = np.exp(-beta * k); pm /= pm.sum()
res = minimize(lambda q: q @ np.log(q), np.ones(6) / 6, method='SLSQP', bounds=[(1e-9, 1)] * 6,
               constraints=[{'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda q: q.sum() - 1}, {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda q: q @ k - 4.5}],
               options={'ftol': 1e-14, 'maxiter': 500})
print(round(beta, 4), pm.round(4), round(-pm @ np.log(pm), 4), round(np.log(6), 4), np.abs(res.x - pm).max() < 1e-5)
print(round(np.exp(-beta), 3))                              # the ratio of neighbouring probabilities
# -0.371 [0.0544 0.0788 0.1142 0.1654 0.2398 0.3475] 1.6136 1.7918 True
# 1.449

# KKT with active and inactive constraints: min |x - c|^2 s.t. x1 + x2 <= 2, x1 >= 0, x2 >= 0
for c in (np.array([2., 1]), np.array([0.8, 0.6])):
    r = minimize(lambda x: (x - c) @ (x - c), np.zeros(2), method='SLSQP', bounds=[(0, None)] * 2,
                 constraints=[{'type': 'ineq', 'fun': lambda x: 2 - x.sum()}], options={'ftol': 1e-14})
    xs = r.x; lam1 = -2 * (xs - c)[0] if abs(xs.sum() - 2) < 1e-9 else 0.0   # stationarity: 2(x - c) + lam1 (1, 1) = 0
    print(c, xs.round(4), round(lam1, 4), abs(round(lam1 * (xs.sum() - 2), 12)))
# [2. 1.] [1.5 0.5] 1.0 0.0
# [0.8 0.6] [0.8 0.6] 0.0 0.0

# projection onto a box is clipping; the multiplier of a bound is the distance clipped away
v = np.array([1.7, -0.4, 0.3]); xb = np.clip(v, 0, 1)
print(xb, np.maximum(v - 1, 0), np.maximum(-v, 0))         # x, multipliers of x <= 1, multipliers of x >= 0
# [1.  0.  0.3] [0.7 0.  0. ] [0.  0.4 0. ]

# projection onto the probability simplex: x = max(v - tau, 0), tau from the sorted values
def proj_simplex(v):
    u = np.sort(v)[::-1]; css = np.cumsum(u) - 1
    rho = np.nonzero(u - css / np.arange(1, len(v) + 1) > 0)[0][-1]
    tau = css[rho] / (rho + 1)
    return np.maximum(v - tau, 0), tau
v = np.array([0.5, 1.2, -0.3]); xs, tau = proj_simplex(v)
lam = xs - v + tau                                          # stationarity of 1/2|x - v|^2 + tau (sum x - 1) - lam.x
r = minimize(lambda x: 0.5 * (x - v) @ (x - v), np.ones(3) / 3, method='SLSQP', bounds=[(0, None)] * 3,
             constraints=[{'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda x: x.sum() - 1}], options={'ftol': 1e-14})
print(xs, tau, lam.round(4), lam @ xs, np.abs(r.x - xs).max() < 1e-6)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                              # 1,000 random vectors against SLSQP
print(max(np.abs(proj_simplex(w)[0] - minimize(lambda x: 0.5 * (x - w) @ (x - w), np.ones(5) / 5, method='SLSQP', bounds=[(0, None)] * 5,
      constraints=[{'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda x: x.sum() - 1}], options={'ftol': 1e-14}).x).max() for w in rng.normal(size=(1000, 5))) < 1e-6)
# [0.15 0.85 0.  ] 0.35 [0.   0.   0.65] 0.0 True
# True

# the direction of maximum variance: stationary points of u' S u on the unit circle are eigenvectors
S = np.array([[2., 1.2], [1.2, 1]])
ang = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 100_001); U = np.stack([np.cos(ang), np.sin(ang)])
var = np.einsum('in,ij,jn->n', U, S, U)
w, V = np.linalg.eigh(S)
print(w, np.abs(V[:, 1]).round(3), round(var.max(), 6), round(var.min(), 6), U[:, var.argmax()].round(3))
# [0.2 2.8] [0.832 0.555] 2.8 0.2 [0.832 0.555]

# in three dimensions the middle eigenvector is a KKT point but a saddle: S = diag(3, 2, 1)
S = np.diag([3., 2, 1]); q1, q2, q3 = np.eye(3)
turn = lambda q, s: (np.cos(s) * q2 + np.sin(s) * q) @ S @ (np.cos(s) * q2 + np.sin(s) * q)   # variance after turning q2 by s
H = 2 * (S - 2 * np.eye(3))                                 # Hessian of the Lagrangian at u = q2, nu = 2
print(round(turn(q1, 0.1), 4), round(turn(q3, 0.1), 4), q1 @ H @ q1, q3 @ H @ q3)
# 2.01 1.99 2.0 -2.0
```

## Next

The sufficiency proof compared $f(x)$ with the Lagrangian, which on the feasible set is never larger than the objective. The [next note](/notes/math/duality) makes that bound the main object. Minimizing the Lagrangian over $x$ for fixed multipliers gives a function of the multipliers alone, the dual function, and its maximum is the best such lower bound. For convex problems it equals the optimum, which turns the SVM into a problem over its multipliers and gives every solver a certificate of how far it is from optimal.
