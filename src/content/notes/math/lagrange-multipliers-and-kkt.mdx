The [previous note](/notes/math/unconstrained-optimization) minimized functions over all of $\mathbb R^n$, where a minimum has a zero gradient. Many problems in machine learning restrict the variables. A probability vector must lie on the simplex, the first principal component is the best direction among unit vectors, and the SVM requires every training point to lie on the correct side of its margin. At a constrained minimum the gradient need not vanish: the function may keep decreasing, but only in directions the constraints forbid. This note turns that observation into equations: the Lagrange conditions for equality constraints and the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions for inequalities.

**Notation.** The problem is

$$
\min_{x \in \mathbb R^n} f(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad g_i(x) \le 0,\ i = 1, \dots, m, \qquad h_j(x) = 0,\ j = 1, \dots, p ,
$$

with differentiable $f$, $g_i$ and $h_j$. A point that satisfies all constraints is **feasible**. Each inequality gets a multiplier $\lambda_i$ and each equality a multiplier $\nu_j$, collected in $\lambda \in \mathbb R^m$ and $\nu \in \mathbb R^p$. An inequality is **active** at $x$ if $g_i(x) = 0$ and **inactive** if $g_i(x) \lt 0$.

## Baseline: eliminate the constraint

Minimize $f(x) = x_1^2 + 2x_2^2$ subject to $x_1 + x_2 = 1$. The constraint can be solved for one variable, $x_1 = 1 - x_2$, which leaves an unconstrained problem in one variable:

$$
\varphi(x_2) = (1 - x_2)^2 + 2x_2^2 , \qquad \varphi'(x_2) = -2(1 - x_2) + 4x_2 = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ x_2 = \tfrac13,\ x_1 = \tfrac23,\ f = \tfrac23 .
$$

Elimination works when the constraint can be solved explicitly and globally. On the unit circle $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 1$ it gives two branches $x_1 = \pm\sqrt{1 - x_2^2}$ with infinite slopes at their ends. With the inequalities $x_i \ge 0$ of the simplex it turns into a case split over which coordinates are zero, $2^n$ cases in $n$ dimensions. The SVM has one inequality per training point. A method is needed that keeps all the variables and treats every constraint the same way.

## Equality constraints: the gradient is parallel to the normal

<LagrangeGeometry />

The idea: at a constrained minimum, no direction that stays on the constraint surface can decrease $f$ to first order, so the gradient of $f$ is perpendicular to the surface, and so is the gradient of the constraint. Two vectors perpendicular to the same surface of dimension $n - 1$ are parallel. For one constraint $h(x) = 0$ with $\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0$, a local minimum $x^*$ satisfies

$$
\boxed{\; \nabla f(x^*) + \nu\,\nabla h(x^*) = 0 , \qquad h(x^*) = 0 \quad \text{for some } \nu \in \mathbb R . \;}
$$

Why. Take any smooth curve $x(t)$ on the surface with $x(0) = x^*$ and velocity $x'(0) = v$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
h(x(t)) = 0 \text{ for all } t \ &\Longrightarrow\ \nabla h(x^*)^\top v = 0 && \text{chain rule at } t = 0 \\
f(x(t)) \ge f(x^*) \text{ near } t = 0 \ &\Longrightarrow\ \nabla f(x^*)^\top v = 0 && \text{a minimum in one variable } t \text{ has zero derivative} \\
\{v : \nabla h^\top v = 0\} &\subseteq \{v : \nabla f^\top v = 0\} && \text{every such } v \text{ is the velocity of some curve on the surface} \\
\nabla f(x^*) &= -\nu\,\nabla h(x^*) && \text{only multiples of } \nabla h \text{ are orthogonal to all of } \nabla h^\perp .
\end{aligned}
$$

The third line is where $\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0$ is needed: by the implicit function theorem the surface is then smooth near $x^*$, and every direction orthogonal to $\nabla h$ is tangent to it. With several constraints $h_1, \dots, h_p$ the same argument, with the tangent directions $\{v : \nabla h_j^\top v = 0 \text{ for all } j\}$, gives $\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_j \nu_j\nabla h_j(x^*) = 0$, provided the gradients $\nabla h_j(x^*)$ are linearly independent. This proviso is a **constraint qualification**; without it the multipliers may not exist.

**Worked example.** For the baseline, $\nabla f = (2x_1, 4x_2)$ and $\nabla h = (1, 1)$. At $x^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)$, $\nabla f = (\tfrac43, \tfrac43)$, parallel to $(1, 1)$, with $\nu = -\tfrac43$. At the feasible point $(0, 1)$ instead, $\nabla f = (0, 4)$ is not parallel to $(1, 1)$. Its component along the line, in the unit direction $v = (1, -1)/\sqrt2$, is $\nabla f^\top v = -4/\sqrt2 = -2.83$, so moving along the line towards larger $x_1$ lowers $f$: the point is not a minimum. In the figure, the level set of $f$ through $x^*$ touches the line; through $(0, 1)$ it crosses it.

## The Lagrangian

The conditions are the stationarity conditions of one function. The **Lagrangian** adds each constraint to the objective with its multiplier:

$$
\mathcal L(x, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_{j=1}^p \nu_j h_j(x) .
$$

Its gradient in $x$ is $\nabla f + \sum_j \nu_j\nabla h_j$, and its gradient in $\nu$ is the vector of constraint values $h_j(x)$. Setting both to zero gives the boxed conditions: a constrained problem in $n$ variables becomes $n + p$ equations in $n + p$ unknowns, with no constraints left. For the baseline they are $2x_1 + \nu = 0$, $4x_2 + \nu = 0$ and $x_1 + x_2 = 1$, a linear system with the solution $(\tfrac23, \tfrac13, -\tfrac43)$. The point $(x^*, \nu^*)$ is a stationary point of $\mathcal L$, not a minimum: $\mathcal L$ is linear in $\nu$ and unbounded in it. Like a zero gradient, the conditions give candidates; the [duality note](/notes/math/duality) explains when they give the answer.

### The nearest point on a plane

The idea: the shortest path from a point to a plane is along the plane's normal, so the nearest point is $p$ moved along $a$ until the constraint holds. For the plane $a^\top x = b$ and a point $p$,

$$
\boxed{\; x^* = p - \frac{a^\top p - b}{\lVert a\rVert^2}\,a , \qquad \lVert x^* - p\rVert = \frac{\lvert a^\top p - b\rvert}{\lVert a\rVert} . \;}
$$

Minimize $\lVert x - p\rVert^2$ with the Lagrangian $\lVert x - p\rVert^2 + \nu(a^\top x - b)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2(x - p) + \nu\,a &= 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ x = p - \tfrac\nu2\,a && \text{stationarity in } x \\
a^\top p - \tfrac\nu2\lVert a\rVert^2 &= b \ \Longrightarrow\ \nu = \frac{2(a^\top p - b)}{\lVert a\rVert^2} && \text{substitute into the constraint} \\
x^* &= p - \frac{a^\top p - b}{\lVert a\rVert^2}\,a && \text{put } \nu \text{ back.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The objective is convex and the constraint affine, so by the sufficiency result below this stationary point is the global minimum. The distance formula is the one the [SVM note](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines#which-separating-line) uses for the margin of a hyperplane. **Numbers:** $a = (1, 2, 2)$, $b = 3$, $p = (1, 1, 1)$. Then $a^\top p = 5$, $\lVert a\rVert^2 = 9$, $\nu = \tfrac49$, $x^* = (1, 1, 1) - \tfrac29(1, 2, 2) = (\tfrac79, \tfrac59, \tfrac59)$, and indeed $\tfrac79 + \tfrac{10}9 + \tfrac{10}9 = 3$. The distance is $\tfrac23$.

### Maximum entropy

The idea: among all distributions with a given mean, the one with the largest entropy spreads its probability as evenly as the constraint allows, and the Lagrange conditions show that the result is always an exponential of the constrained quantity. Maximize the entropy $H(p) = -\sum_i p_i\log p_i$ of a distribution on values $x_1, \dots, x_k$ subject to $\sum_i p_i = 1$ and $\sum_i p_i x_i = m$:

$$
\boxed{\; p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta x_i}}{\sum_j e^{-\beta x_j}} , \qquad \beta \text{ chosen so that } \textstyle\sum_i p_i x_i = m . \;}
$$

Minimize $-H$ with multipliers $\alpha$ for the normalization and $\beta$ for the mean:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}\Big(\sum_r p_r\log p_r + \alpha\big(\textstyle\sum_r p_r - 1\big) + \beta\big(\textstyle\sum_r p_r x_r - m\big)\Big) &= \log p_i + 1 + \alpha + \beta x_i = 0 && \text{stationarity in } p_i \\
p_i &= e^{-1 - \alpha}\, e^{-\beta x_i} && \text{solve for } p_i \\
p_i &= \frac{e^{-\beta x_i}}{\sum_j e^{-\beta x_j}} && \textstyle\sum_i p_i = 1 \text{ fixes } e^{-1-\alpha} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Every $p_i$ is positive, so the omitted constraints $p_i \ge 0$ hold automatically, and $\sum_i p_i\log p_i$ is convex, so the stationary point is the maximum of the entropy. Without the mean constraint ($\beta = 0$) the answer is the uniform distribution. **Numbers:** a die with faces $1, \dots, 6$ constrained to the mean $4.5$ gives $\beta = -0.371$ and $p = (0.054, 0.079, 0.114, 0.165, 0.240, 0.347)$, with entropy $1.614$ nats against $\log 6 = 1.792$ for the fair die. The boxed form is the softmax of the scores $-\beta x_i$: the softmax is the maximum-entropy distribution with prescribed expected scores, and the [exponential families](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#the-exponential-family) of statistics arise the same way. With the entropy replaced by minus a KL divergence to a reference distribution, and the reward moved from a constraint into the objective, the same steps give the optimum of [KL-regularized reinforcement learning](/notes/agents/rl-for-language-models#the-kl-regularized-objective-and-its-optimum): the reference reweighted by an exponential of the reward. Entropy itself is the subject of the [entropy note](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#entropy-expected-surprise).

### What the multiplier measures

The multiplier is the price of the constraint. If the constraint is moved to $h(x) = u$, the optimal value $f^*(u)$ changes at the rate

$$
\boxed{\; \frac{d f^*}{du} = -\nu^* . \;}
$$

Assume the solution $x^*(u)$ depends smoothly on $u$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d f^*}{du} = \nabla f(x^*)^\top\frac{d x^*}{du} &= -\nu^*\,\nabla h(x^*)^\top\frac{d x^*}{du} && \text{chain rule, then stationarity } \nabla f = -\nu\nabla h \\
&= -\nu^*\,\frac{d}{du}h\big(x^*(u)\big) = -\nu^* && \text{chain rule backwards; } h(x^*(u)) = u \text{ has derivative } 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

**Numbers.** For the plane, $f^*(b) = (a^\top p - b)^2/\lVert a\rVert^2$, whose derivative at $b = 3$ is $-2 \cdot 2/9 = -\tfrac49 = -\nu$; here $h(x) = a^\top x - b$, so moving $b$ is moving $u$. For the baseline, $f^*(s) = \tfrac23 s^2$ on the line $x_1 + x_2 = s$, with derivative $\tfrac43 = -\nu$ at $s = 1$. In economics the multiplier is called a shadow price; in the SVM, it measures how much the optimal objective would decrease if the margin required of one training point were relaxed a little.

## Inequality constraints: active or inactive

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> At a constrained minimum the gradient of the objective is balanced by the gradients of the constraints that bind, with multipliers that price each constraint. An inequality either binds, and then acts like an equality whose multiplier must be non-negative, or does not, and then its multiplier is zero. Complementary slackness, $\lambda_i g_i(x^*) = 0$, writes both cases as one equation.</aside>

<KktActiveInactive />

For a constraint $g(x) \le 0$ there are two cases at a local minimum $x^*$:

- **Inactive**, $g(x^*) \lt 0$. Every small move keeps the point feasible, so $x^*$ is an unconstrained local minimum and $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$. The constraint plays no role; give it the multiplier $\lambda = 0$.
- **Active**, $g(x^*) = 0$. Locally the constraint acts like the equality $g = 0$, so $\nabla f + \lambda\nabla g = 0$ for some $\lambda$, and now the sign matters: $\lambda \ge 0$. If $\lambda$ were negative, $-\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ would point into the feasible region, where $g$ decreases, and a small step along it would lower $f$ without leaving the feasible set. With $\lambda \ge 0$ the descent direction $-\nabla f$ points outward, against the wall.

**Worked example in one variable.** Minimize $(x - 2)^2$ subject to $x \le 1$, that is $g(x) = x - 1 \le 0$. The unconstrained minimum $x = 2$ is infeasible, so the constraint is active, $x^* = 1$, and $f'(1) + \lambda g'(1) = -2 + \lambda = 0$ gives $\lambda = 2 \ge 0$. With the looser constraint $x \le 3$, the unconstrained minimum $x = 2$ is feasible, the constraint is inactive and $\lambda = 0$. The multiplier is again a price: moving the wall from 1 to $1 + u$ lowers the optimal value $(1 + u - 2)^2$ at the rate $2 = \lambda$.

**In the figure.** Both panels minimize $\lVert x - c\rVert^2$ over the triangle $x_1 + x_2 \le 2$, $x_1 \ge 0$, $x_2 \ge 0$, written as $g_1 = x_1 + x_2 - 2$, $g_2 = -x_1$ and $g_3 = -x_2$, all $\le 0$. For $c = (0.8, 0.6)$ inside the triangle the answer is $c$ itself, no constraint is active and $\lambda = 0$. For $c = (2, 1)$ outside it the answer is $x^* = (1.5, 0.5)$ on the slanted side, with $\nabla f(x^*) = 2(x^* - c) = (-1, -1)$ and $\nabla g_1 = (1, 1)$, so $\lambda_1 = 1$; $g_2$ and $g_3$ are inactive and $\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = 0$.

## The KKT conditions

The idea: combine the Lagrange condition for the equalities with the two cases for each inequality. A point $x^*$ with multipliers $\lambda, \nu$ satisfies the **Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions** if

$$
\boxed{\;
\begin{aligned}
&\nabla f(x^*) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i\nabla g_i(x^*) + \sum_j \nu_j\nabla h_j(x^*) = 0 && \text{stationarity} \\
&g_i(x^*) \le 0,\quad h_j(x^*) = 0 && \text{primal feasibility} \\
&\lambda_i \ge 0 && \text{dual feasibility} \\
&\lambda_i\, g_i(x^*) = 0 && \text{complementary slackness.}
\end{aligned}
\;}
$$

**Complementary slackness** encodes the case split. The product $\lambda_i g_i$ of a non-negative and a non-positive number is zero only if one factor is zero: either the constraint is active ($g_i = 0$) and its multiplier may be positive, or it is inactive and its multiplier is zero. A positive multiplier certifies that the constraint is tight; a slack constraint has no price. In the SVM, the training points with positive multipliers are exactly the support vectors, which lie on or inside the margin ([support vectors](/notes/ml/support-vector-machines#support-vectors-what-the-dual-variables-tell-you)). Both factors can vanish at once, for a constraint that touches the solution without pushing on it.

**When are they necessary?** At a local minimum the KKT conditions hold if a constraint qualification holds there, for example if the gradients of the active constraints are linearly independent (LICQ), or, for convex problems, if some point satisfies all inequalities strictly (**Slater's condition**, the subject of the [duality note](/notes/math/duality)). Without a qualification they can fail: minimizing $x$ subject to $x^2 \le 0$ has the solution $x = 0$, where $\nabla g = 0$ and $1 + \lambda \cdot 0 = 0$ has no solution.

**When are they sufficient?** For a **convex problem**, with $f$ and every $g_i$ convex and every $h_j$ affine, any point that satisfies the KKT conditions is a global minimum. For every feasible $x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x) &\ge f(x) + \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x) && \lambda_i \ge 0,\ g_i(x) \le 0,\ h_j(x) = 0 \\
&= \mathcal L(x, \lambda, \nu) && \text{definition of the Lagrangian} \\
&\ge \mathcal L(x^*, \lambda, \nu) && \mathcal L(\cdot, \lambda, \nu) \text{ is convex and stationary at } x^* \\
&= f(x^*) && \text{complementary slackness and } h_j(x^*) = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The third line uses the tangent bound of the [convexity note](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#the-first-order-condition-the-tangent-lies-below): a non-negative combination of convex functions plus affine terms is convex, and a convex function is minimized wherever its gradient vanishes. In the figure, the multipliers $\lambda = (1, 0, 0)$ and the point $(1.5, 0.5)$ satisfy all four conditions, so the point is the global minimum.

## Projection onto a box and onto the simplex

Many algorithms need the nearest point of a convex set $C$ to a given $v$, the **projection** $\Pi_C(v) = \arg\min_{x \in C}\tfrac12\lVert x - v\rVert^2$. Projected gradient descent alternates a gradient step with a projection, $x \leftarrow \Pi_C(x - \eta\nabla f(x))$, and so solves constrained problems with the machinery of the previous note. For two sets used constantly in machine learning, the KKT conditions give the projection in closed form.

**The box** $\{x : l_i \le x_i \le u_i\}$. The problem separates into coordinates. For one coordinate, with multipliers $\lambda_i^u$ for $x_i - u_i \le 0$ and $\lambda_i^l$ for $l_i - x_i \le 0$, stationarity reads $x_i - v_i + \lambda_i^u - \lambda_i^l = 0$. If $v_i \gt u_i$, the upper bound must be active (otherwise $x_i = v_i + \lambda_i^l \ge v_i$ would be infeasible), $x_i = u_i$ and $\lambda_i^u = v_i - u_i \gt 0$; symmetrically below. So

$$
\Pi_{\text{box}}(v)_i = \min\big(\max(v_i, l_i), u_i\big) ,
$$

clipping, with multipliers equal to the amounts clipped away. For $v = (1.7, -0.4, 0.3)$ and the unit box: $x^* = (1, 0, 0.3)$, $\lambda_1^u = 0.7$, $\lambda_2^l = 0.4$, all other multipliers zero. The soft-margin SVM dual is a problem over a box, $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$, and the [duality note](/notes/math/duality#the-duality-gap-as-a-stopping-criterion) solves it by projected gradient ascent with exactly this clipping.

**The probability simplex** $\Delta = \{x : x_i \ge 0, \sum_i x_i = 1\}$. The idea: subtract the same threshold $\tau$ from every coordinate and cut at zero, with $\tau$ chosen so that what remains sums to 1.

$$
\boxed{\; \Pi_\Delta(v)_i = \max(v_i - \tau, 0) , \qquad \tau \text{ such that } \textstyle\sum_i \max(v_i - \tau, 0) = 1 . \;}
$$

With multiplier $\tau$ for $\sum_i x_i = 1$ and $\lambda_i$ for $-x_i \le 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_i - v_i + \tau - \lambda_i &= 0 && \text{stationarity of } \tfrac12\lVert x - v\rVert^2 + \tau\big(\textstyle\sum_i x_i - 1\big) - \sum_i\lambda_i x_i \\
x_i \gt 0 \ &\Longrightarrow\ \lambda_i = 0,\ x_i = v_i - \tau && \text{complementary slackness} \\
x_i = 0 \ &\Longrightarrow\ \lambda_i = \tau - v_i \ge 0,\ \text{so } v_i \le \tau && \text{dual feasibility} \\
x_i &= \max(v_i - \tau, 0) && \text{the two cases together.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The left side of the condition on $\tau$ decreases continuously from large values to 0 as $\tau$ grows, so exactly one $\tau$ works. It is found by sorting $v$ in decreasing order, $u_1 \ge u_2 \ge \dots$, taking the largest $r$ with $u_r - \frac1r\big(\sum_{s \le r} u_s - 1\big) \gt 0$, and setting $\tau = \frac1r\big(\sum_{s \le r}u_s - 1\big)$, in $O(n\log n)$ operations ([Duchi et al., 2008](#references)). **Numbers:** for $v = (0.5, 1.2, -0.3)$ the sorted values are $1.2, 0.5, -0.3$; with $r = 1$, $\tau = 0.2$ and $1.2 - 0.2 \gt 0$; with $r = 2$, $\tau = (1.7 - 1)/2 = 0.35$ and $0.5 - 0.35 \gt 0$; with $r = 3$, $\tau = (1.4 - 1)/3 = 0.133$ and $-0.3 - 0.133 \lt 0$. So $\tau = 0.35$, $\Pi_\Delta(v) = (0.15, 0.85, 0)$ and $\lambda_3 = 0.35 + 0.3 = 0.65$. Applied to the logits of a classifier, this projection is **sparsemax** ([Martins and Astudillo, 2016](#references)), an alternative to the softmax that assigns exactly zero probability to low-scoring classes; the softmax was the maximum-entropy answer above, sparsemax is the nearest-point answer.

## The direction of maximum variance is an eigenvector

The [symmetric matrices note](/notes/math/symmetric-matrices#the-rayleigh-quotient-the-extreme-directions) bounds the Rayleigh quotient using the spectral theorem. The Lagrange conditions reach the same answer without it and explain why only eigenvectors can be candidates. For a covariance matrix $\Sigma$, the variance of the data projected on a unit vector $u$ is $u^\top\Sigma u$; PCA maximizes it subject to $u^\top u = 1$. With the Lagrangian $u^\top\Sigma u - \nu(u^\top u - 1)$,

$$
\boxed{\; \Sigma\,u = \nu\,u , \qquad u^\top\Sigma u = \nu . \;}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{u}\big(u^\top\Sigma u - \nu(u^\top u - 1)\big) &= 2\Sigma u - 2\nu u = 0 && \nabla(u^\top\Sigma u) = 2\Sigma u \text{ for symmetric } \Sigma \\
\Sigma u &= \nu u && \text{every stationary point is a unit eigenvector} \\
u^\top\Sigma u &= \nu\,u^\top u = \nu && \text{multiply by } u^\top \text{, use the constraint.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The candidates are the unit eigenvectors, the objective at each one is its eigenvalue, and comparing the candidates picks the largest eigenvalue as the maximum and the smallest as the minimum. The multiplier is the variance captured. **Numbers:** for $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, the covariance of the [multivariate Gaussian note](/notes/probability/multivariate-gaussian#covariance-as-geometry), the eigenvalues are 2.8 and 0.2; the direction $u = (0.832, 0.555)$ captures variance 2.8 of the total 3, and a search over 100,001 unit vectors finds the same maximum. The [dimensionality reduction note](/notes/ml/dimensionality-reduction#pca-as-variance-maximization) builds PCA on it.

## What changed

| | Unconstrained | Equality constraints | Inequality constraints |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| At a minimum | $\nabla f = 0$ | $\nabla f + \sum_j \nu_j\nabla h_j = 0$ | KKT: stationarity, feasibility, $\lambda_i \ge 0$, $\lambda_i g_i = 0$ |
| Multipliers | none | any sign | non-negative, zero when inactive |
| Unknowns | $n$ | $n + p$ | $n + m + p$, plus which constraints are active |
| Sufficient when | $f$ convex | $f$ convex, $h_j$ affine | $f$, $g_i$ convex, $h_j$ affine |
| Meaning of multiplier | | $-df^*/du$ for $h_j = u$ | the same, and zero for a slack constraint |

## Limitations

- **Candidates, not answers.** Without convexity the conditions list stationary points, including maxima and saddles, and the candidates must be compared.
- **A constraint qualification is needed** for the conditions to be necessary; at degenerate points (cusps, tangent constraints) the multipliers may not exist.
- **Solving the KKT system is hard in general.** The active set is unknown, and guessing it is the combinatorial core of constrained optimization; interior-point and active-set methods are the standard algorithms ([Nocedal and Wright, 2006](#references), Chapters 16 and 19).
- **Projections are cheap only for simple sets.** Boxes, balls and simplices have closed forms; a general polyhedron needs a quadratic program per projection.

## What you should remember

- At a constrained minimum with equality constraints the gradient of $f$ is a combination of the constraint gradients: $\nabla f + \sum_j\nu_j\nabla h_j = 0$, the stationarity of the Lagrangian $\mathcal L = f + \sum_j\nu_j h_j$.
- The multiplier is the sensitivity of the optimal value: $df^*/du = -\nu$ for the constraint $h = u$.
- KKT: stationarity, primal feasibility, $\lambda_i \ge 0$, and complementary slackness $\lambda_i g_i = 0$; necessary under a constraint qualification, sufficient for convex problems.
- An active inequality acts like an equality with a non-negative multiplier; an inactive one has multiplier zero.
- Projection onto a box is clipping; onto the simplex it is $\max(v_i - \tau, 0)$ with a threshold found by sorting.
- Maximum entropy under a mean constraint gives the softmax form $p_i \propto e^{-\beta x_i}$; maximum variance on the unit sphere gives the top eigenvector, with the variance as multiplier.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq, minimize

# min x1^2 + 2 x2^2 s.t. x1 + x2 = 1: the stationarity equations of the Lagrangian are linear
K = np.array([[2., 0, 1], [0, 4, 1], [1, 1, 0]])          # 2 x1 + nu = 0, 4 x2 + nu = 0, x1 + x2 = 1
x1, x2, nu = np.linalg.solve(K, [0, 0, 1])
print(round(x1, 4), round(x2, 4), round(nu, 4), round(x1**2 + 2 * x2**2, 4))
# 0.6667 0.3333 -1.3333 0.6667
fstar = lambda s: np.linalg.solve(K, [0, 0, s])[:2] @ np.diag([1., 2]) @ np.linalg.solve(K, [0, 0, s])[:2]
print(round((fstar(1 + 1e-6) - fstar(1 - 1e-6)) / 2e-6, 4))   # d f*/ds = -nu
# 1.3333

# the nearest point on the plane a.x = b to p
a, b, p = np.array([1., 2, 2]), 3.0, np.array([1., 1, 1])
nu = 2 * (a @ p - b) / (a @ a); x = p - nu / 2 * a
print(x.round(4), round(a @ x, 10), round(np.linalg.norm(x - p), 4), round(nu, 4))
pst = lambda b: (a @ p - b) ** 2 / (a @ a)                 # the optimal value as a function of b
print(round((pst(b + 1e-6) - pst(b - 1e-6)) / 2e-6, 4))
# [0.7778 0.5556 0.5556] 3.0 0.6667 0.4444
# -0.4444

# maximum entropy on {1, ..., 6} with mean 4.5: p_i proportional to exp(-beta i)
k = np.arange(1, 7)
mean = lambda beta: k @ np.exp(-beta * k) / np.exp(-beta * k).sum()
beta = brentq(lambda t: mean(t) - 4.5, -5, 5)
pm = np.exp(-beta * k); pm /= pm.sum()
res = minimize(lambda q: q @ np.log(q), np.ones(6) / 6, method='SLSQP', bounds=[(1e-9, 1)] * 6,
               constraints=[{'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda q: q.sum() - 1}, {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda q: q @ k - 4.5}],
               options={'ftol': 1e-14, 'maxiter': 500})
print(round(beta, 4), pm.round(4), round(-pm @ np.log(pm), 4), round(np.log(6), 4), np.abs(res.x - pm).max() < 1e-5)
# -0.371 [0.0544 0.0788 0.1142 0.1654 0.2398 0.3475] 1.6136 1.7918 True

# KKT with active and inactive constraints: min |x - c|^2 s.t. x1 + x2 <= 2, x1 >= 0, x2 >= 0
for c in (np.array([2., 1]), np.array([0.8, 0.6])):
    r = minimize(lambda x: (x - c) @ (x - c), np.zeros(2), method='SLSQP', bounds=[(0, None)] * 2,
                 constraints=[{'type': 'ineq', 'fun': lambda x: 2 - x.sum()}], options={'ftol': 1e-14})
    xs = r.x; lam1 = -2 * (xs - c)[0] if abs(xs.sum() - 2) < 1e-9 else 0.0   # stationarity: 2(x - c) + lam1 (1, 1) = 0
    print(c, xs.round(4), round(lam1, 4), abs(round(lam1 * (xs.sum() - 2), 12)))
# [2. 1.] [1.5 0.5] 1.0 0.0
# [0.8 0.6] [0.8 0.6] 0.0 0.0

# projection onto a box is clipping; the multiplier of a bound is the distance clipped away
v = np.array([1.7, -0.4, 0.3]); xb = np.clip(v, 0, 1)
print(xb, np.maximum(v - 1, 0), np.maximum(-v, 0))         # x, multipliers of x <= 1, multipliers of x >= 0
# [1.  0.  0.3] [0.7 0.  0. ] [0.  0.4 0. ]

# projection onto the probability simplex: x = max(v - tau, 0), tau from the sorted values
def proj_simplex(v):
    u = np.sort(v)[::-1]; css = np.cumsum(u) - 1
    rho = np.nonzero(u - css / np.arange(1, len(v) + 1) > 0)[0][-1]
    tau = css[rho] / (rho + 1)
    return np.maximum(v - tau, 0), tau
v = np.array([0.5, 1.2, -0.3]); xs, tau = proj_simplex(v)
lam = xs - v + tau                                          # stationarity of 1/2|x - v|^2 + tau (sum x - 1) - lam.x
r = minimize(lambda x: 0.5 * (x - v) @ (x - v), np.ones(3) / 3, method='SLSQP', bounds=[(0, None)] * 3,
             constraints=[{'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda x: x.sum() - 1}], options={'ftol': 1e-14})
print(xs, tau, lam.round(4), lam @ xs, np.abs(r.x - xs).max() < 1e-6)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                              # 1,000 random vectors against SLSQP
print(max(np.abs(proj_simplex(w)[0] - minimize(lambda x: 0.5 * (x - w) @ (x - w), np.ones(5) / 5, method='SLSQP', bounds=[(0, None)] * 5,
      constraints=[{'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda x: x.sum() - 1}], options={'ftol': 1e-14}).x).max() for w in rng.normal(size=(1000, 5))) < 1e-6)
# [0.15 0.85 0.  ] 0.35 [0.   0.   0.65] 0.0 True
# True

# the direction of maximum variance: stationary points of u' S u on the unit circle are eigenvectors
S = np.array([[2., 1.2], [1.2, 1]])
ang = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 100_001); U = np.stack([np.cos(ang), np.sin(ang)])
var = np.einsum('in,ij,jn->n', U, S, U)
w, V = np.linalg.eigh(S)
print(w, np.abs(V[:, 1]).round(3), round(var.max(), 6), round(var.min(), 6), U[:, var.argmax()].round(3))
# [0.2 2.8] [0.832 0.555] 2.8 0.2 [0.832 0.555]
```

## Next

The KKT conditions came with a sufficiency proof that compared $f(x)$ with the Lagrangian, a lower bound on the objective over the feasible set. The [next note](/notes/math/duality) makes that bound the main object. Minimizing the Lagrangian over $x$ for fixed multipliers gives a function of the multipliers alone, the dual function, whose maximum is the best such lower bound. For convex problems it equals the optimum, which turns the SVM into a problem over its multipliers and gives every solver a certificate of how far it is from optimal.
