Two candidates pass eight of twelve checks. One fails every case involving a negative number. The other handles those cases but makes more mistakes elsewhere. Their shared score hides a decision about which requests matter. Evaluation starts by defining that decision and the evidence that could change it.

## Define what a benchmark score averages \{#task-level-benchmarks\}

An evaluation protocol fixes the target task, input population, reference information, allowed output and grading rule. It also fixes the checkpoint, prompt template, decoding configuration and resource budget. A score is a result of that protocol. HELM separates scenarios from metrics and deliberately measures several properties within a scenario ([Liang et al., 2023](#references), §1.1). This exposes trade-offs that a single aggregate cannot identify.

For a binary check, let $a_i$ be one when candidate A passes item $i$ and zero otherwise. On $n$ equally weighted items, define its observed pass rate:

$$
\widehat p_A=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n a_i.
$$

The formula describes the evaluated sample. Generalizing to future requests requires a sampling argument. A public benchmark may differ from deployment in language, document length, task definition or available context. Its provenance and contamination checks also matter: excluding an item from fine-tuning does not establish its absence from pretraining.

**Worked example.** Define a new task: given two integers, emit one JSON object with exactly one key, `sum`, whose value is their integer sum. Explanatory prose, a bare integer, a string-valued sum and extra fields fail the format rule. We manually specify the following candidate outputs; they are test fixtures rather than model generations.

| Item | Integers | A output | B output |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1, 4 | `{"sum":5}` | `{"sum":5}` |
| 2 | 2, 5 | `{"sum":7}` | `{"sum":7}` |
| 3 | 3, 1 | `{"sum":4}` | `{"sum":4}` |
| 4 | 4, 6 | `{"sum":10}` | `{"sum":10}` |
| 5 | 5, 2 | `{"sum":7}` | `{"sum":7}` |
| 6 | 6, 3 | `{"sum":9}` | `The sum is 9.` |
| 7 | 2, 8 | `{"sum":10}` | `{"sum":9}` |
| 8 | 7, 4 | `{"sum":11}` | `{"sum":10}` |
| 9 | 9, 2 | `11` | `{"sum":11}` |
| 10 | −3, 7 | `{"sum":10}` | `{"sum":4}` |
| 11 | 8, −5 | `{"sum":13}` | `{"sum":3}` |
| 12 | −4, −2 | `{"sum":6}` | `{"sum":"-6"}` |

A satisfies the format on eleven items and the complete task on eight. B satisfies the format on ten items and the complete task on eight. Formatting and arithmetic locate different defects. Treating every failure as one undifferentiated error would hide what to improve.

Group the first nine items as nonnegative and the last three as signed. The group rates are:

| Candidate | Nonnegative | Signed | All items | Equal-group mean |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A | 8/9 | 0/3 | 8/12 | 4/9 |
| B | 6/9 | 2/3 | 8/12 | 2/3 |

The all-item mean weights groups by their sample sizes. An equal-group mean gives each group half the total weight. Choose weights to match the evaluation question before seeing the results. Reweighting after inspecting failures can be useful for diagnosis, but it is a new reported estimand.

A comparison should preserve item pairing. Define $d_i=a_i-b_i$ and the mean difference:

$$
\widehat\delta=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n d_i.
$$

Our fixture has five shared passes, three A-only passes, three B-only passes and one shared failure. Thus six items contribute zero and six contribute either $+1$ or $-1$. The observed difference is zero. Comparing two unpaired lists would discard which mistakes coincide.

For an exact illustrative randomization calculation, assume the candidate labels are exchangeable independently within each item under the null. Each of the six discordant pairs can have its sign swapped, giving 64 equally weighted assignments. Enumerating them produces the distribution below. This is a null assumption, not an automatic consequence of using the same test set. Related requests may need to be kept together as one sampling unit ([Dror et al., 2018](#references), §§3.2, 5).

<PairedSigns />

The two-sided tail proportion at the observed zero difference is one. It does not establish equivalence of the systems, and says nothing about the unequal group rates. A paired bootstrap would instead resample complete item pairs with replacement to approximate sampling uncertainty. Its validity depends on how those units represent the target population; small or dependent samples remain a limitation. Report the paired effect and error breakdown alongside any uncertainty calculation.

A task with multiple generated attempts introduces another averaging unit. For $n$ candidate solutions with $c$ passing, select $k\le n$ of the candidates uniformly without replacement. There are $\binom nk$ possible subsets and $\binom{n-c}k$ subsets containing only failures. Therefore

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{pass@}k}
=1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom nk}.
$$

The numerator is zero when fewer than $k$ candidates fail. With six candidates, two passing and two selected, six of the fifteen subsets contain only failures, giving 0.6. This is the combinatorial estimator used by Chen et al. ([2021](#references), §2, Appendix A). With independent samples from one fixed generation distribution, it estimates success within $k$ attempts. It does not say that an application can identify the successful answer without the checker, and it cannot be compared fairly with a one-attempt result while ignoring the extra budget.

## Separate retrieval from answer generation \{#retrieval-evaluation\}

A retrieved passage can be relevant even when the final answer ignores it. An answer can also be correct without relying on the retrieved passage. Evaluation should distinguish retrieval of usable evidence, faithful use of that evidence and satisfaction of the final task.

For query $i$, define $r_i$ as the rank of the first relevant result; use an infinite rank when none is returned. The success indicator for finding any relevant result within the first $k$ positions is $\mathbf1[r_i\le k]$. Its mean is Hit@k. Define reciprocal rank as $1/r_i$ for a found result and zero otherwise. Mean reciprocal rank is

$$
\mathrm{MRR}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\frac1{r_i},
$$

with $1/\infty=0$. For newly specified ranks $(1,3,\infty,2)$, Hit@2 is 0.5 and MRR is $11/24\approx0.458333$. These quantities assess the first relevant result; they do not count every relevant passage or establish whether a generated claim is supported.

The relevance labels need a defined corpus version and evidence criterion. A document can contain the topic word while failing to answer the question. Preserve the retrieved IDs and supplied context so a failed answer can be traced to retrieval, context selection or generation. A language-model judge cannot recover missing ground truth merely by explaining its verdict confidently.

## Treat a model judge as a measured component \{#a-model-as-judge\}

For open-ended answers, a model can apply a written rubric, compare two outputs or grade one output with a reference. These procedures produce different observations. The judge itself has a checkpoint, prompt, decoding configuration and output parser. Record them with the candidate systems. A judge's explanation is another generated output, whose factual assertions may need checking.

The MT-Bench/Chatbot Arena study documents position and verbosity effects and limitations in grading reasoning ([Zheng et al., 2023](#references), §§3.1–3.4). Those findings motivate controls; they do not establish a fixed error rate for a different judge, task or prompt. Validate against an independently labeled sample from the intended use case.

### Reverse the order while preserving identities \{#the-experiment\}

Specify four artificial paired-judge outcomes. `A` and `B` below always refer to candidate identity, even when presentation order is reversed:

| Item | Order A then B | Order B then A |
| ---: | --- | --- |
| 1 | A | B |
| 2 | B | B |
| 3 | tie | tie |
| 4 | A | A |

The first item's verdict follows the first position and changes candidate identity. Items 2 and 4 retain a winner; item 3 retains a tie. One of the three non-tied items reverses. These labels were authored to illustrate the check, with no judge model called.

<JudgeOrder />

Counting the reversal as a tie would lose information about inconsistency. Preserve both raw decisions and report a separate disagreement category. Randomizing order can balance exposure across positions, while evaluating both orders reveals sensitivity for a specific pair. Neither procedure repairs an incorrect rubric or proves that a consistent winner is correct.

### Make the grading protocol inspectable \{#practice\}

State the criterion before presenting answers: for example, whether every numerical claim follows from supplied inputs, and whether the output schema is satisfied. Keep format checks deterministic where possible. For semantic claims, ask for evidence tied to the supplied context and check a sample manually. Hide candidate names that could reveal reputation or origin unless identity is genuinely part of the task.

Decide how ties, invalid judge outputs and refusals enter the denominator. Repeated judgments of the same answer measure judge variation; they do not create new independent user tasks. Keep prompt families and repeated runs grouped when estimating uncertainty. Judge prompts and labels selected on the final test set consume that test set as development data.

### Agreement is different from correctness \{#agreement-for-categorical-labels\}

For two raters assigning the same finite set of categories, let $p_o$ be their observed agreement fraction. Let $u_c$ and $v_c$ be their respective marginal fractions for category $c$. Independent labels drawn from those marginals would agree with probability

$$
p_e=\sum_c u_cv_c.
$$

Cohen's kappa compares the observed excess agreement with the available excess above that marginal baseline, provided $p_e<1$:

$$
\kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e}.
$$

The marginal independence construction defines the chance baseline; it does not assert that the real raters label independently. A kappa of one represents complete agreement, while zero represents agreement equal to this baseline. Negative values are possible. When $p_e=1$, the denominator is zero and this coefficient is undefined. Cohen explains the chance correction in his own retrospective ([1986](#references)); the formula above also makes these boundary cases explicit.

Consider the new count table below. Rows are rater 1's labels and columns are rater 2's labels; neither rater is designated as ground truth.

| | Rater 2: pass | Rater 2: fail |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Rater 1: pass | 6 | 2 |
| Rater 1: fail | 1 | 3 |

The observed agreement is $9/12$. Row proportions are $8/12$ and $4/12$; column proportions are $7/12$ and $5/12$. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_e&=\frac8{12}\frac7{12}+\frac4{12}\frac5{12}\\
&=\frac{19}{36},\\
\kappa&=\frac{9/12-19/36}{1-19/36}\\
&=\frac8{17}\approx0.470588.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is agreement under the specified marginal model, not 47% accuracy. Two raters can agree on the same wrong answer. Changing category prevalence changes the baseline, so report the count table and raw agreement rather than transferring a universal verbal label such as “good” across tasks.

## Give human judgments a protocol \{#human-evaluation\}

Human evaluation also needs a rubric, representative examples, independent initial labels and an adjudication procedure. Training annotators can clarify the criterion; it can also align their conventions without making the criterion appropriate. Separate disagreements about the task definition from mistakes in applying it.

Use expertise matched to the claims being assessed. A fluent response may be easy to prefer while requiring specialist evidence to verify. Keep factual support, task completion and presentation quality as separate labels when they can diverge. Record which cases were adjudicated and preserve the original labels; replacing them silently would erase the disagreement evidence.

## Measure the deployed path \{#the-system-not-the-model\}

The evaluated unit can include retrieval, tools, retries, filtering, caching and a final response. A correct intermediate model completion does not prove the whole request succeeds. Count failed and timed-out requests, and state whether retries are included in quality and cost. HELM treats efficiency as a separate dimension and distinguishes runtime measurements with different system boundaries ([Liang et al., 2023](#references), §4.9).

For an interactive system, distinguish time to first token, completion time and throughput. Token generation rate depends on output length and its tokenizer. Concurrent throughput depends on scheduling and load. The reciprocal of one request's latency is not a general estimate of service capacity.

Take ten specified completion times in milliseconds:

`80, 100, 110, 120, 150, 180, 200, 240, 300, 900`.

For a nonempty sample and $0<p\le1$, define the nearest-rank quantile at probability $p$ as the sorted observation at one-based position $\lceil pn\rceil$. The median by this convention is 150 ms, the 90th percentile is 300 ms and the 95th percentile is 900 ms. Another quantile convention can interpolate differently, so state the convention. These are constructed values, not a benchmark of a service.

<LatencyTail />

Measure latency under a declared load, prompt-length distribution, generation budget, cache state and hardware configuration. Keep raw traces so queuing, retrieval and generation can be separated. A fast median can coexist with slow tails or a high failure rate; omitting failed requests can make a system appear faster as it becomes less reliable.

## Preserve the evidence behind the comparison \{#what-changed\}

A headline score becomes a reproducible evaluation when it is connected to versioned inputs, candidate outputs, a grader, matched item-level outcomes and a stated aggregation rule. Component checks locate failures, human and model judgments supply different observations, and operational measurements identify the cost of the complete path.

## State what the sample does not establish \{#limitations\}

A test may be too small, too homogeneous or too familiar to the model. Duplicate prompts and shared source documents create dependence. Searching many checkpoints or rubrics against one test set changes its role. A statistical test cannot repair these selection choices after the fact.

Our fixtures verify arithmetic, grading conventions and diagnostic calculations. They contain no measured LLM quality, judge reliability or service capacity. Real deployment claims require newly collected observations under the stated protocol. Preserve uncertainty about unknown training exposure instead of treating the absence of a known duplicate as proof of no contamination.

## Name the question before the metric \{#what-you-should-remember\}

Specify the population, task, system revision and grader. Preserve pairing and name the averaging unit. Keep agreement separate from correctness. Evaluate retrieval and generation at their own boundaries. Include failures, cost and latency under the same operating conditions.
