We have token ids and a way to measure a classifier. The task for this note is concrete: 120,000 news articles, four topics (World, Sports, Business, Sci/Tech), and a model that assigns a topic to an unseen article. A linear classifier needs a fixed-length vector per document, but documents have different lengths. The simplest way to get a fixed-length vector is to ignore order entirely and count.

**Notation.** $V$ is the vocabulary with $|V|$ entries, $D$ the training corpus with $|D|$ documents. $N(t, d)$ counts occurrences of token $t$ in document $d$; $\mathrm{df}(t) = |\{d \in D : t \in d\}|$ is the number of documents containing $t$. A document is represented by a vector $x_d \in \mathbb R^{|V|}$.

## Baseline: counting tokens

The **bag of words** representation sets

$$
(x_d)_t = N(t, d) \qquad \text{for every } t \in V.
$$

A document becomes a vector whose coordinate for each vocabulary entry is how many times that entry appears. Word order is discarded: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" get the same vector. What survives is which tokens appear and how often, and for topic classification that is most of the signal: an article about a football match contains `match`, `goal` and `coach` whatever order the sentences come in.

Shapes make the cost visible. With the AG News vocabulary from the [preprocessing note](/notes/nlp/text-as-data), $|V| = 29{,}350$, and the training matrix $X$ has shape $120{,}000 \times 29{,}350$. Stored densely that would be $3.5 \times 10^9$ entries; stored sparsely it is tiny, because an article of 40 distinct tokens has 40 non-zero coordinates out of 29,350. Everything in this note works on sparse matrices.

### A linear classifier on counts

Multinomial logistic regression maps the vector to one score per class and normalizes with a softmax:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z &= W x_d + b, \\
W &\in \mathbb R^{K \times |V|},\; b \in \mathbb R^K, \\
p(y = k \mid d) &= \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Row $k$ of $W$ holds one weight per vocabulary entry; a positive weight for `goal` in the Sports row raises the Sports score whenever `goal` appears, in proportion to how often it appears. The model is trained by minimizing the cross-entropy of the true label under the predicted distribution, averaged over the corpus:

$$
\mathcal L = -\frac{1}{|D|}\sum_{d \in D} \log p(y_d \mid d).
$$

Why this loss, and how it relates to entropy and KL divergence, is derived in the [Generative Models course](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#why-cross-entropy-is-the-loss-we-minimize); the [n-gram note](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models#perplexity-from-probability-to-a-number) restates the four equivalent forms for the language-model case. For now: minimizing $\mathcal L$ maximizes the likelihood of the training labels.

### Worked example

Three one-line documents and a five-word vocabulary:

| | `cat` | `dog` | `sat` | `ran` | `fast` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $d_1$ = "the cat sat" | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| $d_2$ = "the dog ran fast" | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| $d_3$ = "the cat ran fast fast" | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 2 |

`the` was removed as a stopword. $d_3$ has a 2 in the `fast` column because the token appears twice. A classifier that has to separate "animal" from "motion" articles can put positive weights on `sat`/`cat`/`dog` in one row and `ran`/`fast` in the other; $d_3$, which mixes both, gets a score that depends on the counts.

## Where raw counts fail

Two effects distort the count vectors.

**Length.** A long document has larger counts than a short one on the same topic, so its vector is longer and its scores $z = Wx$ are scaled up. The classifier's confidence then measures length, not evidence.

**Common tokens.** After stopword removal, some tokens are still in most documents of a corpus: in a news corpus, `said`, `year`, `new`. Their counts are large, so they dominate the dot products $Wx$ while saying nothing about the topic. A token that appears in *every* document says nothing through its presence alone. Its count can still differ between classes (once per article in one topic, ten times in another), but such tokens usually carry little topical signal. IDF down-weights them by a fixed rule that ignores the labels; a trained classifier can also learn small weights for them.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Keep the counts, but multiply each by a weight that is high for tokens concentrated in few documents and low for tokens spread over the whole corpus, then normalize the document vector to unit length so that length stops mattering.</aside>

## TF-IDF: reweighting the counts

**Term frequency** is the count, normalized by document length in some variants:

$$
\mathrm{tf}(t, d) = \frac{N(t, d)}{\sum_{k \in V} N(k, d)}.
$$

**Inverse document frequency** measures how rare a token is across documents:

$$
\mathrm{idf}(t) = \log\frac{|D|}{\mathrm{df}(t)}.
$$

A token in every document has $\mathrm{df}(t) = |D|$ and $\mathrm{idf} = \log 1 = 0$: it is switched off. A token in one document out of a thousand has $\mathrm{idf} = \log 1000 \approx 6.9$. The logarithm compresses the range so that a token in 1 % of documents is not 100 times more important than one in 100 %; it is $\log 100 \approx 4.6$ units more important. The product

$$
\text{tf-idf}(t, d) = \mathrm{tf}(t, d)\cdot \mathrm{idf}(t)
$$

is the new coordinate. The results below use the scikit-learn implementation, which differs in three details that matter when reproducing numbers: it uses the raw count $N(t, d)$ as tf; it smooths the idf as $\log\frac{1 + |D|}{1 + \mathrm{df}(t)} + 1$, as if one extra document contained every token, so that no weight is infinite, and the added 1 keeps a token found in every document at weight 1 instead of 0; and it rescales every document vector to unit Euclidean norm, which removes the length effect directly and makes dividing the counts by the length unnecessary.

### Worked example, continued

On the three documents above, $|D| = 3$. Document frequencies: `cat` 2, `dog` 1, `sat` 1, `ran` 2, `fast` 2. With the plain definition, $\mathrm{idf}(\text{cat}) = \ln(3/2) = 0.405$, $\mathrm{idf}(\text{dog}) = \ln 3 = 1.099$. For $d_3$ (five tokens including `the`, four after stopword removal):

| Token | $N(t, d_3)$ | $\mathrm{tf}$ | $\mathrm{idf}$ | tf-idf |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `cat` | 1 | 0.25 | 0.405 | 0.101 |
| `ran` | 1 | 0.25 | 0.405 | 0.101 |
| `fast` | 2 | 0.50 | 0.405 | 0.203 |

Had `fast` appeared in all three documents, its idf would be 0 and the largest count in the vector would contribute nothing. The weighting does what we asked: counts still matter, but only for tokens that discriminate between documents.

## Results on AG News

The pipeline is: fit the vectorizer on the training texts (vocabulary and idf), transform both splits, train logistic regression, measure accuracy.

| Features | Vocabulary | Train acc. | Test acc. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TF-IDF, unigrams, `min_df=4`, `max_df=0.95` | 29,350 | 0.940 | 0.918 |
| TF-IDF, 1- to 3-grams, `min_df=10`, `max_df=0.95` | 101,284 | 0.951 | 0.920 |

Two frequency cuts shape the vocabulary. `min_df=4` drops tokens seen in fewer than four training documents, which are too rare to estimate a weight for and are mostly typos and names; `max_df=0.95` drops tokens in more than 95 % of documents, which idf would nearly zero out anyway.

The second row adds **n-grams**: contiguous pairs and triples of tokens as extra vocabulary entries. This is the standard partial fix for the order problem: `not done` becomes a feature of its own, distinct from `not` and `done`. The price is vocabulary size. With $|V|$ unigrams there are up to $|V|^2$ bigrams, so the frequency cut has to be raised (`min_df=10`) to keep the matrix manageable, and the gain here is 0.2 points, within the sampling error of a 7,600-example test set: one standard error of an accuracy near 0.92 is $\sqrt{0.92 \cdot 0.08 / 7600} \approx 0.003$, or 0.3 points. On this corpus, topic is carried by individual tokens and bigrams add little.

The 2–3 point gap between train and test accuracy is mild overfitting: with 29,350 weights per class and 120,000 documents, the model can memorize some training-specific tokens.

## When a linear model on TF-IDF is the right choice

Count features with a linear classifier are a stronger baseline than their simplicity suggests: well-chosen variants matched or beat more complex published models on topic and sentiment benchmarks ([Wang and Manning, 2012](#references)), and in this course no network trained from scratch on the AG News articles beat its 0.918 on the test set, while the fastText classifier, a linear model over averaged word and bigram embeddings, matched it within noise at 0.922 ([classification note](/notes/nlp/text-classification#results-across-the-course)). Run it first, and keep it as the final model when one of these holds:

- **The label is a topic.** When the label follows from which words occur, the vocabulary already carries the signal, and order adds little: the bigram row above gained 0.2 points.
- **Compute is scarce.** Training and prediction run on a CPU; a prediction is one sparse dot product per class.
- **Labelled data is small.** The loss is [convex](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#which-machine-learning-losses-are-convex), so every run reaches the same optimum, and the few hyperparameters (regularization strength, frequency cuts) are cheap to choose by cross-validation, where a network trained from scratch would overfit. A fine-tuned pretrained encoder can still do better with few labels ([classification note](/notes/nlp/text-classification#what-improved-and-what-did-not)), at a far higher cost; the baseline measures how much better.
- **Decisions must be explained.** Every weight belongs to one token, so the largest weights of a class row are a readable list of its evidence, and a wrong prediction can be traced to the tokens that pushed it.
- **Documents are long.** A count vector has no length limit, while a Transformer encoder reads a fixed window (512 tokens for BERT) and drops the rest.

It is the wrong choice when the label depends on order, negation or composition beyond a few adjacent tokens (the limitations below), or when the output is a span, a sequence of tags or text.

## What changed

| | Bag of words | TF-IDF |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coordinate | $N(t, d)$ | $\mathrm{tf}(t,d)\cdot\mathrm{idf}(t)$, vector normalized |
| Common tokens | Dominate | Down-weighted to ~0 |
| Document length | Scales the vector | Removed by normalization |
| Word order | Ignored | Ignored (n-grams recover adjacent pairs) |
| Cost | One sparse matrix | Same, plus one pass for idf |

The representation is still a sparse count vector; what changed is the weighting, and that alone makes a linear model competitive. The 0.92 test accuracy is the number to remember: in the [CNN note](/notes/nlp/cnn-text-classification), convolutional networks over word embeddings reach 0.90–0.91, below this baseline, and only a fine-tuned pretrained Transformer clearly exceeds it in the [transfer learning note](/notes/nlp/transfer-learning).

## Limitations

- **No order beyond n-grams.** "The film was not bad, it was great" and "the film was not great, it was bad" share most unigrams and bigrams.
- **No similarity between tokens.** `cat` and `dog` are orthogonal coordinates; weight learned for one says nothing about the other. Every token must be seen often enough in training to get its own weight. The [embeddings note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings) is the response to this.
- **Open vocabulary.** A token absent from the training set has no coordinate and is silently dropped at test time.
- **Dimensionality.** $|V|$ coordinates per document, and $|V|^2$ with bigrams; the representation does not compress. A topic model compresses the same counts into a few topic proportions per document ([Latent Dirichlet allocation](/notes/bayesian-methods/latent-dirichlet-allocation)).
- **Topic, not meaning.** The method works where the label is predictable from which words occur. It cannot answer a question, translate, or tell who did what to whom.

## What you should remember

- Bag of words: a document is the vector of its token counts. Order is discarded; the matrix is sparse.
- A linear classifier on these counts, trained with cross-entropy, is a strong baseline for topic classification. Prefer it when the label is a topic, compute is scarce, decisions must be explained or documents are long; with few labels it is the cheap reference a fine-tuned encoder has to beat.
- TF-IDF multiplies each count by $\log(|D|/\mathrm{df}(t))$, switching off tokens that occur everywhere, and normalizes the vector to cancel length.
- Fit the vocabulary and idf on training data only.
- AG News: 0.918 test accuracy with unigrams, 0.920 with 1- to 3-grams. Neural models in the next notes must beat 0.92 to justify their cost.
