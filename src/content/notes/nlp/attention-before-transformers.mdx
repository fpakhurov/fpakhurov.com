The encoder–decoder of the [previous note](/notes/nlp/seq2seq) compresses the source into one vector before the decoder writes anything, and the outputs show the cost: fluent sentences about the wrong things. The decoder should be able to look back at the source as it writes, and to look at different parts of it for different target words. [Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2015)](#references) added exactly this and called it attention. This note builds the mechanism, works one step by hand, and shows an experiment that measures what it buys.

**Notation.** Encoder states $h_1, \dots, h_S \in \mathbb R^{H}$ (one per source token; $2H$ if bidirectional). Decoder state $s_t \in \mathbb R^{H}$ at step $t$. Scores $e_{t,i}$, weights $\alpha_{t,i}$, context vector $c_t$.

## Baseline: the final state as the only channel

The decoder is initialized with $s_0 = f(h_S)$ and then runs on its own. There is no operation by which step $t$ of the decoder can read $h_3$ specifically. The states $h_1, \dots, h_{S-1}$ are computed and discarded.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Keep all the encoder states. At each decoder step, score every encoder state by its relevance to the current decoder state, normalize the scores into weights that sum to one, and hand the decoder the weighted sum of encoder states. Which source positions matter is decided per output token, from the data.</aside>

## The mechanism

At decoder step $t$:

1. **Score** each encoder state against the decoder state. The simplest score is a dot product, $e_{t,i} = s_t \cdot h_i$; it is large when the two vectors point the same way. Other choices exist (below), and whichever is used, the output is one number per source position.
2. **Normalize** with a softmax over source positions:
   $$
   \alpha_{t,i} = \frac{\exp(e_{t,i})}{\sum_{j=1}^S \exp(e_{t,j})}, \qquad \sum_i \alpha_{t,i} = 1,\ \alpha_{t,i} > 0.
   $$
   The weights form a distribution over the source: "how much of each source token to use now".
3. **Aggregate** into a context vector, the weighted sum of encoder states:
   $$
   c_t = \sum_{i=1}^S \alpha_{t,i}\, h_i \in \mathbb R^{H}.
   $$
4. **Predict** from the decoder state and the context together, for example by concatenating them before the output layer: $P(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x) = \mathrm{softmax}(W_o [s_t; c_t])$, and feeding $c_t$ into the next decoder step as well.

Every operation is differentiable, so the scoring is learned by backpropagation from the translation loss; nothing tells the model which source word corresponds to which target word. The weights are recomputed at every step: $t = 1$ can attend to the first source word and $t = 5$ to the last.

Shapes for one step and a batch of $B$: encoder states $[B, S, H]$, decoder state $[B, H]$, scores $[B, S]$, weights $[B, S]$, context $[B, H]$. Over a whole target of length $T$ the weights form a $[B, T, S]$ tensor, which is what the heatmaps in the [translation note](/notes/nlp/transformer-machine-translation#what-the-cross-attention-learned) display.

### Worked example

Three encoder states in two dimensions, $h_1 = (1, 0)$, $h_2 = (0, 1)$, $h_3 = (1, 1)$, and a decoder state $s = (0.9, 0.2)$:

| $i$ | $e_i = s \cdot h_i$ | $\exp(e_i)$ | $\alpha_i$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.9$ | $2.460$ | $0.368$ |
| 2 | $0.2$ | $1.221$ | $0.183$ |
| 3 | $1.1$ | $3.004$ | $0.449$ |

The denominator is $2.460 + 1.221 + 3.004 = 6.685$. The context is $0.368\,(1, 0) + 0.183\,(0, 1) + 0.449\,(1, 1) = (0.817, 0.632)$. It is a point inside the triangle spanned by the encoder states, closest to the ones the decoder state resembled; nothing was selected, everything was mixed in proportion.

### Scoring functions

The dot product assumes $s_t$ and $h_i$ live in the same space and have comparable scale. Two generalizations are common ([Luong, Pham and Manning, 2015](#references)):

| Name | $e_{t,i}$ | Parameters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| dot | $s_t^\top h_i$ | none |
| general (bilinear) | $s_t^\top W h_i$ | $W \in \mathbb R^{H \times H}$ |
| additive ([Bahdanau et al., 2015](#references)) | $v^\top \tanh(W_1 s_t + W_2 h_i)$ | $W_1, W_2, v$ |

The additive form was the original; the dot form is the cheapest and, with a learned projection of both sides, is what the [self-attention note](/notes/nlp/attention) uses. Cosine similarity, which divides by the norms, is a possible score too but discards magnitude information that the model can use; the standard choice is the unnormalized dot product, rescaled by a constant in the Transformer.

## Evidence: removing the bottleneck from a Transformer

The clean comparison is not LSTM-with-attention versus LSTM-without, because the two models differ in several ways. The notebook does something sharper with the one-layer Transformer of the [translation note](/notes/nlp/transformer-machine-translation), whose decoder reads the encoder through exactly the mechanism above (there called cross-attention). Two versions are trained identically for 10 epochs on Multi30k:

| Decoder may attend to | Test BLEU |
| --- | --- |
| all $S$ encoder states | 0.320 |
| only the first encoder state (an artificial bottleneck) | 0.190 |
| LSTM encoder–decoder, no attention (previous note) | 0.152 |

Restricting the decoder to one encoder vector costs 0.13 BLEU and brings the Transformer down near the LSTM baseline. Everything else (self-attention inside the decoder, the feed-forward layers, the embeddings) is unchanged, so the difference is attributable to the decoder's access to the full set of source states. The bottleneck is the problem, and attention is its removal.

The learned weights are interpretable in this task. Plotted as a matrix with target tokens as rows and source tokens as columns, the one-layer model's cross-attention is close to diagonal, with each English token attending to the German token *after* the one it translates, because the model is predicting the next token and the aligned source word for the next output is the most useful thing to read.

## What attention gives

- **No fixed-size summary.** The decoder reads $S$ vectors, however long the source is. Long sentences are no longer compressed harder than short ones.
- **Alignment for free.** The weights $\alpha_{t,i}$ are a soft word alignment, learned from translation pairs alone.
- **A short gradient path.** The loss at target step $t$ reaches encoder state $h_i$ through one weighted sum instead of through $S + t$ recurrent steps, which eases the vanishing problem of the [RNN note](/notes/nlp/recurrent-networks#gradients-through-time).

## What changed

| | Encoder–decoder | With attention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source information per step | $s_0$, fixed | $c_t$, recomputed |
| Encoder states used | $h_S$ only | all $h_1, \dots, h_S$ |
| Extra computation per step | — | $S$ scores, one softmax, one weighted sum |
| Extra parameters | — | none (dot) to $O(H^2)$ (general, additive) |
| BLEU, Multi30k, 1-layer Transformer decoder | 0.190 (bottlenecked) | 0.320 |

## Limitations

- **The encoder is still recurrent.** The states $h_i$ are computed sequentially, so the encoder cannot be parallelized along the source, and each $h_i$ still carries the source only through a chain of recurrences.
- **Attention is only between decoder and encoder.** Within the source, token $i$ learns about token $j$ only through the recurrence; within the target, likewise. There is no reason the same scoring could not relate positions of one sequence to each other.
- **Cost per step is $O(S)$.** Fine for sentences; a concern for documents, and quadratic once every position attends to every other.
- **Soft weights are not explanations.** A near-diagonal alignment on a caption dataset does not generalize to a claim that attention weights show what the model "uses" ([Jain and Wallace, 2019](#references)).

## What you should remember

- Attention at step $t$: scores $e_{t,i}$ between the decoder state and each encoder state, softmax over $i$, context $c_t = \sum_i \alpha_{t,i} h_i$, prediction from $[s_t; c_t]$.
- The weights are a distribution over source positions, recomputed per output token and learned from the task loss.
- Dot-product scoring is the cheapest; additive and bilinear variants add parameters.
- Removing the decoder's access to all encoder states costs 0.13 BLEU on Multi30k in an otherwise identical model.
- Attention removes the bottleneck but not the recurrence.

## Next

Attention compared a decoder state with encoder states. Nothing in the mechanism requires the two sides to come from different networks: a sequence can attend to *itself*, each position scoring every other position and aggregating their values. Doing that removes the recurrence entirely, because every position's new representation is a weighted sum computed in one matrix product, in parallel over the sequence. The [next note](/notes/nlp/attention) writes self-attention in matrix form, with queries, keys and values, and the Transformer is built on it.
