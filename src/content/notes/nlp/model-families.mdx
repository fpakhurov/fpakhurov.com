Two notes have now trained the same block in two arrangements with two objectives: the bidirectional encoder of the [BERT note](/notes/nlp/bert) with masked tokens, and the causal decoder of the [GPT note](/notes/nlp/gpt) with next tokens. The encoder–decoder of the [translation note](/notes/nlp/transformer-machine-translation) was trained on a task, not pretrained, but it has its own pretraining objective too. This note lines the three up and makes the comparison that the architecture names alone do not: objective, what the model computes, what it is good at, and what it costs.

**Notation.** Input tokens $x$; for encoder–decoders, the target $y$; a contextual vector $h_t$ per position; the mask pattern of self-attention is *full* (every position sees every position) or *causal* (position $t$ sees $\le t$).

## Baseline: the architecture name as the only description

"Encoder-only", "decoder-only" and "encoder–decoder" describe which positions can attend to which, and whether there is a second sequence. That determines what the model *can* compute. What it *does* compute is set by the pretraining objective, and the two are coupled: a bidirectional stack cannot use the next-token objective (the answer leaks), and a causal stack gets no benefit from masking.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Read each family as a pair (mask, objective). The mask fixes which contexts are available; the objective fixes what the model learns to produce from them. Everything downstream, from which tasks fit to which decoding is needed, follows from the pair.</aside>

## The three families

### Encoder-only: full mask, masked-token objective

Every position sees the whole input. Pretraining hides 15 % of the tokens and predicts them (MLM). The output is one contextual vector per input token; there is no mechanism to produce a sequence of new tokens. Representatives: BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa, and the sentence-embedding models of the [semantic search note](/notes/nlp/semantic-search).

What it is good for: anything that maps an input to a label, a label per token, or a span of the input. Classification, named entity recognition, extractive question answering, retrieval embeddings. The [NLP tasks section](/notes/nlp/text-classification) uses an encoder for all of these, with a different small head each time.

### Decoder-only: causal mask, next-token objective

Position $t$ sees $\le t$. Pretraining predicts every token from its prefix. The output at $t$ is a distribution over the next token, so the model generates by sampling and feeding back. Representatives: the GPT series, Llama, Mistral, Qwen, Gemma.

What it is good for: generation, and, through prompting, any task that can be written as text in and text out. The representation at position $t$ is one-directional, so for a pure understanding task the model has to see the whole input before any output position can summarize it; the summary lives at the last position.

### Encoder–decoder: full mask on the source, causal mask on the target, cross-attention between them

The encoder reads $x$ bidirectionally; the decoder generates $y$ causally while attending to the encoder output. Pretraining objectives are **span corruption** (T5, [Raffel et al., 2020](#references)): drop random spans from the input, replace each by a sentinel token, and train the decoder to emit the dropped spans in order; or **denoising** (BART, [Lewis et al., 2020](#references)): corrupt the input by masking, deletion, permutation and infilling, and reconstruct the original. Both are "reconstruct what was damaged", using the encoder for the damaged text and the decoder for the repair.

What it is good for: tasks with a distinct input and a generated output. Translation, summarization, question generation. The [summarization note](/notes/nlp/summarization) fine-tunes a T5 model; T5 frames every task, including classification, as text-to-text (`summarize: …`, `translate English to German: …`).

| | Encoder-only | Decoder-only | Encoder–decoder |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Self-attention mask | full | causal | full (source) + causal (target) |
| Second sequence | no | no (prompt is part of the one sequence) | yes, via cross-attention |
| Pretraining objective | masked tokens | next token | corrupted spans / denoising |
| Trains on | 15 % of positions | all positions | the dropped spans |
| Output | vector per input token | next-token distribution | next-token distribution for $y$ |
| Representation of $x$ | bidirectional | left-to-right | bidirectional |
| Can generate | no | yes | yes |
| Examples | BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa | GPT, Llama, Mistral | T5, BART, mT5 |

## How the objectives differ in what they teach

**MLM** gives the richest per-token representation, because each prediction uses both sides, and the poorest generation, because it never produces a sequence. It trains on a minority of positions per pass.

**Next-token prediction** trains on every position and gives a model that generates. The representation of early tokens is impoverished by never seeing later ones, but the model compensates when it is allowed to read the whole input first and answer afterwards, which is the normal prompting pattern.

**Span corruption** sits between them: the encoder learns bidirectional representations, the decoder learns to generate, and the sentinel mechanism trains the decoder to produce short, targeted outputs rather than whole documents. It costs two stacks and the cross-attention.

An encoder's output is a representation of the *input*; a decoder's output is a representation of *what comes next*. This is the practical rule for choosing: when the quantity of interest is a property of the input (its class, its entities, its similarity to another text), an encoder's vectors are the right feature, and they are cheaper, because one forward pass produces all of them with no decoding loop. When the quantity of interest is new text, a decoder is required.

## Why decoder-only models came to dominate

Three reasons, all empirical.

**Scaling.** Next-token prediction uses every token of every document as a training signal and needs no masking or span machinery; it scaled to trillions of tokens with the least engineering, and the scaling laws were measured on it ([Kaplan et al., 2020](#references)).

**Unification.** A sufficiently large decoder does classification, extraction and translation by prompting, with no new head. One model replaces a zoo of fine-tuned encoders, at a cost in inference compute, because a decoder has to generate the label token by token where an encoder read it off a linear layer.

**In-context learning.** Given a few examples in the prompt, a large decoder performs the task without parameter updates ([Brown et al., 2020](#references)); encoders have no comparable mechanism because they have no output sequence to put the answer in.

What the dominance does not mean: encoders are not obsolete. For a fixed task with labels and a latency budget, a fine-tuned 110 M-parameter encoder is routinely more accurate, 10–100× cheaper to run and easier to evaluate than a prompted decoder; the [transfer learning note](/notes/nlp/transfer-learning) measures the first part of that on AG News, and every retrieval system in the [LLM systems section](/notes/nlp/semantic-search) runs on encoders.

## Choosing, in practice

| Need | Pick | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Label an input, or each token of it, with labelled data available | Encoder, fine-tuned | Best accuracy per parameter; one pass; no decoding |
| Embed texts for search or similarity | Encoder (sentence-embedding variant) | Bidirectional vectors, trained for cosine comparison |
| Generate free text, chat, follow instructions | Decoder | Only family that generates from a prompt alone |
| Transform an input into an output (translate, summarize) with training pairs | Encoder–decoder or decoder | Encoder–decoder is the classical fit; large decoders match it by prompting |
| Do many tasks with no labels | Decoder, instruction-tuned | Prompting and in-context learning |

The decision interacts with the training regime of the next section: fine-tuning favours small encoders, parameter-efficient tuning and prompting favour large decoders.

## Limitations

- **The table is about pretrained checkpoints, not possibilities.** Any arrangement can be trained with any objective that does not leak; a decoder can be fine-tuned to produce embeddings, and encoders can be stacked into generators.
- **"Bidirectional" is not free.** An encoder must re-encode when the input changes; a decoder with a KV cache appends.
- **The comparison is empirical and moving.** Which family wins on a task depends on model size, data and the year; the structural facts (what each mask allows, what each objective trains) are the part that stays.

## What you should remember

- Encoder: full mask, MLM, vector per token, no generation. Decoder: causal mask, next token, generates. Encoder–decoder: both, joined by cross-attention, trained by span corruption or denoising.
- An encoder represents the input; a decoder represents what comes next. Pick by whether the answer is a property of the input or new text.
- Decoders dominate because next-token prediction scales most simply, one model serves many tasks by prompting, and in-context learning needs an output sequence.
- Encoders remain the efficient choice for labelled classification, tagging, extraction and retrieval embeddings.
