[Word2Vec](/notes/nlp/word2vec) and [GloVe](/notes/nlp/glove) give each word type its own vector, learned only from the contexts of that exact string. *Coffee* and *coffees* are trained separately, *learnig* has no vector at all, and in a language like Russian, where a noun has a dozen case and number forms, most forms are rare even in a large corpus. FastText ([Bojanowski et al., 2017](#references)) keeps the skip-gram objective and changes what a word is: a bag of character n-grams, each with its own vector, so that words written alike share parameters and any string, seen or not, gets a vector.

**Notation.** For a word $w$, $\mathcal G_w$ is its set of character n-grams together with the word itself; each $g \in \mathcal G_w$ has a vector $z_g \in \mathbb R^d$. Context words have vectors $v_c$, as in Word2Vec. $K$ is the number of hash buckets.

## Baseline: one vector per word type

On the 537,272 Quora questions of the [previous notes](/notes/nlp/glove#a-run-on-quora-questions), lower-cased and without stopwords, there are 87,511 word types. With the usual cut-off of 5 occurrences, 59,722 of them, 68% of the types, get no vector. They are only 2.4% of the tokens, but 14% of the questions contain at least one of them: misspellings (*programing*, 35 times), rare inflections, names, new words. And the 27,789 words that do get vectors learn nothing from each other: the vector of *learns* is trained from its own few contexts, not helped by the many contexts of *learn*.

## A word is a bag of character n-grams

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Add boundary symbols to the word, cut it into all character n-grams of length 3 to 6, and represent the word by the sum of their vectors (plus a vector for the whole word). Words that share pieces share parameters, and a word never seen in training still has pieces.</aside>

The paper's example is *where*. With boundaries it becomes `<where>`, and its 3-grams are `<wh`, `whe`, `her`, `ere`, `re>`; with the 4-, 5- and 6-grams there are 14 n-grams, and together with `<where>` itself, 15 vectors. The boundaries matter: the 3-gram `her` inside *where* is a different piece from the word *her*, which is `<her>`.

The score of a word and a context word, which in Word2Vec was the dot product $u_w \cdot v_c$, becomes

$$
s(w, c) = \sum_{g \in \mathcal G_w} z_g \cdot v_c ,
$$

and everything else, the [negative-sampling loss](/notes/nlp/word2vec#negative-sampling) and the sampling of negatives, stays as it was. The gradient of every training pair reaches every n-gram vector of the word, so *learns*, *learnt* and *learner* all train the vectors of `<le`, `lea`, `ear`, `arn` and the longer pieces they share with *learn*.

<NgramFlow />

The steps of the figure:

1. **Boundaries.** `<` and `>` mark the start and the end, so prefixes and suffixes are distinct pieces.
2. **N-grams.** All substrings of length 3 to 6, plus the whole word as one more piece: 15 pieces for *where*.
3. **Look-up.** Each piece is hashed to a row of one table of n-gram vectors.
4. **Sum.** The word vector is the sum of the rows. (Implementations such as gensim divide by the number of pieces; the scale does not matter for cosine.)

## Hashing

A vocabulary of all character n-grams of a large corpus would be huge, so the n-grams are not stored by name. Each one is hashed (FNV-1a in the paper) into one of $K = 2 \cdot 10^6$ buckets, and the bucket's row is its vector. Two n-grams in one bucket share a vector; with two million buckets such collisions are rare enough not to matter, and memory is fixed in advance: $2 \cdot 10^6 \times 300$ floats of 4 bytes is 2.4 GB for 300-dimensional vectors, 256 MB for the 32-dimensional vectors used below.

## Vectors for unseen words

A word that was never seen still has n-grams, and most of them were seen in other words. Its vector is the sum of their vectors. Trained on the Quora questions (gensim, CBOW variant, $d = 32$, window 5, words with at least 5 occurrences, 5 epochs), the model answers queries for strings that are not in its vocabulary:

| Query (not in the vocabulary) | Nearest words by cosine |
| --- | --- |
| coffeee | coffee 0.988, koffee 0.928, coffees 0.904 |
| learnig | learnt 0.963, learns 0.955, learnings 0.950, learn 0.943 |
| blockchainify | blockchain 0.967, access 0.864, blockade 0.861 |
| unfriendliness | friendly 0.905, confess 0.893, unfriend 0.884 |
| pyhton | von 0.943, ton 0.941, peyton 0.939 |

The first three work because the misspelling or derivation keeps most pieces: *coffeee* shares 15 of its 22 n-grams with *coffee*, *learnig* 14 with *learning*. *Unfriendliness* is found near *friendly* but also near *confess*, which shares nothing in meaning and three pieces of its ending (`ess`, `ss>`, `ess>`) with the 50 n-grams of *unfriendliness*. *Pyhton* fails: swapping two letters breaks every n-gram that spans them, and it shares only `<py` and `on>` with *python*, 2 of 18. Subwords help with spelling variation that preserves substrings, not with every typo.

## What subwords do to neighbours

The same mechanism changes the neighbours of ordinary words. For in-vocabulary queries, FastText's nearest words are dominated by shared spelling:

| Word | FastText | Word2Vec (CBOW, same data) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| learn | learnt 0.965, learns 0.955, unlearn 0.939 | learning 0.817, beginner 0.750, teach 0.743 |
| coffee | coffees 0.914, koffee 0.897, tea 0.856 | tea 0.900, beer 0.875, starbucks 0.857 |
| india | indias 0.933, indiape 0.917, indiana 0.882 | indian 0.778, overseas 0.753, usa 0.750 |

Inflections and spelling variants move close, which is what the model is for, and so do strings that only look alike: *Indiana*, *indigo* and *Indira* are among the five nearest words to *India*. For tasks that care about morphology (syntactic analogies, rare words, morphologically rich languages) this is a gain; for tasks about topic it can be noise.

The paper measures both sides. On word similarity, computing vectors for out-of-vocabulary words raises the correlation with human judgement on the Russian HJ dataset from 59 (skip-gram) to 66, and on the English rare-words dataset from 43 to 47. On analogies, syntactic accuracy rises sharply in morphologically rich languages, from 52.8% to 77.8% in Czech and from 44.5% to 56.4% in German, and in English from 70.1% to 74.9%, while English semantic accuracy drops slightly, from 78.5% to 77.8%.

## fastText as a classifier

The same library, from the same group, includes a text classifier built on the same idea ([Joulin et al., 2017](#references)): a document is the average of the embeddings of its words, optionally with hashed word bigrams added, and a linear layer with a softmax predicts the label. It is CBOW with the label in place of the middle word. There is no hidden non-linearity; the embeddings have only 10 dimensions in the paper's experiments, and the bigrams give it some local word order at almost no cost.

On AG News, the dataset of the course's classification notes, the paper reports 91.5% test accuracy with words alone and 92.5% with bigrams, against 87.2% for a character-level CNN and 91.3% for a 29-layer one, at about a second per epoch instead of hours. A re-implementation in PyTorch (word and hashed bigram embeddings of size 10 averaged with `EmbeddingBag`, a linear layer, SGD with a linearly decaying rate chosen on 12,000 held-out training articles, 5 epochs, code below) gives the same picture:

| Model | Parameters | Seconds per epoch (CPU) | Test accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| words only, rate 0.05 | 650,474 | 2.2 | 0.914 |
| words + bigrams in 2,000,000 buckets, rate 0.1 | 20,650,474 | 3.0 | 0.922 |

For comparison, [TF-IDF with logistic regression](/notes/nlp/text-classification#results-across-the-course) reaches 0.918 on the same split and the best [CNN](/notes/nlp/cnn-text-classification#results-on-ag-news) 0.911. A linear model over averaged embeddings is a baseline worth running before anything heavier.

## What changed

| | Word2Vec / GloVe | FastText |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unit with a vector | word type | character n-grams of length 3 to 6, plus the word |
| Word vector | one row of a table | sum of 15 or so rows |
| Unseen word | no vector | sum of its known n-grams |
| Parameters | $|V| \times d$ | $(|V| + K) \times d$, with $K = 2 \cdot 10^6$ |
| Neighbours | by context | by context and by spelling |

## Limitations

- **Spelling is not meaning.** Strings that look alike get close vectors whether or not they are related (*India* and *Indiana*, *unfriendliness* and *confess*).
- **Not every typo is repaired.** A transposition breaks the n-grams it spans; *pyhton* lands next to *von* and *ton*.
- **Memory.** The bucket table dominates: 2.4 GB at $d = 300$ before any word vector.
- **Still static.** One vector per string, whatever the sentence; the [BERT note](/notes/nlp/bert#from-static-to-contextual-embeddings) removes this. Subword tokenization, used by every Transformer in the course ([tokenization note](/notes/nlp/tokenization#byte-pair-encoding)), solves the open-vocabulary problem differently: it splits a rare word into a few learned pieces instead of summing all n-grams.

## What you should remember

- A word is `<` + word + `>` cut into all n-grams of length 3 to 6, plus the word itself; its vector is the sum of their vectors, and the skip-gram loss is unchanged.
- N-grams are hashed into $2 \cdot 10^6$ buckets, so memory is fixed and unseen n-grams are impossible.
- An unseen word gets a vector from its pieces; misspellings that keep most pieces land near the right word, transpositions do not.
- Neighbours mix meaning and spelling; the gains are largest for morphology and rare words.
- The fastText classifier, averaged word and bigram embeddings with a linear softmax, reaches 0.922 on AG News in seconds per epoch.

## Code

Subword vectors with gensim, on the Quora questions tokenized as in the [GloVe note](/notes/nlp/glove#code):

```python
from gensim.models import FastText, Word2Vec

# sents: list of token lists (lower-cased, stopwords removed); run with PYTHONHASHSEED=0
ft = FastText(sents, vector_size=32, window=5, min_count=5, min_n=3, max_n=6,
              bucket=2_000_000, workers=1, seed=0)
w2v = Word2Vec(sents, vector_size=32, window=5, min_count=5, workers=1, seed=0)
for w in ['coffeee', 'learnig', 'blockchainify', 'unfriendliness', 'pyhton']:
    print(w, w in w2v.wv.key_to_index, ft.wv.most_similar(w, topn=3))
```

The fastText classifier on AG News, with words and hashed bigrams (the rates were chosen among 0.05, 0.1, 0.25 and 0.5 by training on the first 108,000 training articles and scoring the remaining 12,000):

```python
import re, zlib, numpy as np, pandas as pd, torch, torch.nn as nn

tr = pd.read_csv('ag_news_train.csv', sep='\t'); te = pd.read_csv('ag_news_test.csv', sep='\t')
toks = lambda t: re.findall(r"\w+", t.lower())
Xtr, Xte = [toks(t) for t in tr.text], [toks(t) for t in te.text]
ytr, yte = torch.tensor(tr.label.values), torch.tensor(te.label.values)
vocab = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(sorted({w for s in Xtr for w in s}))}
V, B = len(vocab), 2_000_000
BIGRAMS, LR = True, 0.1                            # words only: False, 0.05

def feats(s):                                      # word ids, then hashed bigram ids
    ids = [vocab[w] for w in s if w in vocab]
    if BIGRAMS:
        ids += [V + zlib.crc32(f'{a} {b}'.encode()) % B for a, b in zip(s, s[1:])]
    return ids

def batch(F, idx):                                 # concatenated ids and offsets for EmbeddingBag
    parts = [torch.tensor(F[i] if F[i] else [0]) for i in idx]
    return torch.cat(parts), torch.tensor([0] + [len(p) for p in parts[:-1]]).cumsum(0)

torch.manual_seed(0); rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Ftr, Fte = [feats(s) for s in Xtr], [feats(s) for s in Xte]
emb = nn.EmbeddingBag(V + (B if BIGRAMS else 0), 10, mode='mean', sparse=True)
lin = nn.Linear(10, 4)
nn.init.uniform_(emb.weight, -0.1, 0.1)
opt = torch.optim.SGD(list(emb.parameters()) + list(lin.parameters()), lr=LR)
epochs, bs = 5, 16
steps, step = epochs * ((len(Ftr) + bs - 1) // bs), 0
for epoch in range(epochs):
    order = rng.permutation(len(Ftr))
    for s in range(0, len(Ftr), bs):
        idx = order[s:s + bs]; x, off = batch(Ftr, idx)
        for g in opt.param_groups:
            g['lr'] = LR * (1 - step / steps)        # linear decay to 0
        loss = nn.functional.cross_entropy(lin(emb(x, off)), ytr[idx], reduction='sum')
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step(); step += 1

with torch.no_grad():
    x, off = batch(Fte, range(len(Fte)))
    print((lin(emb(x, off)).argmax(1) == yte).float().mean().item())
```

The run prints 0.9216 with bigrams and, with `BIGRAMS, LR = False, 0.05`, 0.9142.
