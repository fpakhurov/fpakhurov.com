Classification gives one label per document. Many questions need a label per *piece* of the document: which words name a person, an organization, a place, a date. Named entity recognition (NER) is the standard instance of **sequence labelling**, and it is where the gap between the words a dataset labels and the subword tokens a Transformer reads has to be bridged explicitly.

**Notation.** A sentence is a sequence of words $w_1, \dots, w_n$ with gold tags $y_1, \dots, y_n$; the tokenizer turns it into subword tokens $t_1, \dots, t_m$ with $m \ge n$; the encoder gives a vector $h_j$ per token; a head gives logits over the tag set at every position.

## Task formulation

Input: a sentence. Output: the set of entity spans in it, each with a type. The standard types in the CoNLL-2003 benchmark ([Tjong Kim Sang and De Meulder, 2003](#references)) are `PER`, `ORG`, `LOC` and `MISC`; the training split has 14,041 sentences, validation 3,250, test 3,453. Spans are converted into per-word tags with the **BIO scheme**: `B-X` begins an entity of type `X`, `I-X` continues it, `O` is outside any entity. With four types that is $4 \times 2 + 1 = 9$ classes.

| Token | Barack | Obama | was | born | in | Honolulu |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tag | `B-PER` | `I-PER` | `O` | `O` | `O` | `B-LOC` |

The `B`/`I` distinction is what lets two adjacent entities of the same type be told apart: `B-PER I-PER B-PER` is two people, `B-PER I-PER I-PER` is one.

## Baseline: features and a sequence model

Before neural models, NER was a conditional random field over hand-built features: capitalization, word shape, suffixes, gazetteer membership, the previous and next words ([Lafferty, McCallum and Pereira, 2001](#references)). A CRF models the *sequence* of tags jointly, so it learns transition scores: a low score for `O` followed by `I-PER` makes that transition unlikely. Making it impossible takes a hard constraint, a mask on the transitions that under the IOB2 convention lets `I-X` follow only `B-X` or `I-X`. The first neural systems kept the CRF on top of a bidirectional LSTM over word and character embeddings ([Lample et al., 2016](#references); [Huang, Xu and Yu, 2015](#references)) and reached about 90–91 $F_1$ on CoNLL-2003; capitalization and character shape were still the most useful signals, now learned.

## Token classification with a pretrained encoder

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Each token's contextual vector already encodes whether it is part of a name and of what kind. Put a linear layer with 9 outputs on every position, train it with cross-entropy against the tag of that position, and read the tags off with an argmax.</aside>

$$
\begin{gathered}
\text{logits}_j = W h_j + b \in \mathbb R^{9}, \\
\mathcal L = -\frac{1}{|J|}\sum_{j \in J} \log \operatorname{softmax}(\text{logits}_j)_{y_j},
\end{gathered}
$$

where $J$ is the set of token positions that carry a label. Shapes: ids `[B, m]` → vectors `[B, m, 768]` → logits `[B, m, 9]`, flattened to `[B·m, 9]` against `[B·m]` targets for the loss. No CRF: the encoder's context is strong enough that independent per-token decisions work, and the few invalid transitions can be fixed in post-processing.

The model is *cased*: capital letters are the strongest cue for names, and the uncased tokenizer of earlier notes would throw them away.

### Token or sequence classification

The [classification note](/notes/nlp/text-classification#the-transformer-era-approaches) reads one vector per text, the `[CLS]` vector or the mean over tokens, and maps it to $K$ logits: `[B, m, 768]` → `[B, 768]` → `[B, K]`, one label and one loss term per text. Token classification applies the same kind of linear layer at every position, `[B, m, 768]` → `[B, m, 9]`, one label and one loss term per labelled token. The encoder is the same; what changes is which vectors the head reads and how many targets a text contributes. For `Barack Obama was born in Honolulu` a sentiment classifier outputs one label; the NER head outputs one tag per word, `B-PER I-PER O O O B-LOC`, with `[CLS]`, `[SEP]` and any continuation pieces ignored.

### Aligning words and tokens

The dataset labels words; the model reads WordPiece tokens. `Hendrix penned on a piece of London hotel stationery in late 1966` becomes `Hendrix penned on a piece of London hotel station ##ery in late 1966`, and `10,925` becomes four tokens. The tokenizer's `word_ids()` gives, for each token, the index of the word it came from (`None` for `[CLS]` and `[SEP]`), and labels are assigned by rule:

- special tokens get the label $-100$, which the cross-entropy ignores;
- the first token of a word gets the word's tag;
- continuation tokens (`##ery`) get either $-100$, so that only the first piece is trained, or a copy of the word's tag. A copied `B-X` makes one entity look like several (`B-PER B-PER` is two people), so the copy should turn `B-X` into `I-X` on continuation pieces.

Training and scoring are separate rules. Whatever the training convention, the score uses one tag per word: build a mask from `word_ids()` that keeps the first token of each word and drops special tokens and continuation pieces, apply it to the predictions and the references alike, and only then pass the tag sequences to the scorer. For `London hotel station ##ery`, `word_ids()` gives 6, 7, 8, 8 and the mask keeps the first three. Dropping the $-100$ positions alone gives one tag per word only when continuation pieces were labelled $-100$.

### A frozen encoder is not enough

Freeze all 108.3 M parameters of BERT-base-cased and train the 6,921-parameter head alone (0.01 % of the model) on the CoNLL-2003 training split for two epochs at a learning rate of $5 \times 10^{-3}$, with continuation tokens given the tag of their word. The training loss ends at 0.26, and the first test sentence shows the result:

| | SOCCER | - | JAPAN | GET | LUCKY | WIN | , | CHINA | IN | SURPRISE | DEFEAT | . |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| gold | O | O | B-LOC | O | O | O | O | B-PER | O | O | O | O |
| frozen + head | O | O | B-PER | O | O | O | O | O | O | O | O | O |

The head finds `JAPAN` but gives it the wrong type, and misses `CHINA`, whose gold tag is itself a famous annotation error in this corpus (`CHINA` is a country, here a football team, not a person). On an all-capitals headline, where the casing cue is gone, a linear read-out of frozen features finds some names and cannot type them reliably; this is the linear-probing result of the [fine-tuning note](/notes/nlp/fine-tuning#linear-probing-train-only-the-head) in another task. Fine-tuning the whole encoder on CoNLL-2003 gives 92.4 $F_1$ for BERT-base ([Devlin et al., 2019](#references), Table 7); the recipe is the one from the earlier notes (learning rate $2$ to $5 \times 10^{-5}$, two to four epochs), applied to the same model with the encoder left trainable.

## Evaluation: entities, not tokens

Token accuracy is misleading twice over: most tokens are `O`, and a half-tagged entity is useless. The standard is entity-level precision, recall and $F_1$ from the [evaluation note](/notes/nlp/corpora-and-evaluation#spans-rather-than-tokens), as implemented by `seqeval`: a predicted span counts as correct only if its start, end and type all match a gold span. Predicted `B-PER I-PER` over `Barack Obama` is one true positive; `B-PER` over `Barack` alone is one false positive and one false negative. The first-token mask of the previous section is applied to predictions and references before scoring, and the tag ids are mapped back to strings because the scorer reads the `B-`/`I-` prefixes.

A per-type breakdown (PER, ORG, LOC, MISC) is standard and depends on the id-to-label mapping being the dataset's. A hand-written mapping that orders the tags differently from the dataset, for example with `B-LOC` and `B-ORG` exchanged, leaves the overall $F_1$ unaffected, because the swap is consistent, but exchanges the per-type numbers and the predicted tags between the two types. Hence the rule: take the mapping from the dataset's own label list and never write label mappings by hand.

## Why extract entities

Three uses recur. **Monitoring and analytics**: count mentions of organizations, people and places across a stream of news and link them to a knowledge base. **Slot filling** in assistants: `book a flight to Tokyo next Friday` needs `Tokyo` as a destination and `next Friday` as a date before any booking logic runs; NER is the first stage, followed by linking and normalization. **De-identification**: replace names, addresses, phone numbers and dates in text by placeholders (`[CLIENT_1]`) before the text leaves a controlled environment, for example to be sent to an external model. The last use shows the task's failure modes most sharply: a missed entity is a leak, a wrong boundary leaks half a name, the same person must get the same placeholder across a document (coreference), and the placeholders must be reversible for the authorized reader while not reconstructible from context by anyone else, which NER alone does not guarantee.

## What changed

| | Feature CRF / BiLSTM-CRF | Encoder + linear head, fine-tuned |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Features | hand-built or learned per word and character | contextual subword vectors |
| Sequence model | CRF over tags | none; context inside the encoder |
| Casing, shape | explicit features | learned, needs a cased model |
| CoNLL-2003 test $F_1$ | ≈ 90–91 | ≈ 92–93 |
| Tokens | words | subwords, aligned by `word_ids` |

## Limitations

- **Spans are flat.** BIO cannot express nested entities (`[Bank of [America]]`) or discontinuous ones; nested NER needs span-based models.
- **Types are fixed by the data.** A model trained on four coarse types cannot find drug names or product codes without new labels; zero-shot NER with prompted language models trades accuracy for flexibility.
- **Domain shift** is severe: news-trained models degrade on tweets, legal text and clinical notes, where casing and formatting differ.
- **Boundaries are fragile** at punctuation, hyphens and subword splits, and the metric is unforgiving of one-token errors.
- **The benchmark has errors** (`CHINA` as a person), so the top scores partly measure agreement with the annotators.

## What you should remember

- NER is per-token classification with BIO tags; four types give nine classes.
- Token classification puts the head on every position (`[B, m, 9]`, one loss term per labelled token); sequence classification puts it on one pooled or `[CLS]` vector (`[B, K]`, one per text). Same encoder, different read-out.
- Align word labels to subword tokens with `word_ids`: special tokens $-100$, first piece gets the tag, continuation pieces get $-100$ or the tag with `B-` turned into `I-`. Score one tag per word with a first-token mask applied to predictions and references.
- A linear head on a frozen encoder finds some names and mistypes or misses others; fine-tune the encoder (92.4 $F_1$ for BERT-base on CoNLL-2003).
- Score at the entity level (`seqeval`), per type, with label mappings taken from the dataset.
- Use a cased model; casing is the strongest cue.
