The [previous note](/notes/nlp/model-families#why-decoder-only-models-came-to-dominate) ended on scaling: next-token prediction turns every token of every document into a training signal and needs no extra machinery, so decoder-only models were the ones grown to hundreds of billions of parameters. Growing them raises questions that the architecture does not answer. How fast does the loss fall as the model, the data and the compute grow? Given a fixed compute budget, should it buy a larger model or more training tokens? And at what size does a language model become a *large* language model? This note answers the first two with the scaling laws of [Kaplan et al. (2020)](#references) and [Hoffmann et al. (2022)](#references), revises the answer once more for the cost of running the model, and then answers the third: there is no threshold, and general-purpose LLMs came from a combination of changes, not from size alone.

**Notation.** $N$ is the number of parameters, $D$ the number of training tokens, $C$ the training compute in floating-point operations (FLOPs). $L$ is the cross-entropy per token in nats on held-out text from the training distribution, the loss of the [GPT note](/notes/nlp/gpt#the-objective). Kaplan's power laws have exponents $\alpha_N$ and $\alpha_D$; the fit of Hoffmann et al. has exponents $\alpha$ (parameters) and $\beta$ (data) and constants $E$, $A$, $B$, defined with the formula. $D_{\text{inf}}$ is the number of tokens a model processes after training, at inference.

## Baseline: a bigger model on about 300 billion tokens

Between 2020 and early 2022 the largest dense language models grew from 175 to 530 billion parameters while their training data stayed near 300 billion tokens: GPT-3 175B on 300B, Gopher 280B on 300B, MT-NLG 530B on 270B ([Hoffmann et al., 2022](#references), Table 1). The rule behind this came from the first scaling laws ([Kaplan et al., 2020](#references), §6): when compute grows, spend most of it on parameters. The baseline is therefore: keep the data roughly where the previous model had it, and make the model as large as the budget allows.

Larger models are indeed better. What the baseline lacks is a way to compare a larger model trained on fewer tokens with a smaller model trained on more, at equal cost. That comparison needs a model of the loss as a function of both $N$ and $D$, and a count of what each choice costs.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Under a fixed budget $C \approx 6ND$, a larger model means fewer training tokens. The loss has one term that falls with $N$ and one that falls with $D$, so there is a best split, and it can be computed from a fitted formula before the expensive run. Measured carefully, the best split grows $N$ and $D$ in roughly equal proportion, about 20 tokens per parameter, and the models of 2020–2021 were too large for their data. If the model will also serve many requests, the best model is smaller still and trained on more tokens.</aside>

## What is counted: parameters, tokens and compute

A training run is summarized by three numbers. $N$ counts the weights. Kaplan et al. exclude the embedding matrices, because the loss trends are cleaner without them (§2.1); Hoffmann et al. count all parameters, embeddings included (Appendix F). $D$ counts the tokens processed during training; when each token is seen once, it is also the amount of data used. $C$ counts the arithmetic.

**Where $C \approx 6ND$ comes from.** In a matrix product each weight is used once per token, for one multiplication and one addition. A forward pass therefore costs about two FLOPs per parameter per token. Backpropagation computes, for every weight matrix, two products of the same size as the forward one: the gradient with respect to the layer's input, passed on to the layer below, and the gradient with respect to the weights. So the backward pass costs about twice the forward pass, and over $D$ tokens

$$
\boxed{\;C \approx (\underbrace{2N}_{\text{forward}} + \underbrace{4N}_{\text{backward}})\, D = 6ND.\;}
$$

The approximation leaves out the attention scores, whose cost per token grows with the context length instead of with $N$: about $2\, n_{\text{layer}}\, n_{\text{ctx}}\, d_{\text{model}}$ extra FLOPs per token in the forward pass, small when $d_{\text{model}}$ is large compared with $n_{\text{ctx}}/12$ ([Kaplan et al., 2020](#references), Eq. 2.2 and Table 1). Hoffmann et al. count every operation for six model sizes and find their totals within 10 % of $6ND$ (ratios from 0.99 to 1.10, Table A4).

**Worked example.** GPT-3 has $N = 1.75 \times 10^{11}$ and $D = 3 \times 10^{11}$, so $6ND = 6 \times 1.75 \times 10^{11} \times 3 \times 10^{11} = 3.15 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs; the GPT-3 paper reports $3.14 \times 10^{23}$ with $N$ = 174.6 billion ([Brown et al., 2020](#references), Table D.1). At inference only the forward pass runs, so one token through GPT-3 costs about $2N = 3.5 \times 10^{11}$ FLOPs.

The count fixes the trade-off: for a given $C$, choosing $N$ fixes $D = C/(6N)$, and a model twice as large sees half as many tokens. The count is the same on any hardware. It measures operations, not time; how many operations per second a GPU sustains is a matter of implementation, the subject of the [efficient Transformers note](/notes/nlp/efficient-transformers).

## Loss falls as a power law

Kaplan et al. trained decoder-only Transformers with 768 to 1.5 billion non-embedding parameters on WebText2, a web corpus of $2.29 \times 10^{10}$ tokens (§2.2–2.3), and measured the test loss. When one resource limits the model, the loss follows a power law in that resource (§1.2, Eqs. 1.1–1.2):

$$
\begin{gathered}
L(N) = \Big(\frac{N_c}{N}\Big)^{\alpha_N}, \qquad \alpha_N \approx 0.076, \quad N_c \approx 8.8 \times 10^{13}, \\[4pt]
L(D) = \Big(\frac{D_c}{D}\Big)^{\alpha_D}, \qquad \alpha_D \approx 0.095, \quad D_c \approx 5.4 \times 10^{13}.
\end{gathered}
$$

$L(N)$ describes models trained to convergence on enough data; $L(D)$ describes large models trained on $D$ tokens with early stopping. Taking logarithms, $\log L = \alpha_N (\log N_c - \log N)$: a straight line of slope $-\alpha_N$ on log–log axes. The constants $N_c$ and $D_c$ depend on the tokenizer and have no meaning of their own (§1.2); the exponents carry the content.

**Worked example.** Doubling $N$ multiplies the loss by $2^{-0.076} = 0.949$, about 5 % lower; ten times more parameters multiply it by $10^{-0.076} = 0.839$. A model with $1.5 \times 10^{8}$ non-embedding parameters is predicted at $(8.8 \times 10^{13} / 1.5 \times 10^{8})^{0.076} = 2.74$ nats per token, one with $1.5 \times 10^{9}$ at $2.30$. Each tenfold step buys the same factor, so every further reduction by that factor costs ten times more parameters than the last.

Two properties matter for the rest of the note. The laws are **smooth**: they held across six orders of magnitude in $N$ and eight in compute, with no sign of a change of regime at the large end (§1.1–1.2), and width, depth and number of heads mattered little at fixed $N$ (§3.1). And they come with an **allocation rule**: for compute-efficient training, the optimal model size grew as $N \propto C^{0.73}$ and the data only as $D \propto C^{0.27}$ (§6.1, Eq. 6.1). Ten times more compute should then buy a model $10^{0.73} = 5.4$ times larger and only $10^{0.27} = 1.9$ times more tokens, and training should stop well short of convergence. That is the baseline above.

## Where a language model becomes a large one

A language model is any model of $P(w_t \mid w_{<t})$: the [n-gram model](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models), the [recurrent model](/notes/nlp/recurrent-networks#rnn-as-a-language-model) and GPT all qualify. "Large language model" adds an adjective to that definition, not a new kind of object, and nothing in the models themselves marks where the adjective starts to apply.

- **The objective does not change.** A 124-million-parameter GPT-2 and a 175-billion-parameter GPT-3 minimize the same per-token cross-entropy.
- **The architecture barely changes.** GPT-3's block is GPT-2's, with sparse attention in alternate layers ([GPT note](/notes/nlp/gpt#from-gpt-1-to-gpt-3)).
- **The loss changes smoothly.** The power laws above have no break, no size at which the curve bends.
- **The word moves.** The 340-million-parameter encoder of 2018 was named BERT-large ([Devlin et al., 2019](#references)); in 2022 the "current LLMs" of Hoffmann et al. (Table 1) ranged from 137 to 530 billion parameters; models of a few billion parameters are now often called small.

Abilities are a harder case. Some benchmark scores stay near chance for small models and rise sharply above some size. [Wei et al. (2022)](#references) call such abilities *emergent*: present in larger models, absent in smaller ones, and therefore not predictable by extrapolating the smaller models. [Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023)](#references) argue that many of these jumps are produced by the metric: a score such as exact match, which gives credit only when the whole answer is right, turns a gradual improvement into a sudden one, and the same model outputs scored with a continuous metric improve smoothly. Whether every reported jump dissolves this way is still debated. The loss, which is what scaling laws describe, improves smoothly either way.

So "LLM" names a regime, not a class: a language model large enough, and trained on enough varied text, that it can be used for many tasks by prompting. What put models into that regime is the subject of [the last section before the summary](#what-made-language-models-general-purpose). First, how to spend a compute budget well.

## Compute-optimal training

### One formula for both resources

[Hoffmann et al. (2022)](#references) trained over 400 models, from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters, on 5 to 500 billion tokens of their MassiveText corpus, and fitted the final loss with one formula in both resources (§3.3, Eq. 2):

$$
\boxed{\;L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}\;}
$$

Each term has a meaning (Appendix D.2):

- $E$ is the loss of the ideal model of the data distribution, the entropy of the text itself. No model goes below it.
- $A/N^{\alpha}$ is the excess loss of the best model with $N$ parameters trained on unlimited data: the price of a finite model.
- $B/D^{\beta}$ is the excess loss of training on only $D$ tokens, each seen once: the price of finite training.

The fitted values, with the loss in nats per token, are (Appendix D.2, Eq. 10)

$$
E = 1.69, \qquad A = 406.4, \qquad B = 410.7, \qquad \alpha = 0.34, \qquad \beta = 0.28 .
$$

The fit minimizes a robust (Huber) loss between the predicted and the observed log-losses of the runs (§3.3, Eq. 3). For Chinchilla, 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens, it gives $L = 1.69 + 0.0835 + 0.1632 = 1.9366$.

### Splitting a budget

With $C$ fixed, substitute $D = C/(6N)$, so that the loss depends on $N$ alone; set the derivative to zero; collect the powers of $N$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(N) &= E + A N^{-\alpha} + B \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{-\beta} N^{\beta}
&& \text{substitute } D = \tfrac{C}{6N} \\[4pt]
0 = \frac{dL}{dN} &= -\alpha A N^{-\alpha - 1} + \beta B \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{-\beta} N^{\beta - 1}
&& \text{differentiate} \\[4pt]
\alpha A N^{-\alpha} &= \beta B \Big(\frac{C}{6N}\Big)^{-\beta} = \beta B D^{-\beta}
&& \text{multiply by } N \\[4pt]
N^{\alpha + \beta} &= \frac{\alpha A}{\beta B} \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\beta}
&& \text{collect the powers of } N .
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the $(\alpha + \beta)$-th root gives the compute-optimal split (Hoffmann et al., Eq. 4):

$$
\boxed{\;
\begin{gathered}
N_{\text{opt}} = G \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha + \beta}}, \qquad
G = \Big(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha + \beta}}, \\[4pt]
D_{\text{opt}} = \frac{C}{6 N_{\text{opt}}} = G^{-1} \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}} .
\end{gathered}
\;}
$$

The stationary point is the minimum. In $x = \ln N$ the loss is $E + A e^{-\alpha x} + B (C/6)^{-\beta} e^{\beta x}$, a sum of two [convex functions](/notes/math/convex-sets-and-functions#operations-that-preserve-convexity) of $x$, so it has exactly one minimum and no other stationary point.

The third line of the derivation says more than the final formula: at the optimum, $\alpha \cdot A/N^{\alpha} = \beta \cdot B/D^{\beta}$. The two excess terms stand in the ratio $\beta : \alpha$, and neither resource is left as the bottleneck.

With the fitted constants, $\beta/(\alpha + \beta) = 0.28/0.62 = 0.452$ and $\alpha/(\alpha + \beta) = 0.548$. Each tenfold increase of compute multiplies the optimal model by $10^{0.452} = 2.83$ and its data by $10^{0.548} = 3.53$: both grow at almost the same rate, against Kaplan's 0.73 and 0.27.

**Worked example: the Gopher budget.** $C = 5.76 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs, so $C/6 = 9.6 \times 10^{22}$.

1. $\alpha A = 0.34 \times 406.4 = 138.18$ and $\beta B = 0.28 \times 410.7 = 115.00$. Their ratio is $1.2016$, and $G = 1.2016^{1/0.62} = 1.345$.
2. $\log_{10}(9.6 \times 10^{22}) = 22.982$; times $0.28/0.62 = 0.4516$ this is $10.379$, so $(C/6)^{0.4516} = 2.394 \times 10^{10}$.
3. $N_{\text{opt}} = 1.345 \times 2.394 \times 10^{10} = 3.22 \times 10^{10}$ and $D_{\text{opt}} = 9.6 \times 10^{22} / 3.22 \times 10^{10} = 2.98 \times 10^{12}$: 32 billion parameters on 3.0 trillion tokens, 93 tokens per parameter.
4. Check: $A/N^{\alpha} = 0.109$ and $B/D^{\beta} = 0.132$, in the ratio $0.823 = 0.28/0.34$. The loss is $1.69 + 0.109 + 0.132 = 1.931$.

### Three estimates and the rule of 20 tokens per parameter

The parametric fit is one of three estimates in the paper, each a different analysis of the same kind of runs (§3.1–3.3, Table 2). Approach 1 takes, for every compute value, the lowest loss reached by any run, the envelope of all training curves. Approach 2 trains several model sizes at each of nine fixed budgets from $6 \times 10^{18}$ to $3 \times 10^{21}$ FLOPs and fits a parabola to each iso-FLOP curve. Approach 3 is the formula above.

| Estimate | Exponent $a$ in $N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{a}$ | Exponent $b$ in $D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{b}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Approach 1: envelope of training curves | 0.50 | 0.50 |
| Approach 2: iso-FLOP profiles | 0.49 | 0.51 |
| Approach 3: parametric fit | 0.46 | 0.54 |
| Kaplan et al. (2020) | 0.73 | 0.27 |

With $a = b = 0.5$ the ratio $D/N$ does not depend on the budget, and Approach 1 puts it near 20: 1 billion parameters on 20.2 billion tokens, 10 billion on 205 billion, 67 billion on 1.5 trillion (Table 3). This is the rule of **20 tokens per parameter**, and Chinchilla, 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens, follows it. The parametric fit with the published constants gives more tokens per parameter, and a ratio that grows with the budget: 50 at $10^{21}$ FLOPs, 93 at the Gopher budget. The paper notes that Approach 3 predicts smaller models than the other two at large budgets (§3.4).

**Why Kaplan's exponent was different.** Hoffmann et al. give two reasons (§2). Kaplan et al. used one learning-rate schedule and one token count for all runs and read off losses part of the way through. With a cosine schedule set for 130 billion tokens, the loss after fewer tokens overestimates what a run planned for that shorter length would reach, so shorter runs on less data looked worse than they are, which pushed the conclusion toward growing the model faster than the data. And most of Kaplan's runs were small, many under 100 million parameters, while the frontier curves slightly at larger sizes (Appendix E). The two analyses also count $N$ differently, without and with embeddings. None of these is an architecture change: all concern the training procedure and how the experiments were read.

### How much the answer depends on the constants

*Optional depth: why the parametric fit and the rule of 20 tokens disagree. The rest of the note does not depend on it.*

The printed constants are rounded. The LaTeX source of the paper keeps the unrounded fit in a comment, $E = 1.6934$, $\alpha = 0.3392$, $\beta = 0.2849$, with $A$ and $B$ unchanged at four digits; [Besiroglu et al. (2024)](#references) pointed out that the rounding matters. At the Gopher budget the unrounded values give $N_{\text{opt}} = 40$ billion and 59 tokens per parameter, the 40 billion that the paper's Figure 4 reports; the rounded values give 32 billion and 93. The exponent $\beta/(\alpha + \beta)$ moves only from 0.452 to 0.457, but it multiplies $\ln(C/6) \approx 53$.

Besiroglu et al. also refitted the formula to the runs as reconstructed from the paper's Figure 4 and obtained $E = 1.82$, $A = 482.0$, $B = 2085.4$, $\alpha = 0.348$, $\beta = 0.366$ (their Table 1). With these constants the optimum at the Gopher budget is 72 billion parameters on 1.3 trillion tokens, 18 tokens per parameter, in line with Approaches 1 and 2 and with how Chinchilla was trained. They report that the original fit stopped its optimizer before convergence, an explanation confirmed by one of the paper's authors.

The lesson holds for any scaling law: the exponents are estimated on runs two to four orders of magnitude smaller than the run being planned, and small errors in them become large errors after extrapolation. The direction of the result, many more tokens per parameter than the models of 2020–2021 used, holds under all three estimates and both fits; the exact ratio does not.

## Gopher and Chinchilla on the same budget

The prediction was tested at full scale (§4). Gopher ([Rae et al., 2021](#references)) has 280 billion parameters and was trained on 300 billion tokens. Chinchilla has 70 billion parameters and was trained on 1.4 trillion tokens of the same corpus, for the same budget by the paper's own count of $5.76 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs; the $6ND$ shortcut gives $5.0 \times 10^{23}$ and $5.9 \times 10^{23}$. Both have 80 layers; Chinchilla has half the width, $d_{\text{model}} = 8{,}192$ against $16{,}384$ (Table 4).

Not everything else was equal (§4.1, Appendix G). Chinchilla was trained with AdamW instead of Adam, with a tokenizer that skips NFKC normalization, on a slightly different mixture of the same corpus, and with a float32 copy of the weights in the optimizer state. The comparison therefore changes model size, data volume and training procedure together; it is a demonstration at scale, and the controlled evidence is the iso-FLOP experiments of the previous section.

Evaluated as in the Gopher paper, Chinchilla scored higher on most tasks: 67.6 % against 60.0 % average 5-shot accuracy over the 57 tasks of MMLU (Table 6), and 65.1 % against 54.4 % average accuracy over 62 BIG-bench tasks (§4.2.4). Being four times smaller, it is also four times cheaper per token at inference: $2N = 1.4 \times 10^{11}$ FLOPs against $5.6 \times 10^{11}$.

## Compute-optimal is not inference-optimal

Compute-optimal answers one question: the lowest loss for a given training budget. A deployed model is trained once and then runs on every request, at about $2N$ FLOPs per token read or generated. A model that processes $D_{\text{inf}}$ tokens over its lifetime costs in total ([Sardana et al., 2024](#references), §2, Eq. 3)

$$
C_{\text{total}} = 6 N D + 2 N D_{\text{inf}} .
$$

The second term grows with $N$ and does not depend on $D$. For a fixed target quality, a smaller model can reach the target by training on more tokens: it pays more in the first term and saves in the second. The best size therefore falls below the compute-optimal one, and further the more the model will be used.

**Worked example.** Take Chinchilla's fitted loss, $L_{\text{target}} = 1.937$, as the target. Solving the fit for $D$ gives the tokens that a model of $N$ parameters needs:

$$
D(N) = \Big(\frac{B}{L_{\text{target}} - E - A/N^{\alpha}}\Big)^{1/\beta},
$$

defined when $A/N^{\alpha}$ is below $L_{\text{target}} - E = 0.247$, that is for $N$ above 2.9 billion. Under the fit, a smaller model cannot reach this loss on any amount of data.

- **Training only** ($D_{\text{inf}} = 0$): the cheapest model has 30 billion parameters and needs 2.73 trillion tokens, $4.92 \times 10^{23}$ FLOPs. By the fit, Chinchilla itself spent 20 % more, $5.88 \times 10^{23}$, to reach the same loss.
- **Serving $10^{13}$ tokens afterwards**: the cheapest model has 15.8 billion parameters and needs 6.05 trillion tokens, 382 per parameter. Its training costs $5.75 \times 10^{23}$, 17 % more than the 30-billion model's, but the total is $8.92 \times 10^{23}$ against $10.92 \times 10^{23}$: 18 % less.

<InferenceCost />

This is the reasoning behind LLaMA ([Touvron et al., 2023](#references), §1): for a target level of performance, "the preferred model is not the fastest to train but the fastest at inference". LLaMA trained 6.7 billion parameters on 1 trillion tokens, about 150 per parameter (Table 2). The paper sets this against the Chinchilla recommendation of 200 billion tokens for a 10-billion model and reports that the 7B model was still improving after 1 trillion tokens; its 13B model outperformed GPT-3 175B on most of the benchmarks in the paper. [Sardana et al. (2024)](#references) build the trade-off into the scaling law and conclude that a model expected to serve on the order of a billion requests should be trained smaller and longer than Chinchilla-optimal; in their own runs, quality kept improving up to 10,000 tokens per parameter. Later open models went further: Llama 2 was trained on 2 trillion tokens and Llama 3 on 15 trillion (as listed by Sardana et al., §1).

Over-training is a change of training procedure motivated by a systems cost. The architecture is untouched: the model is smaller and sees far more data, which moves the burden to the data pipeline. Inference compute can also be spent per question, by sampling several answers or reasoning longer; how that trades against a larger model is the subject of [Reasoning models and test-time compute](/notes/agents/reasoning-models#compute-optimal-allocation).

## What made language models general-purpose

Scale explains why the loss falls; it does not by itself explain why one model can be asked to translate, summarize, write code and answer questions. General-purpose LLMs came from several changes of different kinds, layered on an architecture that stayed almost the same.

| Ingredient | Kind of change | What it contributed | Where in the course |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Parameters, data and compute grown together | Training procedure (budget) | The loss falls predictably; compute-optimal and inference-aware budgets | This note |
| Broad, filtered, deduplicated web-scale text | Data | Many domains, languages and kinds of task in one corpus | [Pretraining data](/notes/nlp/pretraining-data) |
| Prompting and in-context learning | Use of the model, no training change | A task stated in the prompt, with a few examples, instead of a new head | [GPT note](/notes/nlp/gpt#from-gpt-1-to-gpt-3) |
| Instruction tuning and preference optimization | Training procedure after pretraining | Answers instead of continuations; follows the request and stops | [Instruction tuning](/notes/nlp/instruction-tuning#the-stages-of-llm-training) |
| Efficient kernels, parallelism, sparse layers | Implementation and architecture | Makes the scale affordable to train and to run | [Efficient Transformers](/notes/nlp/efficient-transformers) |
| The block itself | Architecture, small refinements | Pre-norm, RMSNorm, RoPE, gated FFN, grouped-query attention | [Transformer note](/notes/nlp/transformers) |

No single row suffices. GPT-3 had the scale, the data and in-context learning, yet as a base model it continues a text instead of answering it (the [GPT note](/notes/nlp/gpt#what-a-base-model-does) shows the behaviour). Post-training changes that with comparatively little compute: in the human evaluations of [Ouyang et al. (2022)](#references), on their distribution of prompts (written by labelers or submitted to the OpenAI API), labelers preferred the outputs of a 1.3-billion-parameter instruction-tuned model to those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3. But post-training starts from what pretraining put into the weights, and the scaling laws govern that part. The answer to "when does a language model become an LLM" is therefore a combination: enough scale and broad enough data that prompting works, plus the post-training that makes the model follow the prompt.

## Limitations

- **A fit, not a law of nature.** The constants belong to one training distribution, tokenizer and recipe (Kaplan et al. note that $N_c$ and $D_c$ depend on the vocabulary). A different corpus, architecture or optimizer moves them, and comparing losses across tokenizers is not meaningful.
- **Extrapolation over orders of magnitude.** The fits come from models up to 16 billion parameters and are used to plan much larger runs. Rounding two exponents in the third decimal moved the Gopher-budget answer from 40 to 32 billion.
- **Loss is not ability.** The laws predict the cross-entropy; benchmark scores depend on the metric and can change abruptly where the loss changes smoothly.
- **Every token is assumed new.** The data term describes one pass over fresh data (Hoffmann et al. analyse the regime where the corpus is larger than the training run). Repeated data gives less, and the stock of high-quality text is finite.
- **FLOPs are not time or money.** $C$ ignores memory traffic, communication and hardware utilization, and $2N$ per token ignores the cost of long contexts and of the key–value cache at inference ([GPT note](/notes/nlp/gpt#making-generation-faster)).
- **The inference-aware optimum needs a forecast** of how many tokens the model will serve, made before training.

## What you should remember

- $C \approx 6ND$: two FLOPs per parameter per token forward, four backward; inference costs about $2N$ per token.
- The loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute. Nothing in the curves, the objective or the architecture marks where a language model becomes a large one; "LLM" names a regime.
- Chinchilla's fit: $L = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}$. Under $C = 6ND$ the optimum balances $\alpha A/N^{\alpha} = \beta B/D^{\beta}$, and $N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\beta/(\alpha + \beta)}$.
- Compute-optimal training scales parameters and tokens about equally, roughly 20 tokens per parameter. A 70 B model on 1.4 T tokens beat a 280 B model on 300 B tokens at the same budget. Kaplan's 0.73 came from the experimental design.
- Scaling-law constants are fragile under extrapolation; the direction of the result is robust, the exact ratio is not.
- A model that will serve many tokens should be smaller and trained longer than compute-optimal (LLaMA).
- General-purpose LLMs combine scale, broad data, prompting and post-training on an almost unchanged architecture.

## Code

The budget split for a given $C$, by direct search along the iso-FLOP curve and by the closed form; Gopher and Chinchilla on the fit; the sensitivity to the constants; and the inference-aware example. Standard library only:

```python
import math

# L(N, D) = E + A / N^alpha + B / D^beta, in nats per token (Hoffmann et al., 2022, Approach 3)
FITS = {
    'published': (1.69, 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28),            # the constants printed in the paper
    'unrounded': (1.6934, 406.4, 410.7, 0.3392, 0.2849),      # the same fit before rounding
    'refit':     (1.8172, 482.01, 2085.43, 0.3478, 0.3658),   # Besiroglu et al., 2024
}

def loss(N, D, E, A, B, alpha, beta):
    return E + A / N**alpha + B / D**beta

def best_split(C, fit):
    """Minimize the loss along D = C / (6N) by ternary search over log N (the loss is convex in log N)."""
    f = lambda x: loss(math.exp(x), C / (6 * math.exp(x)), *fit)
    lo, hi = math.log(1e6), math.log(1e14)
    for _ in range(200):
        m1, m2 = lo + (hi - lo) / 3, hi - (hi - lo) / 3
        lo, hi = (lo, m2) if f(m1) < f(m2) else (m1, hi)
    N = math.exp((lo + hi) / 2)
    return N, C / (6 * N)

def closed_form(C, fit):
    E, A, B, alpha, beta = fit
    G = (alpha * A / (beta * B)) ** (1 / (alpha + beta))
    N = G * (C / 6) ** (beta / (alpha + beta))
    return N, C / (6 * N)

fit = FITS['published']
print(f'6ND for GPT-3: {6 * 175e9 * 300e9:.3g}')
# 6ND for GPT-3: 3.15e+23

for C in (1e21, 5.76e23, 1e25):                                        # 5.76e23 = the Gopher budget
    N, D = best_split(C, fit)
    print(f'C={C:.3g}  N={N:.3g}  D={D:.3g}  D/N={D / N:.0f}  L={loss(N, D, *fit):.4f}  '
          f'closed form N={closed_form(C, fit)[0]:.3g}')
# C=1e+21  N=1.82e+09  D=9.14e+10  D/N=50  L=2.3289  closed form N=1.82e+09
# C=5.76e+23  N=3.22e+10  D=2.98e+12  D/N=93  L=1.9307  closed form N=3.22e+10
# C=1e+25  N=1.17e+11  D=1.43e+13  D/N=122  L=1.8453  closed form N=1.17e+11

E, A, B, alpha, beta = fit
for name, N, D in (('Gopher', 280e9, 300e9), ('Chinchilla', 70e9, 1.4e12)):
    print(f'{name:10s} 6ND={6 * N * D:.3g}  N-term={A / N**alpha:.4f}  D-term={B / D**beta:.4f}  '
          f'L={loss(N, D, *fit):.4f}')
# Gopher     6ND=5.04e+23  N-term=0.0521  D-term=0.2511  L=1.9933
# Chinchilla 6ND=5.88e+23  N-term=0.0835  D-term=0.1632  L=1.9366
C = 5.76e23                                                            # the iso-FLOP curve of that budget is shallow near its minimum
print('  '.join(f'{N / 1e9:.0f}B: {loss(N, C / (6 * N), *fit):.4f}' for N in (20e9, 32.2e9, 70e9, 280e9)))
# 20B: 1.9334  32B: 1.9307  70B: 1.9376  280B: 1.9840

for name, f in FITS.items():                                           # how much the answer depends on the constants
    N, D = best_split(5.76e23, f)
    print(f'{name:9s} N={N:.3g}  D={D:.3g}  D/N={D / N:.0f}')
# published N=3.22e+10  D=2.98e+12  D/N=93
# unrounded N=4.03e+10  D=2.38e+12  D/N=59
# refit     N=7.22e+10  D=1.33e+12  D/N=18

# reach Chinchilla's fitted loss at the lowest total cost 6 N D_train + 2 N D_inference
target = loss(70e9, 1.4e12, *fit)
def tokens_needed(N):
    gap = target - E - A / N**alpha
    return (B / gap) ** (1 / beta) if gap > 0 else math.inf
print(f'target L={target:.4f}  smallest N that can reach it: {(A / (target - E)) ** (1 / alpha):.3g}')
# target L=1.9366  smallest N that can reach it: 2.89e+09
for D_inf in (0, 1e13):
    cost = lambda N: 6 * N * tokens_needed(N) + 2 * N * D_inf
    N = min((10 ** (9 + k / 1000) for k in range(3001)), key=cost)     # N from 1e9 to 1e12
    D = tokens_needed(N)
    print(f'D_inf={D_inf:.0e}  N={N:.3g}  D={D:.3g}  D/N={D / N:.0f}  train={6 * N * D:.3g}  total={cost(N):.3g}')
# D_inf=0e+00  N=3e+10  D=2.73e+12  D/N=91  train=4.92e+23  total=4.92e+23
# D_inf=1e+13  N=1.58e+10  D=6.05e+12  D/N=382  train=5.75e+23  total=8.92e+23
```

The direct search and the closed form agree at every budget, which checks the derivation. Changing `fit` to `FITS['refit']` reproduces the rule of about 20 tokens per parameter at every budget in the first loop.

## Next

Compute-optimal training makes the data grow as fast as the model, and inference-aware training asks for more still: hundreds of tokens per parameter, trillions of tokens in all. Hoffmann et al. draw the conclusion themselves: further scaling depends on larger high-quality datasets, and their fit assumes every token is new and useful.
