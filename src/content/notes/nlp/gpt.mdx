The encoder of the [previous note](/notes/nlp/bert) fills in blanks and cannot continue a text. Generation needs the factorization of the [n-gram note](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models#from-a-joint-probability-to-a-product-of-conditionals), $P(w_{1:T}) = \prod_t P(w_t \mid w_{<t})$, with a model that conditions on the whole prefix, or as much of it as its context window holds. A Transformer decoder with a causal mask is exactly that model, and training it on a large corpus is what the GPT series did ([Radford et al., 2018](#references), [2019](#references); [Brown et al., 2020](#references)). This note covers the objective, the architecture in two concrete sizes, the behaviour of the resulting base model, and the decoding step that every use of it goes through.

**Notation.** Tokens $w_{1:T}$; logits $z_t \in \mathbb R^{|V|}$ at position $t$; $P(w \mid w_{<t}) = \operatorname{softmax}(z_t)_w$. Temperature $\tau$.

## Baseline: the recurrent language model

The [RNN note](/notes/nlp/recurrent-networks#rnn-as-a-language-model) already trains a next-token predictor by teacher forcing. Its context is the whole prefix, through a state that forgets, and it processes positions one after another. Replacing the recurrence with causal self-attention keeps the objective and removes both constraints, at the price of a fixed context window (1,024 positions for GPT-2 small): attention reads every earlier position inside the window directly, but the history is not unbounded.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Stack decoder blocks whose self-attention is masked so that position $t$ sees positions $\le t$. One forward pass over a training sequence then produces, at every position, a prediction for the next token with no leakage, and the loss at all $T$ positions is computed at once.</aside>

## The objective

$$
\mathcal L = -\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(w_t \mid w_{<t}),
$$

the per-token cross-entropy, whose exponential is the perplexity. The input is the sequence, the target is the sequence shifted left by one, and two mechanisms work together. **Teacher forcing** supplies the gold prefix: the prediction at position $t$ is conditioned on the true $w_{<t}$, not on the model's own earlier outputs, as in the recurrent model. The **causal mask** keeps the later gold tokens out of reach: position $t$ cannot attend to $w_{t+1}$ or anything after it, so one forward pass over the whole gold sequence computes all the predictions at once without leakage. In the scores of the [self-attention note](/notes/nlp/attention#masks), every entry $s_{tj}$ with key position $j > t$ is set to $-\infty$ before the softmax, so its weight is exactly 0. Without the mask the input at position $t + 1$ is the target of position $t$; one attention head copying it would drive the loss to zero without the model learning anything, which is the failure the [BERT note](/notes/nlp/bert#baseline-a-language-model-objective-on-an-encoder) starts from.

**Worked example (three positions).** For the sequence `the cat sat down`, the input is `the cat sat` and the target `cat sat down`:

| Position | Input token | May attend to | Predicts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `the` | `the` | `cat` |
| 2 | `cat` | `the cat` | `sat` |
| 3 | `sat` | `the cat sat` | `down` |

Every row reads gold tokens only (teacher forcing), and no row reads its own target or anything after it (the mask).

All positions are therefore trained in parallel on the same forward pass. This is the practical advantage over the recurrent model that the [Transformer note](/notes/nlp/transformers) promised: training cost per sequence is one pass of $T$ positions in parallel, not $T$ sequential steps.

Nothing else is needed. No masking rule, no segment pairs, no auxiliary task: every token in the corpus is a training example, which is why decoder-only pretraining is more sample-efficient than MLM and why it scaled first.

## From GPT-1 to GPT-3

The three GPT papers use one recipe: a decoder-only Transformer trained on next-token prediction. What changed from paper to paper is the scale, the data and, above all, how the pretrained model is used.

| | GPT-1 (2018) | GPT-2 (2019) | GPT-3 (2020) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Largest model | 12 blocks, $d = 768$ | 48 blocks, $d = 1{,}600$, 1.5 B parameters | 96 blocks, $d = 12{,}288$, 175 B parameters |
| Context | 512 tokens | 1,024 | 2,048 |
| Pretraining data | BooksCorpus, over 7,000 unpublished books | WebText: just over 8 M documents, 40 GB, from outbound Reddit links with at least 3 karma | 300 B tokens drawn from filtered Common Crawl, WebText2, two book corpora and Wikipedia |
| Block | The original decoder block, post-norm | Pre-norm, an extra LayerNorm after the last block, residual weights scaled at initialization | As GPT-2, with dense and locally banded sparse attention in alternate layers |
| Use on a task | Fine-tuned on each task | Zero-shot: the task is written as text that the model continues | Zero-, one- and few-shot: solved examples in the prompt, no weight updates |

**GPT-1** ([Radford et al., 2018](#references), §3) showed that generative pretraining helps understanding tasks. The decoder was pretrained on books, then fine-tuned as a whole, with one linear layer added, on each labelled task; structured inputs such as sentence pairs were written as one sequence with delimiter tokens. BERT followed the same pretrain-then-fine-tune pattern a few months later, with an encoder.

**GPT-2** ([Radford et al., 2019](#references), §2–3) dropped the fine-tuning. Trained on a larger and more varied web corpus, the model performed some tasks when the task was phrased as a text to be continued: appending `TL;DR:` to an article and sampling gave a summary (§3.6), and conditioning on example pairs `english sentence = french sentence` followed by a final `english sentence =` gave a translation (§3.7). The quality was far below supervised systems on most tasks, 5 BLEU for English to French for instance, but it rose steadily with model size, and even the largest model still underfit WebText.

**GPT-3** ([Brown et al., 2020](#references), §2) kept the architecture, made the model about a hundred times larger, and measured **in-context learning**. The prompt holds a short task description and $K$ solved examples, typically 10 to 100 to fill the 2,048-token context, followed by one unsolved example; the model completes it, and its weights are never updated. With $K = 0$ this is the zero-shot setting of GPT-2, with $K = 1$ one-shot. Larger models gained more from the examples in the prompt than smaller ones (Figure 1.2 of the paper); on some benchmarks few-shot GPT-3 came close to or matched the fine-tuned systems of the time, on others it stayed far behind, and the paper reports both. What the examples in a prompt contribute is discussed in [The language model as a policy](/notes/agents/language-model-as-a-policy#prompting-as-conditioning).

The architecture did not drive this sequence: GPT-3's block is GPT-2's, and GPT-2's is GPT-1's with the normalization moved. What moved the use of the model from fine-tuning to prompting was scale and data. How the recipes of both BERT and GPT were refined after these first versions is the subject of the [evolution note](/notes/nlp/evolution-of-pretrained-models).

## Two models by the numbers

Two decoder-only models of different generations make the architecture concrete.

**GPT-2 small** ([Radford et al., 2019](#references)): byte-level BPE vocabulary of 50,257, learned positions up to 1,024, 12 pre-norm blocks with $d = 768$, 12 heads and $d_{\text{ff}} = 3{,}072$. The attention projections are fused into one $768 \times 2{,}304$ matrix producing $Q, K, V$ together. Parameters: $12 \times 12 \times 768^2 \approx 85$ M in the blocks plus $50{,}257 \times 768 \approx 38.6$ M in the embedding, which is also the output matrix (tied), plus $1{,}024 \times 768$ positions: 124 M in total, the count the [fine-tuning note](/notes/nlp/fine-tuning) starts from.

**Llama 3.2 1B**: vocabulary 128,256; 16 pre-norm blocks with $d = 2{,}048$; RMSNorm; rotary positions; a gated FFN with three $2{,}048 \times 8{,}192$ matrices; and **grouped-query attention**, with a $2{,}048 \times 2{,}048$ query projection next to $2{,}048 \times 512$ key and value projections: 32 query heads of width 64 share 8 key–value heads, which cuts the memory of cached keys and values by four at generation time. Per block: 10.5 M attention + 50.3 M FFN ≈ 60.8 M; 16 blocks ≈ 973 M; embeddings $128{,}256 \times 2{,}048 \approx 263$ M, tied with the output; about 1.24 B in all. The 1B model has ten times GPT-2 small's parameters and a vocabulary 2.5 times larger, and the design changes (pre-norm, RMSNorm, RoPE, gated FFN, GQA) are the ones the [architecture note](/notes/nlp/transformers) listed as modern defaults.

## What a base model does

A pretrained decoder is a text continuer. Given `Find the value of n in the equation n + 2 = 6.`, a 1 B base model produces a plausible continuation of a web page containing that sentence: it restates the problem, wanders through some irrelevant rules of algebra, finds n = 4, then keeps going with "If n is an integer, what is the value of n? n = 4", "n = 5", "n = 6", and so on until the token budget runs out, because such lists are what follows such sentences in its training data. Given the same problem written as a completion, `… Answer: n = `, it emits `4` and then continues with a numbered list of equations. The model has the knowledge; it does not have the behaviour of answering and stopping. That behaviour is added by the [instruction tuning note](/notes/nlp/instruction-tuning), and the base and tuned checkpoints there have identical architectures.

## Decoding: turning probabilities into text

The model outputs a distribution at each step; something has to pick a token from it, append the token, and repeat. The choice shapes the text as much as the model does. There are two kinds of rule. **Search** rules (greedy, beam) are deterministic: they choose tokens of highest probability, and the same prompt always gives the same text. **Sampling** rules draw the token at random, $w_t \sim P(\cdot \mid w_{<t})$ or a modified version of it, so every run can differ. Temperature, top-$k$ and top-$p$ are the usual modifications.

<GenerationLoop />

### Greedy and beam search

**Greedy** takes $\arg\max_w P(w \mid w_{<t})$. It is a reasonable first choice: it is deterministic and cheap, each step takes the single best-supported token, and for short answers with one right continuation (a number, a name, a label) it is often exactly what is wanted. Its weakness shows on long, open-ended text. The most probable token at each step is the most generic one, so the text is bland, and it loops: a 135 M-parameter model asked to continue a story produces "Lily's mom, Mrs. Jenkins, was very kind and helpful" and then repeats the sentence, because once a phrase has been generated it is the most probable continuation of itself. **Beam search** (the [translation note](/notes/nlp/transformer-machine-translation#beam-search)) finds higher-probability sequences and makes the problem worse for open-ended text: the most probable long text is bland and repetitive ([Holtzman et al., 2020](#references)). It remains the right tool when there is a correct output, as in translation.

### Temperature

Divide the logits by $\tau$ before the softmax:

$$
P_\tau(w) = \frac{\exp(z_w / \tau)}{\sum_{w'} \exp(z_{w'} / \tau)}.
$$

$\tau = 1$ is the model's distribution; $\tau \to 0$ approaches greedy; $\tau > 1$ flattens. Temperature **reshapes the whole distribution**: every token keeps a nonzero probability, the order of the tokens does not change, only the contrast between them does. It rescales *all* tokens, including the implausible tail, so high temperatures produce incoherent text as easily as varied text.

### Truncated sampling: top-$k$ and top-$p$

Sample, but only from the head of the distribution. Both rules choose a set $S$ of tokens, set the probability of every other token to zero, and renormalize:

$$
P_S(w) = \frac{P(w)}{\sum_{w' \in S} P(w')} \;\text{ for } w \in S, \qquad P_S(w) = 0 \;\text{ otherwise}.
$$

They **truncate the support**, the set of tokens that can be drawn at all, and leave the ratios inside $S$ as they were. **Top-$k$** takes for $S$ the $k$ most probable tokens; it is predictable and ignores how peaked the distribution is, so $k = 40$ is too many when one token has 95 % of the mass and too few when the distribution is flat. **Top-$p$** (nucleus sampling, [Holtzman et al., 2020](#references)) keeps the smallest set $S$ of most probable tokens with $\sum_{w \in S} P(w) \ge p$; the set adapts to the shape of the distribution, which is why $p \approx 0.9$ is the usual default for open-ended generation. Both remove the long tail that temperature alone would sometimes sample.

**Worked example: reshape versus truncate.** Five tokens A–E with logits $(3, 2, 1.5, 0.5, -1)$:

| Rule | A | B | C | D | E | Tokens that can be drawn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\tau = 0.5$ | 0.839 | 0.114 | 0.042 | 0.006 | 0.0003 | 5 |
| $\tau = 1$ (the model) | 0.591 | 0.217 | 0.132 | 0.049 | 0.011 | 5 |
| $\tau = 2$ | 0.400 | 0.243 | 0.189 | 0.115 | 0.054 | 5 |
| Top-$k$, $k = 2$ | 0.731 | 0.269 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 2 |
| Top-$p$, $p = 0.9$ | 0.629 | 0.231 | 0.140 | 0 | 0 | 3 |

Top-$p$ keeps three tokens because the cumulative mass is $0.591$, then $0.809$, still below $0.9$, then $0.941$. Temperature moved mass between A and the tail but left E drawable in every row; truncation removed D and E and kept $P(\text{A})/P(\text{B}) = e \approx 2.72$, as at $\tau = 1$. In practice the two are combined, a moderate temperature followed by top-$p$.

### Penalties and contrast

A **repetition penalty** $\rho > 1$ divides the logit of every token already in the context by $\rho$ if positive and multiplies it if negative ([Keskar et al., 2019](#references)); OpenAI-style *frequency* and *presence* penalties instead subtract a term proportional to the token's count. Both suppress loops at the cost of sometimes suppressing a token that should repeat (a name).

**Contrastive search** ([Su et al., 2022](#references)) scores candidates by $\lambda \log P(w) - (1 - \lambda)\max_{j<t}\cos(h_w, h_j)$: probability minus the similarity of the candidate's hidden state to earlier ones, so repetition of representation is penalized, not just of tokens. **Typical sampling** ([Meister et al., 2023](#references)) keeps tokens whose surprise $-\log P(w)$ is close to the entropy of the distribution, discarding both the predictable and the bizarre. **DoLa** ([Chuang et al., 2024](#references)) decodes from the difference between the logits of a late layer and an early layer, on the hypothesis that early layers carry generic predictions and late layers specific ones; on the same 135 M-parameter model and story prompt as above, without the repetition penalty that the method's documentation recommends, it degenerates into "Max is 11 and 1 and 1 and 1…".

| Method | Knob | Deterministic | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Greedy | — | Yes | Short factual answers; loops on long text |
| Beam | $B$, $\alpha$ | Yes | Translation, summarization with references |
| Temperature | $\tau$ | No | Combined with truncation |
| Top-$k$ | $k$ | No | Legacy default |
| Top-$p$ | $p$ | No | Open-ended generation |
| Repetition penalty | $\rho$ | — | Added to any of the above |
| Contrastive search | $\lambda$ or $\alpha$, $k$ | Yes | Coherent long text without sampling |

## Making generation faster

Each generated token requires a forward pass over the prefix. Two mechanisms recover most of the waste.

**KV caching.** Keys and values of earlier positions do not change when a token is appended; cache them and compute only the new position's query, key and value. Memory per token is $2 \times L \times d_{kv}$ numbers, which is why grouped-query attention, which shrinks $d_{kv}$, matters for serving.

**Speculative decoding** ([Leviathan, Kalman and Matias, 2023](#references)). A small *draft* model proposes $k$ tokens; the large *target* model verifies them in one forward pass over the extended prefix; accepted tokens are kept and the first rejected one is replaced by the target's own choice. With greedy decoding the output is identical to the target model's; with sampling, an acceptance rule makes the output distribution identical. With a 1.7 B target and a 135 M draft, 128 greedy tokens take 41 s instead of 101 s on the same hardware. The texts generated with and without the draft agree for the first sentence and then diverge. In exact arithmetic they would be identical; the likely cause is that the verification pass scores several tokens at once in 16-bit floating point, its logits differ slightly from those of one-token steps, and a near-tie between two continuations is enough to change a greedy choice. Serving engines add **continuous batching** (requests join and leave a running batch), **paged KV memory** ([paging, as in virtual memory](/notes/acos/virtual-memory#paging-beyond-the-operating-system)) and **prefix caching** (a shared long prompt is encoded once), which raise throughput further without changing the model.

## What changed

| | Recurrent LM | Decoder-only Transformer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Context | Whole prefix, through a forgetting state | Every earlier position within the context window, by attention |
| Training over a sequence | $T$ sequential steps | One parallel pass with a causal mask |
| Scale reached | Tens of millions of parameters | $10^8$ to $10^{12}$ |
| Use of the pretrained model | Perplexity, sampling | Completion, few-shot prompting, then tuning |

## Limitations

- **Left to right only.** The representation of token $t$ never sees $t + 1$; for understanding tasks an encoder's bidirectional vector is a better feature. The [model-families note](/notes/nlp/model-families) compares.
- **Fluent is not correct.** The objective rewards plausible continuations, so a base model fabricates with the same confidence it states facts.
- **A continuer, not an assistant.** Without further training the model does not follow instructions, stop, or refuse.
- **Generation is sequential** and memory-bound at inference even though training is parallel.
- **Decoding choices are part of the system.** The same model gives different text under different samplers; evaluations must fix them.

## What you should remember

- Objective: $-\frac1T\sum_t \log P(w_t \mid w_{<t})$ over every token; the causal mask trains all positions in one pass.
- GPT-2 small: 12 blocks, $d = 768$, 124 M, tied embeddings. Llama 3.2 1B: 16 blocks, $d = 2{,}048$, GQA, RoPE, gated FFN, ≈ 1.24 B.
- A base model continues text; prompting it as a completion works better than asking it a question.
- GPT-1 → GPT-2 → GPT-3: the same decoder recipe with more parameters and data (books, then WebText, then a filtered web mix), used first by fine-tuning, then zero-shot, then few-shot in context.
- Decoding: search (greedy, beam) is deterministic, sampling draws at random. Greedy is reasonable for short determined answers and bland or looping on long text; beam search is for reference-matched tasks. Temperature reshapes the whole distribution and keeps every token; top-$k$ and top-$p$ truncate the support and renormalize, top-$p$ adaptively. Penalties and contrastive search target repetition.
- KV caching and speculative decoding speed generation without changing the output, up to floating-point effects.
