The [previous note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings) used embedding tables that were downloaded or returned by a single library call, and found that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors. Nothing there explained why. This note opens the library call. The idea is older than the method: words that occur in the same contexts tend to have similar meanings ([Harris, 1954](#references)), or, in Firth's phrase, a word is characterized by the company it keeps ([Firth, 1957](#references)). A model that has to predict a word's neighbours through a narrow bottleneck is forced to give words with the same neighbours similar bottleneck vectors. Word2Vec ([Mikolov et al., 2013a](#references), [2013b](#references)) turns that into two small networks and two tricks, hierarchical softmax and negative sampling, that make them trainable on billions of tokens; this note develops negative sampling, the one used in practice.

**Notation.** The corpus is a token sequence $w_1, \dots, w_T$ over a vocabulary $V$; $d$ is the embedding dimension, as in the previous note. For a position $t$ the *centre* word is $w_t$ and the *context* is the window $C_t = (w_{t+j})_{j \in J_t}$ with $J_t = \{j : 1 \le |j| \le m,\ 1 \le t + j \le T\}$, a list of up to $2m$ neighbours in which repeated words are kept; $m$ is the window radius. Each word $w$ has two vectors: an input (centre) vector $v_w \in \mathbb R^d$, a row of $W_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb R^{|V| \times d}$, and an output (context) vector $u_w \in \mathbb R^d$, a row of $W_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb R^{|V| \times d}$. $W_{\text{in}}$ is the embedding table $E$ of the previous note. $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$; $k$ is the number of negative samples and $P_n$ the distribution they are drawn from.

## Baseline: fit the embeddings with a labelled task

The [previous note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings#the-embedding-table) listed sources for the table. The first, training it as a layer of a classifier, works (the [CNN note](/notes/nlp/cnn-text-classification#trainable-embeddings) measures it) and needs labels: the table only learns about words that appear in the labelled set, and only the aspects of them that help predict the label. Labelled text is scarce; unlabelled text is almost unlimited. The question is what to predict when there is no label.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Make the text its own label. Hide one word and predict it from its neighbours, or take one word and predict its neighbours. Every position of a corpus becomes a training example, and no annotation is needed. A network with a single linear hidden layer of size $d$ solves this task, and the weights of its first layer are the embedding table.</aside>

## A network whose first layer is the table

The simplest model that implements the key idea is a two-layer linear network. For an input word $c$ and a target word $o$:

1. take the one-hot vector $e_c \in \{0, 1\}^{|V|}$ of $c$;
2. multiply by the first layer: $h = W_{\text{in}}^\top e_c = v_c \in \mathbb R^d$, with no bias and no non-linearity, so $h$ is simply the row of $c$;
3. score every vocabulary word against $h$: $s = W_{\text{out}}\, h \in \mathbb R^{|V|}$, so $s_w = u_w \cdot v_c$;
4. turn the scores into a distribution with a softmax:

$$
p(o \mid c) = \frac{\exp(u_o \cdot v_c)}{\sum_{w \in V}\exp(u_w \cdot v_c)}.
$$

The network is fitted with the cross-entropy loss $-\log p(o \mid c)$ on (input, target) pairs cut from the corpus. Then everything except the first layer is thrown away: the prediction is a means, and the row $v_c$ that the input selects is the embedding.

Shapes for one pair: $e_c$ is $[|V|]$, $W_{\text{in}}$ and $W_{\text{out}}$ are $[|V|, d]$, $h$ is $[d]$, scores and probabilities are $[|V|]$. In code the product with a one-hot vector is a lookup, so a batch of $B$ ids $[B]$ becomes $[B, d]$ after the first layer and $[B, |V|]$ after the second. The model has $2|V|d$ parameters.

<SkipGram />

Why two tables rather than one? With a single table the score of a word against itself would be $v_w \cdot v_w = \|v_w\|^2$, which is large for every word with a non-trivial vector, while a word rarely occurs in its own window; two tables let $u_w \cdot v_w$ be small ([Goldberg and Levy, 2014](#references)). After training, libraries return $W_{\text{in}}$ (the `wv` attribute in `gensim`). Combining the tables is a common alternative: GloVe, for example, releases the sum of its two tables ([Pennington, Socher and Manning, 2014](#references), §4.2).

## Two prediction tasks: CBOW and skip-gram

The network leaves open which words are inputs and which are targets. Word2Vec defines two choices, both built on the same windows.

**Continuous bag of words (CBOW)** predicts the centre word from its context. The hidden vector is the mean of the context input vectors, and that vector is classified among the $|V|$ possible centre words:

$$
h_t = \frac{1}{|J_t|}\sum_{j \in J_t} v_{w_{t+j}},
\qquad
p(w_t \mid C_t) = \frac{\exp(u_{w_t} \cdot h_t)}{\sum_{w' \in V}\exp(u_{w'} \cdot h_t)}.
$$

"Bag" because the mean ignores the order of the words inside the window. Each position gives one training example; the input is $[|J_t|, d]$ before averaging and $[d]$ after.

**Skip-gram** reverses the direction: the centre word predicts each context word separately, with the softmax of the previous section, and training minimizes

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{SG}} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{j \in J_t} \log p(w_{t+j} \mid w_t).
$$

Each position gives up to $2m$ (centre, context) pairs, one per neighbour.

<TrainingPairs />

**Worked example: training pairs.** Take the sentence `we drink hot coffee every morning`, so $T = 6$, with $m = 2$. For the centre `coffee` the context is `drink, hot, every, morning`. Skip-gram makes four pairs from it: (coffee, drink), (coffee, hot), (coffee, every), (coffee, morning). CBOW makes one example: the mean of $v_{\text{drink}}, v_{\text{hot}}, v_{\text{every}}, v_{\text{morning}}$ has to predict `coffee`. Position $i$ has $\min(m, i - 1) + \min(m, T - i)$ neighbours, which over the sentence is $2 + 3 + 4 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 18$ skip-gram pairs against 6 CBOW examples.

A third choice is to predict the next word from the previous ones, $p(w_t \mid w_{t-n+1}, \dots, w_{t-1})$. That is a neural language model ([Bengio et al., 2003](#references)), the neural counterpart of the [n-gram model](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models). Its first layer is also an embedding table, but the model is built for the prediction itself, with a non-linear hidden layer that makes it far more expensive. Word2Vec only wants the table, so it drops the non-linearity and uses context on both sides.

The two tasks differ in cost and in what they learn. CBOW computes one output distribution per position and skip-gram up to $2m$, so CBOW is cheaper by about a factor of the window size ([Mikolov et al., 2013a](#references), §3.1–3.2). In the comparison of that paper (640-dimensional vectors, same training data, Table 3), skip-gram was far better on semantic analogies (55% against 24%) and slightly worse on syntactic ones (59% against 64%). Skip-gram is also usually reported to give better vectors for rare words ([word2vec project page](#references)). The number of updates is not the difference: in CBOW a rare word also enters the windows of up to $2m$ centres. But each skip-gram pair trains the rare word's vector on its own, while CBOW updates it only through a mean with up to $2m - 1$ other context vectors, which dilutes its individual signal. The library used in the notebooks, `gensim`, trains CBOW by default (`sg=0`); skip-gram is selected with `sg=1`.

Implementations do not use every neighbour equally. For each centre the effective radius $R$ is drawn uniformly from $\{1, \dots, m\}$ ([Mikolov et al., 2013a](#references), §3.2), so a neighbour at distance $j$ is used with probability $(m - j + 1)/m$: for $m = 5$ that is $1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2$. Near neighbours count more without an explicit weight.

## Why the softmax is the problem

Write the skip-gram loss for one pair $(c, o)$ as $-\log p(o \mid c) = -u_o \cdot v_c + \log \sum_{w \in V} \exp(u_w \cdot v_c)$. The derivative of the log-sum-exp with respect to $v_c$ is the softmax-weighted average of the $u_w$, and with respect to one $u_w$ it is $p(w \mid c)\, v_c$. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\,(-\log p(o \mid c))}{\partial v_c} &= -u_o + \sum_{w \in V} p(w \mid c)\, u_w, \\
\frac{\partial\,(-\log p(o \mid c))}{\partial u_w} &= \big(p(w \mid c) - \mathbb 1[w = o]\big)\, v_c .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line reads "observed minus expected": a gradient step pulls $v_c$ towards the vector of the context word that occurred and pushes it away from the context vector the model currently expects. The second line is non-zero for *every* word $w$, so one training pair writes to all $|V|$ rows of $W_{\text{out}}$, and computing the normalizer alone needs all $|V|$ dot products ([Rong, 2014](#references)).

Count the cost with a realistic vocabulary of $|V| = 200{,}000$ words and $d = 300$. The model has $2|V|d = 1.2 \times 10^8$ parameters. One pair costs $|V| d = 6 \times 10^7$ multiply-adds for the scores and updates $|V| d + d = 60{,}000{,}300$ parameters. A corpus of $10^9$ tokens with $m = 5$ gives up to $10^{10}$ pairs per pass. The model is small; the normalization makes training infeasible.

<CostScaling />

Two repairs exist. **Hierarchical softmax** ([Morin and Bengio, 2005](#references)) places the vocabulary at the leaves of a binary tree (a Huffman tree in Word2Vec, [Mikolov et al., 2013b](#references), §2.1) and replaces the $|V|$-way softmax by about $\log_2 |V|$ binary decisions along the path to the target word, about 18 for 200,000 words. **Negative sampling** drops the normalization altogether; it is the default in practice and the subject of the rest of this note.

## Negative sampling

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Replace the $|V|$-way classification by binary ones. Instead of "which of the $|V|$ words is the context?", ask "did this (centre, context) pair really occur?". The observed pair is a positive example; $k$ pairs with randomly drawn context words are negatives. Each is a logistic regression on its score $u_w \cdot v_c$, so an update touches the centre vector and $k + 1$ output rows, whatever the size of the vocabulary.</aside>

<NegativeSampling />

For an observed pair $(c, o)$ and noise words $n_1, \dots, n_k$ drawn from $P_n$, the skip-gram negative-sampling (SGNS) loss is

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{NS}}(c, o) = -\log \sigma(u_o \cdot v_c) - \sum_{i=1}^k \log \sigma(-u_{n_i} \cdot v_c).
$$

$\sigma(u_w \cdot v_c)$ is the model's probability that a pair is real, and $\sigma(-x) = 1 - \sigma(x)$, so each term of the sum is the log-probability that a noise pair is recognized as fake. With the scores $s_o = u_o \cdot v_c$ and $s_i = u_{n_i} \cdot v_c$, and $\frac{d}{dx}\log\sigma(x) = 1 - \sigma(x)$, the gradients are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\text{NS}}}{\partial v_c} &= \big(\sigma(s_o) - 1\big)\, u_o + \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma(s_i)\, u_{n_i}, \\
\frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\text{NS}}}{\partial u_o} &= \big(\sigma(s_o) - 1\big)\, v_c,
\qquad
\frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\text{NS}}}{\partial u_{n_i}} = \sigma(s_i)\, v_c .
\end{aligned}
$$

The shape is the same "observed minus expected" as the softmax gradient, with the expectation over $|V|$ words replaced by $k$ sampled words, each weighted by how plausible the model currently finds it. All other rows have zero gradient.

CBOW uses the same loss with the context mean $h_t$ of the CBOW section in place of the centre vector and the centre word as the positive:

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{CBOW-NS}}(t) = -\log \sigma(u_{w_t} \cdot h_t) - \sum_{i=1}^k \log \sigma(-u_{n_i} \cdot h_t).
$$

Its gradients are the ones above with $v_c$ replaced by $h_t$. The chain rule through the mean would send $1/|J_t|$ of the gradient with respect to $h_t$ to each context vector; the reference implementation `word2vec.c` ([word2vec project page](#references)) and `gensim` (with the default `cbow_mean=1`) add the whole of it to every context vector, which amounts to a larger learning rate for the input table. This is the model trained in the notebooks.

The cost per pair is $d$ input parameters plus $(k + 1) d$ output parameters. With $d = 300$ and $k = 5$ that is $300 + 1{,}800 = 2{,}100$ parameters instead of 60,000,300, about 28,600 times fewer, and the count does not grow with $|V|$. [Mikolov et al. (2013b)](#references), §2.2, report that $k$ between 5 and 20 works for small training sets and that 2 to 5 is enough for large ones; `gensim` uses $k = 5$.

### Worked example

Toy numbers: $d = 2$, $k = 2$ and learning rate $\eta = 0.5$, large so that the movement is visible. The centre word is `coffee`, the observed context word `drink`, and the sampled negatives are `car` and `tax`.

| Vector | Value | Score with $v_{\text{coffee}}$ | $\sigma(\text{score})$ | Loss term |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $v_{\text{coffee}}$ | $(1.0, 0.5)$ | | | |
| $u_{\text{drink}}$ (observed) | $(0.8, 0.6)$ | $1.10$ | $0.750$ | $-\log \sigma(1.10) = 0.287$ |
| $u_{\text{car}}$ (negative) | $(-0.2, 0.9)$ | $0.25$ | $0.562$ | $-\log \sigma(-0.25) = 0.826$ |
| $u_{\text{tax}}$ (negative) | $(0.6, -0.4)$ | $0.40$ | $0.599$ | $-\log \sigma(-0.40) = 0.913$ |

The loss is $0.287 + 0.826 + 0.913 = 2.026$. The gradients from the formulas above:

- $\partial \mathcal L / \partial v_{\text{coffee}} = -0.250\,(0.8, 0.6) + 0.562\,(-0.2, 0.9) + 0.599\,(0.6, -0.4) = (0.047, 0.117)$;
- $\partial \mathcal L / \partial u_{\text{drink}} = -0.250\,(1.0, 0.5) = (-0.250, -0.125)$;
- $\partial \mathcal L / \partial u_{\text{car}} = 0.562\,(1.0, 0.5) = (0.562, 0.281)$ and $\partial \mathcal L / \partial u_{\text{tax}} = 0.599\,(1.0, 0.5) = (0.599, 0.299)$.

One step $x \leftarrow x - \eta\, \partial\mathcal L/\partial x$ on all four vectors gives $v_{\text{coffee}} = (0.977, 0.442)$, $u_{\text{drink}} = (0.925, 0.662)$, $u_{\text{car}} = (-0.481, 0.759)$ and $u_{\text{tax}} = (0.301, -0.550)$. The observed score rises from $1.10$ to $1.20$, the negative scores fall from $0.25$ and $0.40$ to $-0.13$ and $0.05$, and the loss falls from $2.026$ to $1.611$. Four rows changed; with a vocabulary of 200,000 words the other 199,997 output rows were not read.

The gradient on $v_{\text{coffee}}$, $\sum_w p(w \mid c)\, u_w - u_{\text{drink}} = (-0.193, -0.277)$, needs all six rows. It also pushes `cup` down, a plausible context word the model ranks second; negative sampling pushes a word down only when it is drawn.

### Which words are negatives

Negatives are drawn from the unigram distribution raised to the power $3/4$ and renormalized ([Mikolov et al., 2013b](#references), §2.2):

$$
P_n(w) = \frac{\operatorname{count}(w)^{3/4}}{\sum_{w' \in V} \operatorname{count}(w')^{3/4}} .
$$

Frequent words remain the most common negatives, but less overwhelmingly so. With toy counts `the` 600, `coffee` 30 and `espresso` 3, sampling in proportion to the counts gives probabilities $0.948, 0.047, 0.005$, and the $3/4$ power gives $0.889, 0.094, 0.017$: `espresso` becomes 3.5 times more likely to be drawn. The exponent is empirical: it worked clearly better than both the plain unigram distribution and the uniform one in the original experiments.

### Subsampling frequent words

Pairs with `the` or `a` are numerous and say little about either word. Before windows are formed, each occurrence of a word $w$ with relative frequency $f(w)$ is discarded with probability

$$
P_{\text{discard}}(w) = 1 - \sqrt{\theta / f(w)}
$$

for a threshold $\theta \approx 10^{-5}$, and never when $f(w) \le \theta$ ([Mikolov et al., 2013b](#references), §2.3). For toy frequencies $0.05$, $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$ the discard probabilities are $0.986$, $0.9$ and $0.68$. Removing a token also shortens the distance between the words around it, so subsampling widens the effective window. `gensim` uses a variant of this rule controlled by its `sample` argument, default $10^{-3}$.

### What negative sampling optimizes

Negative sampling is not an unbiased approximation of the softmax gradient; it is a different objective, a simplification of noise-contrastive estimation ([Gutmann and Hyvärinen, 2010](#references); [Mnih and Teh, 2012](#references)). NCE also avoids the normalizer, but it classifies with the corrected logit $u_o \cdot v_c - \log\big(k\, P_n(o)\big)$, which needs the noise probabilities and makes its optimal score the log conditional probability $\log P(o \mid c)$. Negative sampling uses the raw score $u_o \cdot v_c$ and needs only samples from $P_n$ ([Mikolov et al., 2013b](#references), §2.2). Its optimum is therefore shifted, and it is known. If $d$ is large enough for every score to be set independently, the optimal score of a pair is the pointwise mutual information shifted by $\log k$ ([Levy and Goldberg, 2014](#references), §3.1):

$$
u_o \cdot v_c = \operatorname{PMI}(c, o) - \log k,
\qquad
\operatorname{PMI}(c, o) = \log \frac{P(c, o)}{P(c)\, P_n(o)} .
$$

With $P_n$ the plain unigram distribution this is the usual PMI, the case analysed by Levy and Goldberg. With the $3/4$ power it is a smoothed PMI: rare context words get a larger $P_n(o)$ and hence a smaller value ([Levy, Goldberg and Dagan, 2015](#references), "context distribution smoothing").

<aside class="callout"><strong>Where the shifted PMI comes from.</strong> Fix a pair $(c, o)$ and write $x = u_o \cdot v_c$. Over the corpus the pair occurs $\#(c, o)$ times as a positive, contributing $-\#(c, o) \log \sigma(x)$. The word $c$ is a centre in $\#(c)$ pairs, each drawing $k$ negatives that equal $o$ with probability $P_n(o)$, contributing in expectation $-k\, \#(c)\, P_n(o) \log \sigma(-x)$. Setting the derivative of the sum to zero gives $\#(c, o)\,(1 - \sigma(x)) = k\, \#(c)\, P_n(o)\, \sigma(x)$, so $e^x = \sigma(x)/(1 - \sigma(x)) = \#(c, o) / (k\, \#(c)\, P_n(o))$ and $x = \log \frac{\#(c, o)}{\#(c)\, P_n(o)} - \log k$. Since $\#(c, o)/\#(c) = P(o \mid c) = P(c, o)/P(c)$, the first term is the PMI above.</aside>

<SgnsOptimum />

So SGNS factorizes a matrix of shifted PMI values into $W_{\text{in}} W_{\text{out}}^\top$ with rank $d$: the learned vectors compress co-occurrence statistics. With a finite $d$ the factorization is approximate, and that compression is what makes words with similar rows of the PMI matrix end up close. Pairs that never co-occur have $\operatorname{PMI} = -\infty$: their per-pair objective has only the negative term and keeps decreasing as the score goes down, so even with a large $d$ the factorization can only be approximate there.

## What the notebook model learned

The notebook trains one model on the Quora questions, lower-cased, tokenized and with English stopwords removed; `wordpunct_tokenize` keeps punctuation such as `?` and `'` as tokens, and stopword removal leaves them in: `Word2Vec(data_tok, vector_size=32, min_count=5, window=5)`. Everything else is a `gensim` default, so this is **CBOW** with negative sampling ($k = 5$, noise exponent $0.75$), context mean (`cbow_mean=1`), subsampling with `sample=1e-3`, learning rate decayed linearly from 0.025 to 0.0001, 5 epochs and randomly shrunk windows.

The saved outputs are a vector and a neighbour list. The vector of `word` has 32 coordinates between $-4.09$ and $4.18$ and Euclidean norm 10.7 (computed from the saved coordinates); individual coordinates mean nothing, only directions relative to other vectors do. Its nearest neighbours by cosine, listed in the [previous note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings#reading-a-vector-space), start with `sentence` (0.836), `words` (0.796) and `verb` (0.787). Two features of that list follow from the objective. Singular and plural forms sit side by side (`word` and `words`, `phrase` and `phrases`) because the text was not lemmatized and both forms fill the same slots in questions. And the neighbours are topical (questions about language) as much as grammatical, because after stopword removal a window of radius 5 reaches up to five tokens on each side, mostly content words plus punctuation such as `?` and `'`, which covers more of the question than five raw tokens would. Subsampling (`sample=1e-3`) discards a large share of the occurrences of very frequent tokens such as `?`, which ends almost every question, and so widens the window further. No loss curve, training time, vocabulary size or evaluation score was saved for this model.

These choices matter as much as the choice of algorithm: with tuned window, subsampling, negatives and noise exponent, count-based methods and SGNS perform much more alike than the original comparisons suggested ([Levy, Goldberg and Dagan, 2015](#references)).

## What changed

| | Task-trained embeddings | Full-softmax Word2Vec | Word2Vec with negative sampling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Training signal | Labels of one task | The text itself | The text itself |
| Data available | Thousands of labelled examples | Billions of tokens | Billions of tokens |
| Vocabulary coverage | Words in the labelled set | Words in the corpus | Words in the corpus |
| Output per step | $K$ classes | $\lvert V \rvert$-way softmax | $k + 1$ binary classifiers |
| Parameters touched per pair ($\lvert V \rvert = 200{,}000$, $d = 300$) | — | 60,000,300 | 2,100 ($k = 5$) |
| What the vectors encode | Whatever separates the labels | Co-occurrence structure | Shifted PMI, compressed to rank $d$ |

Here $K$ is the number of classes of the labelled task. The result is a table that any model can reuse. The [CNN note](/notes/nlp/cnn-text-classification) feeds it to a classifier on AG News and then asks whether the task-specific alternative is better after all.

## GloVe and FastText

Two later static-embedding methods appear in the course as downloaded tables. **GloVe** ([Pennington, Socher and Manning, 2014](#references)) does not sample windows; it builds the full word–word co-occurrence matrix $X$ once and fits $u_w \cdot v_c + b_w + b_c \approx \log X_{wc}$ by weighted least squares, with a weight that caps the influence of very frequent pairs. The shifted-PMI result above shows why the two families end up close: both fit a function of co-occurrence counts with dot products. The Twitter vectors used for the analogies in the previous note were trained this way. **FastText** ([Bojanowski et al., 2017](#references)) represents a word as the sum of vectors of its character n-grams (`where` → `<wh`, `whe`, `her`, `ere`, `re>`), so morphologically related words share parameters and a word never seen in training still gets a vector from its pieces; its training objective is skip-gram with negative sampling. Both are planned topics of this course rather than full notes.

## Limitations

- **Static.** One vector per word type, whatever the sentence: `bank` is a single compromise between the river and the money. The [BERT note](/notes/nlp/bert#from-static-to-contextual-embeddings) removes this.
- **Closed vocabulary.** Words below `min_count` or absent from the training corpus get no vector. FastText builds vectors from character n-grams; subword tokenization solves the same problem for later models.
- **Window-bound.** Only neighbours within $m$ positions shape a vector, and CBOW ignores their order. Sentence-level structure, negation and long-range dependencies do not enter.
- **Similarity means substitutability.** `good` and `bad`, `hot` and `cold`, `Monday` and `Tuesday` share contexts and end up close. For sentiment that is the wrong geometry.
- **Data-hungry.** Half a million short questions give sensible neighbours for frequent words such as `word`, but rare words get noisy vectors or none, and the analogies of the previous note come from vectors trained on billions of tokens.
- **Sensitive to settings, not exactly reproducible.** Preprocessing, window, subsampling and $k$ change the neighbours. Training with several worker threads makes two runs with the same seed differ.
- **Not a sentence representation.** Averaging the vectors of a sentence discards order; the next section of the course builds models that read the sequence.

## What you should remember

- Self-supervision: predict the context from the centre (skip-gram) or the centre from the context (CBOW). The network is linear, and its first layer, $W_{\text{in}}$, is the embedding table; the output layer only provides the training signal.
- The full-softmax gradient is "observed minus expected" and touches all $|V|$ output rows per pair: $|V| d$ weights, tens of millions for a realistic vocabulary.
- Negative sampling turns the task into logistic classification of the real pair against $k$ random pairs, updating $(k + 2) d$ parameters per pair, with negatives drawn from the $3/4$-power unigram distribution and frequent words subsampled.
- At its optimum SGNS sets $u_o \cdot v_c = \operatorname{PMI}(c, o) - \log k$: the vectors are a low-rank compression of co-occurrence statistics.
- `gensim` trains CBOW by default (`sg=0`), with negative sampling ($k = 5$).
