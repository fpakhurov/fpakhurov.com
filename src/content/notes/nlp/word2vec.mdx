The [previous note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings) found that library-trained embedding tables give similar vectors to words used in similar contexts, without explaining why. Words that occur in the same contexts tend to have similar meanings ([Harris, 1954](#references); [Firth, 1957](#references)), and a model that must predict a word's neighbours through a narrow bottleneck has to give words with the same neighbours similar bottleneck vectors. Word2Vec ([Mikolov et al., 2013a](#references), [2013b](#references)) turns that into two small networks, made trainable on billions of tokens by hierarchical softmax or negative sampling; this note develops negative sampling, the one used in practice.

**Notation.** The corpus is a token sequence $w_1, \dots, w_T$ over a vocabulary $V$; $d$ is the embedding dimension. For a position $t$ the *centre* word is $w_t$ and the *context* is the window $C_t = (w_{t+j})_{j \in J_t}$ with $J_t = \{j : 1 \le |j| \le m,\ 1 \le t + j \le T\}$, up to $2m$ neighbours with repeats kept; $m$ is the window radius. Each word $w$ has two vectors: an input (centre) vector $v_w \in \mathbb R^d$, a row of $W_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb R^{|V| \times d}$, and an output (context) vector $u_w \in \mathbb R^d$, a row of $W_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb R^{|V| \times d}$. $W_{\text{in}}$ is the embedding table $E$ of the previous note. $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$; $k$ is the number of negative samples and $P_n$ the distribution they are drawn from.

## Baseline: fit the embeddings with a labelled task

The [previous note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings#the-embedding-table) listed sources for the table. Training it as a layer of a classifier works (the [CNN note](/notes/nlp/cnn-text-classification#trainable-embeddings) measures it) but learns only about words in the labelled set and only what helps predict the label. Labelled text is scarce; unlabelled text is almost unlimited. What should a model predict when there is no label?

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Make the text its own label: predict a word from its neighbours, or its neighbours from the word. Every position of a corpus becomes a training example without annotation. A network with one linear hidden layer of size $d$ solves this task, and its first layer is the embedding table.</aside>

## A network whose first layer is the table

The key idea needs only a two-layer linear network. For an input word $c$ and a target word $o$:

1. take the one-hot vector $e_c \in \{0, 1\}^{|V|}$ of $c$;
2. multiply by the first layer: $h = W_{\text{in}}^\top e_c = v_c \in \mathbb R^d$, the row of $c$ (no bias, no non-linearity);
3. score every vocabulary word against $h$: $s = W_{\text{out}}\, h \in \mathbb R^{|V|}$, so $s_w = u_w \cdot v_c$;
4. turn the scores into a distribution with a softmax:

$$
p(o \mid c) = \frac{\exp(u_o \cdot v_c)}{\sum_{w \in V}\exp(u_w \cdot v_c)}.
$$

The network is fitted with the cross-entropy $-\log p(o \mid c)$ on (input, target) pairs cut from the corpus; then all but the first layer is thrown away, and the row $v_c$ is the embedding. In code the one-hot product is a lookup: a batch of ids $[B]$ becomes $[B, d]$, then $[B, |V|]$. The model has $2|V|d$ parameters.

<SkipGram />

Two tables are needed because with one, a word's score against itself, $\|v_w\|^2$, would be large, while a word rarely occurs in its own window; two tables let $u_w \cdot v_w$ be small ([Goldberg and Levy, 2014](#references)). Libraries return $W_{\text{in}}$ (`wv` in `gensim`).

## Two prediction tasks: CBOW and skip-gram

The network leaves open which words are inputs and which are targets. Word2Vec defines two choices on the same windows.

**Continuous bag of words (CBOW)** predicts the centre word from its context: the mean of the context input vectors is classified among the $|V|$ possible centre words:

$$
h_t = \frac{1}{|J_t|}\sum_{j \in J_t} v_{w_{t+j}},
\qquad
p(w_t \mid C_t) = \frac{\exp(u_{w_t} \cdot h_t)}{\sum_{w' \in V}\exp(u_{w'} \cdot h_t)}.
$$

"Bag" because the mean ignores word order; each position gives one example.

**Skip-gram** reverses the direction: the centre word predicts each context word separately, with the softmax of the previous section, minimizing

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{SG}} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{j \in J_t} \log p(w_{t+j} \mid w_t).
$$

Each position gives up to $2m$ (centre, context) pairs.

<TrainingPairs />

**Worked example: training pairs.** In `we drink hot coffee every morning` ($T = 6$, $m = 2$) the centre `coffee` gives four skip-gram pairs and one CBOW example, in which the mean of the four context vectors predicts `coffee` (figure above). Position $i$ has $\min(m, i - 1) + \min(m, T - i)$ neighbours, so the sentence gives $2 + 3 + 4 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 18$ skip-gram pairs against 6 CBOW examples.

A neural language model ([Bengio et al., 2003](#references)) also starts with an embedding table but predicts the next word, like the [n-gram model](/notes/nlp/n-gram-language-models), through a costly non-linear hidden layer. Word2Vec only wants the table, so it drops the non-linearity and uses context on both sides.

CBOW computes one output distribution per position and skip-gram up to $2m$, so CBOW is cheaper by about the window size, but in the comparison of [Mikolov et al. (2013a)](#references), §3.1–3.2 and Table 3 (640-dimensional vectors), skip-gram was far better on semantic analogies (55% against 24%) and slightly worse on syntactic ones (59% against 64%). It is also reported to be better for rare words ([word2vec project page](#references)), whose vectors CBOW only updates through a mean with up to $2m - 1$ others.

For each centre the effective radius is drawn uniformly from $\{1, \dots, m\}$ ([Mikolov et al., 2013a](#references), §3.2), so a neighbour at distance $j$ is used with probability $(m - j + 1)/m$ ($1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2$ for $m = 5$): near neighbours count more without an explicit weight.

## Why the softmax is the problem

Write the skip-gram loss of one pair $(c, o)$ as $-\log p(o \mid c) = -u_o \cdot v_c + \log \sum_{w \in V} \exp(u_w \cdot v_c)$. The derivative of the log-sum-exp with respect to $v_c$ is the softmax-weighted average of the $u_w$, and with respect to one $u_w$ it is $p(w \mid c)\, v_c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\,(-\log p(o \mid c))}{\partial v_c} &= -u_o + \sum_{w \in V} p(w \mid c)\, u_w, \\
\frac{\partial\,(-\log p(o \mid c))}{\partial u_w} &= \big(p(w \mid c) - \mathbb 1[w = o]\big)\, v_c .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line reads "observed minus expected": a step pulls $v_c$ towards the context word that occurred and away from the one the model expects. The second is non-zero for *every* $w$, so one pair writes to all $|V|$ rows of $W_{\text{out}}$, and the normalizer alone needs $|V|$ dot products ([Rong, 2014](#references)).

With $|V| = 200{,}000$ and $d = 300$ the model has $2|V|d = 1.2 \times 10^8$ parameters. One pair costs $|V| d = 6 \times 10^7$ multiply-adds for the scores and updates $|V| d + d = 60{,}000{,}300$ parameters, and a corpus of $10^9$ tokens with $m = 5$ gives up to $10^{10}$ pairs per pass. The model is small; the normalization makes training infeasible.

<CostScaling />

**Hierarchical softmax** ([Morin and Bengio, 2005](#references)) puts the vocabulary at the leaves of a binary (Huffman, [Mikolov et al., 2013b](#references), §2.1) tree, so a prediction takes about $\log_2 |V|$ binary decisions, 18 for 200,000 words. **Negative sampling** drops the normalization; it is the default in practice and the subject of the rest of this note.

## Negative sampling

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Replace the $|V|$-way classification by binary ones: did this (centre, context) pair really occur? The observed pair is a positive; $k$ pairs with randomly drawn context words are negatives. Each is a logistic regression on its score $u_w \cdot v_c$, so an update touches the centre vector and $k + 1$ output rows, whatever the vocabulary size.</aside>

<NegativeSampling />

For an observed pair $(c, o)$ and noise words $n_1, \dots, n_k \sim P_n$, the skip-gram negative-sampling (SGNS) loss is

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{NS}}(c, o) = -\log \sigma(u_o \cdot v_c) - \sum_{i=1}^k \log \sigma(-u_{n_i} \cdot v_c).
$$

$\sigma(u_w \cdot v_c)$ is the model's probability that a pair is real and $\sigma(-x) = 1 - \sigma(x)$, so each term of the sum is the log-probability that a noise pair is recognized as fake. With the scores $s_o = u_o \cdot v_c$ and $s_i = u_{n_i} \cdot v_c$, and $\frac{d}{dx}\log\sigma(x) = 1 - \sigma(x)$, the gradients are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\text{NS}}}{\partial v_c} &= \big(\sigma(s_o) - 1\big)\, u_o + \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma(s_i)\, u_{n_i}, \\
\frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\text{NS}}}{\partial u_o} &= \big(\sigma(s_o) - 1\big)\, v_c,
\qquad
\frac{\partial \mathcal L_{\text{NS}}}{\partial u_{n_i}} = \sigma(s_i)\, v_c .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is "observed minus expected" with the expectation over $|V|$ words replaced by $k$ sampled words, each weighted by how plausible the model finds it; all other rows have zero gradient.

CBOW uses the same loss with the context mean $h_t$ in place of the centre vector and the centre word as the positive:

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{CBOW-NS}}(t) = -\log \sigma(u_{w_t} \cdot h_t) - \sum_{i=1}^k \log \sigma(-u_{n_i} \cdot h_t).
$$

Its gradients are those above with $h_t$ for $v_c$. The chain rule through the mean would send $1/|J_t|$ of $h_t$'s gradient to each context vector; `word2vec.c` ([word2vec project page](#references)) and `gensim` (`cbow_mean=1`), the model of the notebooks, add all of it, in effect a larger learning rate for the input table.

A pair updates $(k + 2) d$ parameters, $2{,}100$ for $d = 300$ and $k = 5$. [Mikolov et al. (2013b)](#references), §2.2, report that $k$ between 5 and 20 works for small training sets and 2 to 5 for large ones; `gensim` uses $k = 5$.

### Worked example

Toy numbers: $d = 2$, $k = 2$, learning rate $\eta = 0.5$ (large, so that the movement is visible), centre `coffee`, observed context `drink`, negatives `car` and `tax`.

| Vector | Value | Score with $v_{\text{coffee}}$ | $\sigma(\text{score})$ | Loss term |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $v_{\text{coffee}}$ | $(1.0, 0.5)$ | | | |
| $u_{\text{drink}}$ (observed) | $(0.8, 0.6)$ | $1.10$ | $0.750$ | $-\log \sigma(1.10) = 0.287$ |
| $u_{\text{car}}$ (negative) | $(-0.2, 0.9)$ | $0.25$ | $0.562$ | $-\log \sigma(-0.25) = 0.826$ |
| $u_{\text{tax}}$ (negative) | $(0.6, -0.4)$ | $0.40$ | $0.599$ | $-\log \sigma(-0.40) = 0.913$ |

The loss is $0.287 + 0.826 + 0.913 = 2.026$, and the gradients are

- $\partial \mathcal L / \partial v_{\text{coffee}} = -0.250\,(0.8, 0.6) + 0.562\,(-0.2, 0.9) + 0.599\,(0.6, -0.4) = (0.047, 0.117)$;
- $\partial \mathcal L / \partial u_{\text{drink}} = -0.250\,(1.0, 0.5) = (-0.250, -0.125)$;
- $\partial \mathcal L / \partial u_{\text{car}} = 0.562\,(1.0, 0.5) = (0.562, 0.281)$ and $\partial \mathcal L / \partial u_{\text{tax}} = 0.599\,(1.0, 0.5) = (0.599, 0.299)$.

One step $x \leftarrow x - \eta\, \partial\mathcal L/\partial x$ gives $v_{\text{coffee}} = (0.977, 0.442)$, $u_{\text{drink}} = (0.925, 0.662)$, $u_{\text{car}} = (-0.481, 0.759)$ and $u_{\text{tax}} = (0.301, -0.550)$; the two figures below show the vectors and the scores moving, and the loss falls from $2.026$ to $1.611$. Four rows changed; with 200,000 words the other 199,997 output rows were not read.

### Which words are negatives

Negatives are drawn from the unigram distribution raised to the power $3/4$ and renormalized ([Mikolov et al., 2013b](#references), §2.2):

$$
P_n(w) = \frac{\operatorname{count}(w)^{3/4}}{\sum_{w' \in V} \operatorname{count}(w')^{3/4}} .
$$

With toy counts `the` 600, `coffee` 30 and `espresso` 3, sampling in proportion to the counts gives probabilities $0.948, 0.047, 0.005$, and the $3/4$ power gives $0.889, 0.094, 0.017$: `espresso` becomes 3.5 times more likely to be drawn.

### Subsampling frequent words

Pairs with `the` or `a` are numerous and uninformative. Before windows are formed, each occurrence of a word $w$ with relative frequency $f(w)$ is discarded with probability

$$
P_{\text{discard}}(w) = 1 - \sqrt{\theta / f(w)}
$$

for a threshold $\theta \approx 10^{-5}$, and never when $f(w) \le \theta$ ([Mikolov et al., 2013b](#references), §2.3). For toy frequencies $0.05$, $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-4}$ the discard probabilities are $0.986$, $0.9$ and $0.68$. Removing a token brings the words around it closer, so subsampling also widens the effective window. `gensim` uses a variant of this rule with threshold `sample`, default $10^{-3}$.

### What negative sampling optimizes

Negative sampling optimizes a different objective from the softmax, a simplification of noise-contrastive estimation ([Gutmann and Hyvärinen, 2010](#references); [Mnih and Teh, 2012](#references)), which classifies with the corrected logit $u_o \cdot v_c - \log\big(k\, P_n(o)\big)$ and whose optimum is $\log P(o \mid c)$. Negative sampling uses the raw score ([Mikolov et al., 2013b](#references), §2.2), so its optimum is shifted: if $d$ is large enough for every score to be set independently, it is the pointwise mutual information shifted by $\log k$ ([Levy and Goldberg, 2014](#references), §3.1):

$$
u_o \cdot v_c = \operatorname{PMI}(c, o) - \log k,
\qquad
\operatorname{PMI}(c, o) = \log \frac{P(c, o)}{P(c)\, P_n(o)} .
$$

With the $3/4$ power this is a smoothed PMI: rare context words get a larger $P_n(o)$ and a smaller value ([Levy, Goldberg and Dagan, 2015](#references), "context distribution smoothing").

<aside class="callout"><strong>Where the shifted PMI comes from.</strong> Fix a pair $(c, o)$ and write $x = u_o \cdot v_c$. Over the corpus the pair occurs $\#(c, o)$ times as a positive, contributing $-\#(c, o) \log \sigma(x)$. The word $c$ is a centre in $\#(c)$ pairs, each drawing $k$ negatives that equal $o$ with probability $P_n(o)$, contributing in expectation $-k\, \#(c)\, P_n(o) \log \sigma(-x)$. Setting the derivative of the sum to zero gives $\#(c, o)\,(1 - \sigma(x)) = k\, \#(c)\, P_n(o)\, \sigma(x)$, so $e^x = \sigma(x)/(1 - \sigma(x)) = \#(c, o) / (k\, \#(c)\, P_n(o))$ and $x = \log \frac{\#(c, o)}{\#(c)\, P_n(o)} - \log k$. Since $\#(c, o)/\#(c) = P(o \mid c) = P(c, o)/P(c)$, the first term is the PMI above.</aside>

<SgnsOptimum />

So SGNS factorizes the shifted PMI matrix into $W_{\text{in}} W_{\text{out}}^\top$ of rank $d$, and this compression puts words with similar PMI rows close together. Pairs that never co-occur have $\operatorname{PMI} = -\infty$ (dotted curve), so there it stays approximate for any $d$.

## What changed

| | Task-trained embeddings | Full-softmax Word2Vec | Word2Vec with negative sampling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Training signal | Labels of one task | The text itself | The text itself |
| Data available | Thousands of labelled examples | Billions of tokens | Billions of tokens |
| Vocabulary coverage | Words in the labelled set | Words in the corpus | Words in the corpus |
| Output per step | $K$ label classes | $\lvert V \rvert$-way softmax | $k + 1$ binary classifiers |
| Parameters touched per pair ($\lvert V \rvert = 200{,}000$, $d = 300$) | — | 60,000,300 | 2,100 ($k = 5$) |
| What the vectors encode | Whatever separates the labels | Co-occurrence structure | Shifted PMI, compressed to rank $d$ |

The result is a table that any model can reuse.

## GloVe and FastText

Two later static embeddings appear in the course as downloaded tables (planned topics, not yet notes). **GloVe** ([Pennington, Socher and Manning, 2014](#references)), which gave the Twitter vectors of the previous note, fits $u_w \cdot v_c + b_w + b_c \approx \log X_{wc}$ to the co-occurrence matrix $X$ by weighted least squares; like SGNS it fits a function of co-occurrence counts with dot products, so the two end up close. **FastText** ([Bojanowski et al., 2017](#references)) trains SGNS but represents a word as the sum of its character n-gram vectors (`where` → `<wh`, `whe`, `her`, `ere`, `re>`), so related words share parameters and an unseen word still gets a vector.

## Limitations

- **Static.** One vector per word type: `bank` is a single compromise between the river and the money. The [BERT note](/notes/nlp/bert#from-static-to-contextual-embeddings) removes this.
- **Closed vocabulary.** Words below `min_count` or absent from the corpus get no vector; FastText and subword tokenization address this.
- **Window-bound.** Only neighbours within $m$ positions shape a vector, and CBOW ignores their order; negation and long-range dependencies do not enter.
- **Similarity means substitutability.** `good` and `bad`, `hot` and `cold`, `Monday` and `Tuesday` share contexts and end up close, the wrong geometry for sentiment.
- **Data-hungry.** Half a million questions suffice for frequent words such as `word`, not for rare ones; the analogies of the previous note use billions of tokens.
- **Sensitive, not reproducible.** Preprocessing, window, subsampling and $k$ change the neighbours, and multithreaded runs with one seed differ.

## What you should remember

- Skip-gram predicts the context from the centre, CBOW the centre from the context. The network is linear; its first layer $W_{\text{in}}$ is the embedding table, the output layer only a training signal.
- The full softmax updates all $|V|$ output rows per pair; negative sampling classifies the real pair against $k$ pairs drawn from the $3/4$-power unigram distribution and updates $(k + 2) d$ parameters.
- At its optimum SGNS sets $u_o \cdot v_c = \operatorname{PMI}(c, o) - \log k$: the vectors are a low-rank compression of co-occurrence statistics.
- `gensim` trains CBOW with $k = 5$ negatives by default; skip-gram needs `sg=1`, whatever the surrounding text says.

Hierarchical softmax with a Huffman tree (§2.1), negative sampling, the range of $k$ and the $3/4$ power (§2.2), subsampling (§2.3).
- Y. Goldberg, O. Levy. [word2vec Explained: deriving Mikolov et al.'s negative-sampling word-embedding method](https://arxiv.org/abs/1402.3722). arXiv, 2014. The negative-sampling objective and the reason for two tables.
- O. Levy, Y. Goldberg. [Neural Word Embedding as Implicit Matrix Factorization](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2014/hash/b78666971ceae55a8e87efb7cbfd9ad4-Abstract.html). NeurIPS, 2014. SGNS as shifted-PMI factorization (§3.1).
- X. Rong. [word2vec Parameter Learning Explained](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.2738). arXiv, 2014. Softmax gradients for CBOW and skip-gram.
- J. Pennington, R. Socher, C. D. Manning. [GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation](https://aclanthology.org/D14-1162/). EMNLP, 2014.
- O. Levy, Y. Goldberg, I. Dagan. [Improving Distributional Similarity with Lessons Learned from Word Embeddings](https://aclanthology.org/Q15-1016/). *TACL*, 3, 2015. Hyperparameters matter as much as the algorithm; context distribution smoothing (the $3/4$ power as smoothed PMI).
- P. Bojanowski, E. Grave, A. Joulin, T. Mikolov. [Enriching Word Vectors with Subword Information](https://aclanthology.org/Q17-1010/). *TACL*, 5, 2017. FastText.
- D. Jurafsky, J. H. Martin. [Speech and Language Processing](https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/), 3rd edition draft of August 19, 2026, chapter 5 "Embeddings", §5.5 "Word2vec" and §5.7 "Semantic properties of embeddings". Textbook treatment of skip-gram with negative sampling; the effect of the window size.
- T. Mikolov et al. [word2vec](https://code.google.com/archive/p/word2vec/). Project page and reference C implementation (`word2vec.c`), Google Code Archive, 2013. Practical comparison of skip-gram and CBOW ("better for infrequent words"); the CBOW update that adds the whole hidden-layer gradient to every context vector.
- R. Řehůřek, P. Sojka. [Software Framework for Topic Modelling with Large Corpora](https://doi.org/10.13140/2.1.2393.1847). LREC Workshop on New Challenges for NLP Frameworks, 2010. The `gensim` library; defaults from its [Word2Vec API documentation](https://radimrehurek.com/gensim/models/word2vec.html).
- J. Starmer (StatQuest). [Word Embedding and Word2Vec, Clearly Explained!!!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viZrOnJclY0). Video, 2023. The explanation of the cost of one update and how negative sampling reduces it follows this video.
- J. Alammar. [The Illustrated Word2vec](https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-word2vec/). Blog post, 2019. An illustrated walk through skip-gram with negative sampling.
