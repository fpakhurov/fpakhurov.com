A summary is a short text that keeps what matters in a long one. Two definitions of "keeps" give two tasks: an **extractive** summary is made of pieces of the input, and an **abstractive** summary is new text about it. The first is a labelling problem like those of the previous notes; the second is generation, and it is where the encoder–decoder family of the [model-families note](/notes/nlp/model-families) has its natural use. The metric is the same for both and favours the first.

**Notation.** A document $d$, a reference summary $r$, a system summary $s$; $n$-grams of a text are counted with multiplicity.

## Task formulation and data

Input: a document (a news article, a dialogue, a paper). Output: a summary, with the length set by the dataset's convention. Two datasets appear in this note:

| Dataset | Input | Reference | Train size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| XSum ([Narayan, Cohen and Lapata, 2018](#references)) | BBC article | its one-sentence opening summary, written by the journalist | 204,045 |
| SAMSum ([Gliwa et al., 2019](#references)) | a short chat dialogue | a one- or two-sentence third-person summary | 14,732 |

Both references are abstractive: they rephrase, and the XSum sentence is deliberately not in the article. That choice matters for what a metric can measure.

## ROUGE

ROUGE ([Lin, 2004](#references)) counts $n$-gram overlap between the system summary and the reference. The original is recall-oriented ("how much of the reference is covered"), the usual report is the $F_1$ form:

$$
\mathrm{ROUGE\text{-}}n_{\text{recall}} = \frac{|\text{$n$-grams}(s) \cap \text{$n$-grams}(r)|}{|\text{$n$-grams}(r)|}, \qquad
\mathrm{ROUGE\text{-}}n_{\text{prec}} = \frac{|\text{$n$-grams}(s) \cap \text{$n$-grams}(r)|}{|\text{$n$-grams}(s)|},
$$

and $F_1$ is their harmonic mean. ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-2 use unigrams and bigrams; **ROUGE-L** uses the longest common subsequence between the two texts, which rewards in-order matches without requiring adjacency, and `rougeLsum` applies it per sentence. Stemming is usually on. Scores are multiplied by 100.

Toy example: reference *the cat sat on the mat*, system *the cat lay on a mat*. Unigram overlap: `the`, `cat`, `on`, `mat` = 4 of 6 reference unigrams and 4 of 6 system unigrams, so ROUGE-1 $F_1 = 66.7$. Bigram overlap: `the cat` only (the second `the` is not followed by `mat`), 1 of 5, ROUGE-2 $F_1 = 20.0$. A summary that copied the sentence would score 100 on both; one that said *a feline rested on the rug* would score near zero and be correct.

This is the property to keep in mind: ROUGE measures lexical overlap with one reference. It is cheap, reproducible and correlates with human judgements across systems on news summarization; it does not detect a fabricated detail that reuses the article's words, and it penalizes a correct paraphrase.

## Extractive summarization

**Baseline.** For news, take the first $k$ sentences (Lead-3). On datasets with a lead-biased writing style it is hard to beat; on XSum, whose references are written to not be the lead, it is weak. The classical learned approach scores sentences by position, length, term frequency and similarity to the document centroid and picks the top ones ([Nenkova and McKeown, 2011](#references)).

**Neural extractive summarization** is sequence labelling at the sentence level: encode the document, represent each sentence (BERTSum inserts a `[CLS]` before every sentence and reads its vector, [Liu and Lapata, 2019](#references)), and classify each as in or out of the summary. Labels come from the reference by an **oracle**: greedily add the sentences that most increase ROUGE against the reference. The output is a set of whole sentences, grammatical by construction.

The notebook tries a cruder version on SAMSum: label each *token* of the dialogue 1 if the substring covered by some reference-summary token occurs in the dialogue, 0 otherwise, then train a linear head on a frozen BERT to predict the labels and concatenate the positive tokens. On a six-line chat whose reference is *Laura is going to buy a printer.*, the output is

> Laura new : Laura: thinking about Laura: _ : new ? Jamie buy hand one Laura: be

The procedure fails before the model is trained. Token-level labels built by string matching mark every occurrence of `Laura`, `new` and `buy` wherever they appear, so the positive set is a bag of words, not a span, and the "summary" is the bag read in order. Extraction has to select units that are readable on their own (sentences, or at least clauses), and the oracle has to score candidate units, not match tokens. The classifier itself (1,538 trained parameters on frozen features, evaluation loss 0.12) is doing what it was asked; what it was asked for was wrong.

## Abstractive summarization

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Treat summarization as translation from a long text to a short one. An encoder reads the document; a decoder generates the summary token by token, attending to the encoder through cross-attention, trained with the next-token loss on reference summaries.</aside>

The model is the encoder–decoder Transformer of the [translation note](/notes/nlp/transformer-machine-translation), pretrained. T5 ([Raffel et al., 2020](#references)) frames the task as text-to-text with a prefix: the input is `summarize: ` + article, truncated to 1,024 tokens; the target is the reference, truncated to 128. The loss is cross-entropy on the target tokens; padding in the targets is set to $-100$ so it is ignored. Generation uses beam search or greedy decoding, and evaluation decodes the generated ids to text and scores them with ROUGE against the decoded references.

Results on 1,000 XSum validation articles with `t5-small` (60 M parameters):

| Model | ROUGE-1 | ROUGE-2 | ROUGE-L | Mean length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `t5-small`, no XSum fine-tuning | 16.8 | 2.1 | 13.3 | 19.9 tokens |
| `t5-small` after 500 steps of fine-tuning (batch 8, lr $2 \times 10^{-5}$) | not evaluated in the saved run; training loss 3.83 → 3.13 | | | |
| Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, prompted, **10 articles** | 19.1 | 3.7 | 13.2 | — |

Two cautions on reading the table. The pretrained T5 already produces something summary-like because it was multi-task pretrained with the same prefix; its 16.8 ROUGE-1 is the starting point, and the 500-step run (4,000 articles, 2 % of the training set) lowers the training loss by 0.7 nats but was not scored. The prompted 0.5 B model's numbers come from ten articles and are not a measurement of anything beyond "it produces a summary". For reference, the XSum paper's best abstractive model scored 31.9 / 11.5 / 25.8 on the test set, and fine-tuned encoder–decoders of a few hundred million parameters reach the mid-40s on ROUGE-1 ([Lewis et al., 2020](#references)). XSum ROUGE-2 is low for every system because the references are one abstractive sentence.

## Language models as summarizers

An instruction-tuned decoder summarizes from a prompt, with no fine-tuning, in any style the prompt asks for. Against a fine-tuned encoder–decoder it trades: no training data needed, controllable length and focus, better fluency and paraphrase; worse adherence to a dataset's conventions (hence lower ROUGE against its references), higher cost per document, and the same faithfulness risk as any generator. Human evaluations have found large instruction-tuned models preferred to fine-tuned systems on news summarization even where ROUGE says otherwise ([Goyal, Li and Durrett, 2022](#references)), which is a statement about the metric as much as about the models.

## What changed

| | Extractive (classical / neural) | Abstractive (encoder–decoder) | Prompted LM |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Output | sentences of the input | new text | new text |
| Grammatical | by construction | learned | learned |
| Faithful | by construction (but can mislead by omission) | can fabricate | can fabricate |
| Training | sentence labels from an oracle | reference summaries | none |
| ROUGE on abstractive references | low ceiling | highest when fine-tuned | lower; style mismatch |

## Evaluation pitfalls

- **ROUGE rewards copying.** Extractive outputs and near-copies score well; paraphrase scores badly. Report it with a faithfulness check.
- **Fabrication is invisible to overlap.** A summary that adds a wrong number using the article's words loses nothing on ROUGE. Faithfulness metrics (entailment models, QA-based checks) or a judge model, with the caveats of the [LLM evaluation note](/notes/nlp/llm-evaluation), are needed.
- **One reference.** Many good summaries exist; a single reference makes the metric noisy, and implementations differ in tokenization and stemming.
- **Length.** Longer summaries raise recall-based scores; fix or report length.
- **Tiny evaluation sets** (10 articles) measure nothing.

## Limitations

- Extractive methods cannot compress across sentences or resolve pronouns; abstractive ones can invent.
- Input length is bounded by the encoder (1,024 tokens for T5); long documents need chunking and a second pass.
- Domain and style are learned from the references; a model fine-tuned on one-sentence BBC summaries writes one-sentence BBC summaries.

## What you should remember

- Extractive: select units of the input (sentences, via an oracle); abstractive: generate with an encoder–decoder or a prompted decoder.
- ROUGE-$n$ is $n$-gram overlap $F_1$ with the reference; ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence. It rewards copying and cannot see fabrication.
- Token-level extraction with string-matched labels produces word salad; the unit must be readable.
- T5 does summarization as text-to-text with a `summarize:` prefix, targets truncated to 128 tokens and padding ignored in the loss.
- Prompted language models summarize without training and score lower on ROUGE than fine-tuned models while often being preferred by people.
