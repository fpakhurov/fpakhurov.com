The bag-of-words classifier answers "which topic", not "what comes next". To generate text, autocomplete a query, or score whether a sentence is plausible, we need a model of the *sequence*: a probability $P(w_1, \dots, w_T)$ for any token sequence. This note builds the simplest such model from counts, introduces the metric used for every language model in the course, and runs into the problem that a count-based model cannot generalize.

**Notation.** A sequence of $T$ tokens is $w_1, \dots, w_T$, written $w_{1:T}$. $w_{<i}$ means $w_1, \dots, w_{i-1}$. $c(\cdot)$ counts occurrences of a token sequence in the training corpus. $V$ is the vocabulary.

## From a joint probability to a product of conditionals

Every joint distribution over a sequence factorizes by the **chain rule** of probability, with no assumption:

$$
P(w_1, \dots, w_T) = P(w_1)\,P(w_2 \mid w_1)\,P(w_3 \mid w_1, w_2)\cdots = \prod_{i=1}^T P(w_i \mid w_{<i}).
$$

Each factor is the probability of the next token given everything before it. This is the form that every language model in the course takes, from this note to the [GPT note](/notes/nlp/gpt): a language model *is* a next-token predictor, and predicting the next token is a classification problem with $|V|$ classes.

The factors cannot be estimated directly. $P(w_i \mid w_{<i})$ conditions on a prefix that, for $i$ beyond a few tokens, has never occurred before in any corpus. The **Markov assumption** cuts the prefix to the last $n - 1$ tokens:

$$
P(w_i \mid w_{<i}) \approx P(w_i \mid w_{i-n+1}, \dots, w_{i-1}).
$$

The resulting model is an **n-gram language model**. For $n = 2$ (bigram) each token depends on one predecessor; for $n = 3$ (trigram) on two. The assumption is wrong (a pronoun can refer back fifty tokens) and useful (two tokens of context already fix most of the local grammar).

## Baseline: estimate the conditionals by counting

With a finite context, each conditional is a categorical distribution over $V$ given a specific prefix, and its maximum-likelihood estimate is a ratio of counts:

$$
P(w \mid \text{prefix}) = \frac{c(\text{prefix},\, w)}{\sum_{w' \in V} c(\text{prefix},\, w')}.
$$

Two special tokens make the sequence boundaries explicit. The first tokens of a line have no full prefix, so the line is padded on the left with $n - 1$ copies of a start symbol; an end symbol `[EOS]` is appended so that the model can learn to stop.

### Worked example

A toy corpus of three lines and a bigram model ($n = 2$):

```
the cat sat
the cat ran
the dog sat
```

Pad each line with one start symbol `[S]` and end with `[EOS]`. Bigram counts:

| prefix | next | count | $P(\text{next} \mid \text{prefix})$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `[S]` | `the` | 3 | 1 |
| `the` | `cat` | 2 | 2/3 |
| `the` | `dog` | 1 | 1/3 |
| `cat` | `sat` | 1 | 1/2 |
| `cat` | `ran` | 1 | 1/2 |
| `dog` | `sat` | 1 | 1 |
| `sat` | `[EOS]` | 2 | 1 |
| `ran` | `[EOS]` | 1 | 1 |

The probability of the new line `the dog ran` is

$$
P = P(\text{the} \mid \text{[S]})\,P(\text{dog} \mid \text{the})\,P(\text{ran} \mid \text{dog})\,P(\text{[EOS]} \mid \text{ran}) = 1 \cdot \tfrac13 \cdot 0 \cdot 1 = 0.
$$

`dog ran` never occurred, so the whole line gets probability zero. Hold on to this: it is the failure that the rest of the note is about.

### Generation

Because the model factorizes left to right, it generates the same way. Start from the padding, sample $w_1 \sim P(\cdot \mid \text{[S]})$, append it, sample $w_2 \sim P(\cdot \mid w_1)$, and stop at `[EOS]`. The output at each step is fed back as input to the next: generation is **autoregressive**, and this loop is the same in every generative language model in the course. The alternative to sampling, taking the most probable token at each step, is one of the decoding strategies compared in the [GPT note](/notes/nlp/gpt#decoding-turning-probabilities-into-text).

A trigram model trained on a corpus of short Russian jokes, with BPE tokens, produces lines that are locally grammatical and globally meaningless, such as

> мужчина должен постро ##ить дом и вот я однажды мгнове ##нно удалил это сообщение , …

Every run of three consecutive tokens occurred somewhere in the corpus; the sentence as a whole did not and does not cohere. Two tokens of memory are enough for agreement between neighbours and for nothing else.

## Perplexity: from probability to a number

To compare two language models we need a score on held-out text. The sequence probability itself is unusable: it shrinks with length, so a long test set always has a tiny probability whatever the model. Take the logarithm, which turns the product into a sum, and divide by length, which gives a per-token average:

$$
H = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^T \log P(w_i \mid w_{<i}).
$$

This is the **cross-entropy** of the test text under the model, in nats per token (bits if the logarithm is base 2). It is the same quantity as the classifier loss of the [previous note](/notes/nlp/bag-of-words-and-tf-idf#a-linear-classifier-on-counts), seen four ways:

1. **As a sum over classes.** At position $i$ the "true distribution" is one-hot on the observed token $w_i$: $q_i(w) = \mathbb 1[w = w_i]$. The cross-entropy between $q_i$ and the model $P(\cdot \mid w_{<i})$ is $-\sum_{w \in V} q_i(w)\log P(w \mid w_{<i}) = -\log P(w_i \mid w_{<i})$. Every term but one vanishes.
2. **As an expectation.** Averaging over positions is a Monte Carlo estimate of $\mathbb E_{w \sim \text{data}}[-\log P(w \mid \text{context})]$, the expected surprise of the model on text drawn from the true distribution.
3. **As negative log-likelihood.** $T \cdot H = -\log P(w_{1:T})$ by the chain rule, so minimizing $H$ is maximum-likelihood estimation of the sequence.
4. **As entropy plus a divergence.** If the true next-token distribution is $p$, then $H(p, P) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, P)$. The entropy of language is fixed; the model can only reduce the KL term. The decomposition is derived in the [Generative Models course](/notes/generative-models/entropy-and-divergences#kullbackleibler-divergence).

**Perplexity** is the exponential of the cross-entropy:

$$
\mathrm{PPL} = e^{H} = \exp\!\Big(-\frac1T\sum_{i=1}^T \log P(w_i \mid w_{<i})\Big) = P(w_{1:T})^{-1/T}.
$$

The last form is the geometric mean of the inverse per-token probabilities, which is why perplexity reads as an *effective branching factor*: a model with perplexity 50 is, on average, as uncertain as if it chose uniformly among 50 tokens at every step. Two checks fix the scale. A model that assigns probability 1 to every token has $H = 0$ and $\mathrm{PPL} = 1$. A uniform model over $|V|$ tokens has $H = \log|V|$ and $\mathrm{PPL} = |V|$. Lower is better, and the base of the logarithm cancels as long as the same base is used for $\log$ and $\exp$.

On the toy corpus the line `the dog ran` has probability 0, so $H = \infty$ and the perplexity is infinite. On training data the opposite happens: on five lines used both to fit and to score, a unigram model has perplexity 81.7, a trigram model 1.14 and a 10-gram model 1.10. The long-context models have memorized the lines, and the number says nothing about new text.

Every perplexity measured on the joke corpus in this note is a mean over lines: the perplexity of each line is computed separately, and the values are averaged. That weights every line equally, where the formula above weights every token equally, so the values are not the corpus perplexity. A toy example shows the difference: a line of 2 tokens with per-token NLL 1 has perplexity $e \approx 2.72$, a line of 8 tokens with NLL 3 has $e^3 \approx 20.1$. Their mean is 11.4, while the corpus perplexity, the exponential of the summed NLL over the summed token count, is $\exp((2 \cdot 1 + 8 \cdot 3)/10) = e^{2.6} \approx 13.5$. The corpus perplexity is the standard recipe and the one to use for new measurements; the means over lines are kept here because they are the measured results this note reports, comparable only within one table, where every model is scored the same way.

### When two perplexities can be compared

Perplexity scores a model on one test text cut into one set of tokens. Lower perplexity means a better predictor of that text, and two values rank two models only when everything except the model is held fixed ([Jurafsky and Martin](#references), §3.3):

- **The same test text.** Perplexity on news and on tweets measures the texts as much as the models, and a test text that leaked into training gives an artificially low value.
- **The same tokens.** The average is per token, so a tokenizer that cuts the same text into more, easier pieces lowers the number without predicting any better. Across tokenizers, divide the total negative log-likelihood by a shared unit (words, characters or bytes), as the [tokenization note](/notes/nlp/tokenization#what-changed) does.
- **The same vocabulary and the same treatment of unknown words.** A model that maps every rare word to one `[UNK]` token predicts that frequent token easily. Toy example: a uniform model over 10,000 words has perplexity $10{,}000$; keep only the 100 most frequent words plus `[UNK]` and the uniform model has perplexity $101$, although it can no longer tell any rare word from another. Clipping zero probabilities, as in the next section, is another such convention.
- **The same averaging.** Corpus perplexity and the mean of per-line perplexities differ, 13.5 against 11.4 in the toy example above.

## Unseen n-grams make perplexity explode

Split the joke corpus 75/25, fit on the training part and score the held-out part with word tokens. The perplexities are on the order of $10^{46}$ for the unigram model and $10^{48}$ for the trigram model. The cause is the zero from the worked example: any test n-gram that never occurred in training has $P = 0$, and $\log 0 = -\infty$. Probabilities below $10^{-50}$ are clipped to $10^{-50}$ so that the sum stays finite, and the clipped terms dominate everything else. The *more* context the model uses, the more test n-grams are unseen, so trigrams score worse than unigrams. On this measurement the model with the most memory is the worst, and the measurement is the problem: it reports how many test n-grams were unseen, not how well the model predicts.

## Smoothing

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Reserve part of the probability mass for events the training data never showed. The counts stay the estimate for what was seen; what was not seen gets a small, non-zero share instead of zero.</aside>

**Additive (Laplace) smoothing** adds a pseudo-count $\delta$ to every token in every context:

$$
P(w \mid \text{prefix}) = \frac{c(\text{prefix},\, w) + \delta}{\sum_{w' \in V}\big(c(\text{prefix},\, w') + \delta\big)} = \frac{c(\text{prefix},\, w) + \delta}{c(\text{prefix}) + \delta\,|V|}.
$$

The denominator grows by $\delta|V|$, so the mass given to unseen tokens in a context is $\delta(|V| - \text{seen})/(c(\text{prefix}) + \delta|V|)$, shared equally among them. The estimate is the posterior mean of the next-token probabilities under a symmetric Dirichlet prior with $\delta$ pseudo-counts per token ([Conjugate priors](/notes/bayesian-methods/conjugate-priors#dirichlet-categorical)). With $\delta = 1$ and a vocabulary the size of this corpus's BPE vocabulary, 37,580 tokens, a context seen 10 times keeps only $10/(10 + 37{,}580) \approx 0.03\,\%$ of its mass for the tokens actually observed. That is far too much smoothing, which is why add-one is the illustrative method rather than the practical one, but it removes the zeros:

| Tokens | $n = 1$ | $n = 2$ | $n = 3$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Words, $\delta = 1$ | 39,956 | 30,058 | 70,786 |
| BPE, $\delta = 1$ | 2,696 | 3,860 | 15,598 |

Read the table within a row; the two rows are not comparable. A BPE text has more tokens than the same text in words, each easier to predict, so a lower per-token perplexity does not mean a better model, and per word the ranking can reverse (the [tokenization note](/notes/nlp/tokenization#what-changed) does the conversion). Within a row two things are visible. The perplexities are finite and in a range where differences mean something. And the trigram model is *worse* than the bigram model: with more context, each prefix is seen fewer times, the counts $c(\text{prefix})$ are small, and add-one smoothing flattens the distribution towards uniform. The best $n$ is a trade-off between context and data sparsity, and under a crude smoother it is small.

Better smoothers fix the uniform-sharing flaw. **Interpolation** mixes the trigram, bigram and unigram estimates with learned weights, so a rare trigram context falls back on its bigram statistics rather than on uniform. **Kneser–Ney smoothing** ([Kneser and Ney, 1995](#references); [Chen and Goodman, 1999](#references)) subtracts a fixed discount from every seen count and redistributes it in proportion to how many *distinct* contexts a token appears in, which is the state of the art for count-based models. A good perplexity for a modern neural model on English web text is in the tens; for the small joke corpus here, anything below a few hundred would be a reasonable target that add-one smoothing does not reach.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Question | Which label? | What comes next? |
| Statistic | Token counts per document | Counts of $n$-token sequences |
| Output | One distribution over $K$ labels | One distribution over $V$ per position |
| Order | Ignored | Last $n - 1$ tokens |
| Can generate | No | Yes, autoregressively |
| Metric | Accuracy, $F_1$ | Perplexity |

## Limitations

- **Sparsity grows with $n$.** The number of possible n-grams is $|V|^n$. Most are never seen, so the model cannot use more than a few tokens of context however much data it has.
- **No generalization across tokens.** `the cat sat` and `the dog sat` are unrelated events; seeing the first does not raise the probability of the second. Every token is an atom, as in the bag of words. This is the limitation that the [next note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings) addresses.
- **Storage.** The model is a table of counts for every observed prefix. It grows with the corpus and is awkward to compress.
- **Perplexity is intrinsic.** A lower perplexity does not guarantee better generations or a better downstream system; see the [evaluation note](/notes/nlp/corpora-and-evaluation#intrinsic-and-downstream-evaluation). It is also not comparable across different tokenizers, because the per-token average changes when the tokens change.

## What you should remember

- Chain rule: $P(w_{1:T}) = \prod_i P(w_i \mid w_{<i})$. A language model is a next-token classifier over $V$.
- Markov assumption: condition on the last $n-1$ tokens; estimate by count ratios; pad with start and end symbols.
- Cross-entropy $H = -\frac1T\sum_i \log P(w_i \mid w_{<i})$ is the per-token negative log-likelihood; perplexity is $e^{H}$, an effective branching factor; uniform over $V$ gives $|V|$.
- Lower perplexity is better, but only on the same test text, with the same tokenizer and vocabulary, the same handling of unknown words and the same averaging; across tokenizers convert to per-word or per-character units.
- An unseen n-gram gives $P = 0$ and infinite perplexity. Smoothing reserves mass for the unseen; add-$\delta$ is the simplest and over-smooths large vocabularies.
- Generation is autoregressive: sample, append, repeat.

## Next

The n-gram model treats every token as an atom with no relation to any other. It cannot transfer what it learned about `cat sat` to `dog sat`, and with a vocabulary of tens of thousands of atoms, most short sequences are never seen at all. The [next note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings) replaces the atom with a vector, so that similar tokens have similar vectors and whatever the model learns for one of them carries over to its neighbours.
