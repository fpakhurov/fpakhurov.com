Every model in the course so far was trained on the data of its own task. The Transformer trained from scratch on 120,000 news articles in the [architecture note](/notes/nlp/transformers#a-small-encoder-on-ag-news) did not beat a bag of counts. Unlabelled text, by contrast, is unlimited, and the [Word2Vec note](/notes/nlp/word2vec) showed that a prediction task on it yields reusable representations. BERT ([Devlin et al., 2019](#references)) applies the same idea to a full Transformer encoder: pretrain it to fill in blanks, then reuse the whole network.

**Notation.** Input tokens $w_{1:n}$, of which a subset $M$ is selected for prediction; the corrupted input is $\tilde w$; the encoder output at position $t$ is $h_t \in \mathbb R^d$.

## Baseline: a language model objective on an encoder

The natural self-supervised task is next-token prediction, which gives the decoder-only models of the [next note](/notes/nlp/gpt). An *encoder* is bidirectional: position $t$ attends to every position, including $t + 1$. Train it to predict $w_{t+1}$ from $h_t$ and the network can copy the answer from the input through one attention head; the loss goes to zero and nothing is learned. Left-to-right prediction and bidirectional context are incompatible in the same stack.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> Remove the token from the input before asking for it. Replace a random 15 % of the tokens by a placeholder <code>[MASK]</code>, run the encoder with full bidirectional attention, and train it to recover the hidden tokens from everything else in the sentence.</aside>

## Masked language modelling

For each training sequence, 15 % of the token positions are sampled into $M$. For a position in $M$, the input token is replaced by `[MASK]` with probability 0.8, by a random vocabulary token with probability 0.1, and left unchanged with probability 0.1. The model predicts the original token at every position in $M$ through a softmax over the vocabulary, and the loss is the cross-entropy over those positions only:

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{MLM}} = -\sum_{t \in M} \log P(w_t \mid \tilde w_{1:n}), \qquad P(\cdot \mid \tilde w) = \operatorname{softmax}(W_{\text{out}}\, g(h_t)),
$$

where $g$ is a small transform (a linear layer, GELU and LayerNorm) and $W_{\text{out}}$ is tied to the input embedding matrix. The 80/10/10 rule exists because `[MASK]` never appears at fine-tuning time: if the model only ever saw real tokens at unmasked positions, it would learn to ignore them, and the random and unchanged cases force it to build a useful representation of every position.

For a 128-token sequence, about 19 positions are selected; roughly 15 become `[MASK]`, 2 become a random token and 2 stay as they are. The model is trained on 19 predictions per 128 tokens read, a fraction that makes MLM slower to converge per token than next-token prediction, which trains on all 128.

BERT also pretrains a second task, **next-sentence prediction**: the input is two segments, and a classifier on the first position predicts whether the second followed the first in the corpus. Later work found it unnecessary ([Liu et al., 2019](#references)); it survives in BERT's input format.

## The input

A BERT input is `[CLS] segment A [SEP] segment B [SEP]`, padded to the batch length. Three embedding tables are summed: token (WordPiece, 30,522 entries for the uncased English model), position (a learned table of 512 rows, so 512 tokens is the hard maximum), and segment (`token_type_ids`, 2 rows, marking A or B). The sum is normalized and passed to the encoder. `[CLS]` is a token with no content whose output vector is trained, through next-sentence prediction, to summarize the pair; it is the conventional handle for sentence-level tasks.

## The architecture by the numbers

BERT-base is 12 post-norm encoder blocks with $d = 768$, 12 heads of width 64, $d_{\text{ff}} = 3{,}072$ and GELU:

| Component | Parameters |
| --- | --- |
| Token embeddings $30{,}522 \times 768$ | 23.4 M |
| Position embeddings $512 \times 768$ | 0.4 M |
| 12 blocks × $12 d^2$ | 84.9 M |
| Pooler (one linear layer on `[CLS]`) | 0.6 M |
| **Total** | **≈ 110 M** |

The arithmetic is the one from the [architecture note](/notes/nlp/transformers#parameters-of-a-block). BERT-large doubles the depth and widens to 1,024 (340 M). Pretraining used 3.3 billion words of books and Wikipedia; the resulting checkpoint is what the notebooks download in one line.

## From static to contextual embeddings

The output $h_t$ is a vector *for token $t$ in this sentence*. It was computed by twelve rounds of attention over every other token, so it depends on them. The word `bank` gets different vectors in "river bank" and "bank account"; the Word2Vec vector of the [embeddings note](/notes/nlp/word-embeddings#limitations) was one vector for both. This is the sense in which a pretrained encoder produces **contextual embeddings**, and it is the reason the same network is useful for tasks that static vectors could not serve:

- A token's vector can be classified by a linear layer to tag it (the [NER note](/notes/nlp/named-entity-recognition)) or to mark it as the start or end of an answer (the [QA note](/notes/nlp/question-answering)).
- The vector of `[CLS]`, or the mean of all vectors, is a sentence embedding that can be compared with others by cosine similarity, which is what the [semantic search note](/notes/nlp/semantic-search) builds retrieval on.
- The whole stack can be fine-tuned on a labelled task, which the [transfer learning note](/notes/nlp/transfer-learning) measures.

What the vectors contain is an empirical question studied by probing: lower layers carry mostly lexical and syntactic information, upper layers more semantic and task-oriented information ([Tenney, Das and Pavlick, 2019](#references)).

## What the pretrained head can do

The MLM head is a usable model on its own. Feed `Elephants are playing in the zoo with monkeys. The story is about [MASK].` and read the distribution at the masked position:

| Token | $P$ |
| --- | --- |
| elephants | 0.216 |
| monkeys | 0.109 |
| animals | 0.086 |
| elephant | 0.039 |
| children | 0.022 |

The top predictions are the entities of the sentence and their hypernym, read off from a model that was never told what a "story" is or asked a question. This is the mechanism behind **zero-shot** use of BERT, in which a classification task is rephrased as a fill-in-the-blank and the answer is read from the vocabulary; the [transfer learning note](/notes/nlp/transfer-learning#zero-shot-classification-with-the-mlm-head) runs it on AG News and finds both its promise and its limits. The `fill-mask` pipeline in the `transformers` library wraps tokenization, the model and the top-$k$ readout into one call and returns the same numbers.

## What changed

| | Transformer trained on the task | Pretrained encoder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Training data | Task labels (thousands to millions) | Unlabelled text (billions of tokens) |
| Objective | Task loss | Fill in masked tokens |
| Token representation | Learned for this task | Contextual, reusable |
| Direction | Any | Bidirectional |
| Use | One task | Features, zero-shot, fine-tuning |
| Cost to the user | Full training | Download 110 M parameters |

## Limitations

- **No generation.** The encoder predicts hidden tokens given the rest; it cannot continue a text. The decoder-only models of the [next note](/notes/nlp/gpt) can.
- **Pretrain–fine-tune mismatch.** `[MASK]` is seen only in pretraining; the 80/10/10 rule reduces but does not remove the gap.
- **Sample inefficiency.** 15 % of positions carry loss, so each sequence yields few training signals.
- **512 tokens.** The learned position table fixes the maximum length.
- **Static vocabulary and casing.** `bert-base-uncased` cannot distinguish `US` from `us`; the cased variant can, at the cost of a larger effective vocabulary.
- **Needs adaptation.** Out of the box, the zero-shot route is weak; the model's value appears after fine-tuning or when its vectors feed another model.

## What you should remember

- MLM: hide 15 % of tokens (80 % `[MASK]`, 10 % random, 10 % unchanged), predict them with full bidirectional attention, loss on masked positions only.
- Input: `[CLS] A [SEP] B [SEP]` with token + position + segment embeddings; 512 positions maximum.
- BERT-base: 12 blocks, $d = 768$, 12 heads, ≈ 110 M parameters, 85 M of them in the blocks.
- Output vectors are contextual: one vector per token per sentence, usable for tagging, span extraction, sentence similarity and fine-tuning.
- The MLM head answers cloze prompts without any task training, which is zero-shot use.
