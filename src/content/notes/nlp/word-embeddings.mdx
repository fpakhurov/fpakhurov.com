Every model so far has treated a token as an atom. The bag-of-words classifier gave `cat` and `dog` orthogonal coordinates; the n-gram model counted `cat sat` and `dog sat` as unrelated events. Whatever was learned about one word was useless for the other. This note asks what representation would let knowledge transfer between similar words, tries the obvious encodings in turn, and arrives at the dense vector that every later model in the course is built on.

**Notation.** $V$ is the vocabulary, $d$ the embedding dimension. The one-hot vector of token $i$ is $e_i \in \{0, 1\}^{|V|}$ with a single 1 at position $i$. The embedding table is $E \in \mathbb R^{|V| \times d}$, and $E_i$ denotes its $i$-th row.

## Baseline: one-hot vectors

A token is a vector of length $|V|$ with a 1 at its index. Every pair of distinct tokens has dot product 0 and Euclidean distance $\sqrt 2$: `cat` is exactly as far from `dog` as from `therefore`. The representation has no notion of similarity, and its size is the vocabulary size. A model with a first linear layer over one-hot inputs has $|V| \times h$ weights for a hidden size $h$; with $|V| = 3 \times 10^6$ (a web-scale word vocabulary) and $h = 100$, that is $3 \times 10^8$ parameters in one layer, and the activation for a sentence of 20 tokens is a $20 \times 3{,}000{,}000$ matrix that is almost entirely zeros.

## Four attempts

The problem has two parts: the vectors are too large, and they carry no similarity. Each attempt fixes one and reveals the next constraint.

1. **One number per word.** Replace the one-hot vector by its index, a single integer. Size is solved. But the index is arbitrary: `cool` might be 1,502 and `awesome` 7 and `therefore` 1,503. Words that mean the same are far apart, and a word's neighbours in the encoding are meaningless.
2. **Similar numbers for similar words.** Assign indices so that synonyms are adjacent: `cool` 1,502, `awesome` 1,503. Now a single scalar has to encode every axis of similarity at once. `cool` is close to `awesome` on one axis (approval) and to `cold` on another (temperature); a line cannot place it next to both. Sarcasm and polysemy have no room at all.
3. **A short vector of chosen features.** Give each word a few hand-designed coordinates (sentiment, concreteness, part of speech). This is the right shape, but the features have to be invented and labelled by hand for every word, and the ones that matter for a task are rarely the ones we would think to write down.
4. **A short vector of learned features.** Keep the shape of attempt 3, and let the coordinates be learned from data. Nothing is designed; a $d$-dimensional vector per word is a parameter, and training moves it.

<aside class="callout"><strong>Key idea.</strong> A word is a learned point in $\mathbb R^d$ with $d \ll |V|$. Similar words should end up near each other, so that whatever a model learns about one of them applies to its neighbours. Which words are "similar" is decided by the data and the training signal, not by hand.</aside>

## The embedding table

Attempt 4 is a matrix $E \in \mathbb R^{|V| \times d}$. The vector of token $i$ is row $E_i$. In code this is a lookup, and in linear algebra it is a product with the one-hot vector:

$$
E^\top e_i = E_i \in \mathbb R^{d},
$$

which is the matrix-multiplication view that makes the first linear layer of a network over one-hot inputs *equal* to an embedding lookup: a $|V| \times h$ weight matrix multiplied by a one-hot vector just reads out one row. The layer `nn.Embedding(|V|, d)` is that matrix with the multiplication replaced by indexing.

Shapes for a batch: token ids `[B, T]` (batch size, sequence length) become `[B, T, d]` after the lookup. For $|V| = 30{,}000$ and $d = 100$ the table has 3 M parameters, independent of sequence length; a sequence of 20 tokens produces a $20 \times 100$ activation instead of $20 \times 30{,}000$.

Where do the values come from? Three options, used in this order in the course:

- **Learned with the task.** The table is a parameter of a classifier and receives gradients from the classification loss. The [CNN note](/notes/nlp/cnn-text-classification#trainable-embeddings) does this and measures the effect.
- **Pretrained on unlabelled text.** The table is fitted on a large corpus with a self-supervised objective, then used as fixed input features. The [next note](/notes/nlp/word2vec) builds the objective.
- **Downloaded.** Tables trained by others on billions of tokens, such as the GloVe vectors trained on Twitter used below.

## Reading a vector space

Once words are points, the geometry can be inspected. Three tools recur throughout the course.

**Cosine similarity** compares directions and ignores length:

$$
\cos(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^d u_j v_j}{\sqrt{\sum_j u_j^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_j v_j^2}} \in [-1, 1].
$$

Lengths of embedding vectors vary for reasons unrelated to meaning (frequent words tend to have larger norms), so the angle is the usual measure. A 32-dimensional model trained on a few hundred thousand Quora questions lists the nearest neighbours of `word` as `sentence` (0.836), `words`, `verb`, `phrase`, `sentences`, `pronounce`, `noun`: words that occur in the same kind of question. "Similar" here means *used in similar contexts*, which is the only signal the training had.

**Analogies** test whether differences between vectors are meaningful. If the direction from `man` to `woman` encodes gender, then adding it to `king` should land near `queen`:

$$
v_{\text{king}} - v_{\text{man}} + v_{\text{woman}} \approx v_{\text{queen}}.
$$

With 100-dimensional GloVe vectors trained on Twitter, the nearest word to that sum (excluding the three inputs) is `queen` with cosine 0.705, followed by `prince` (0.667) and `mother` (0.644). The same arithmetic with `latte` − `milk` + `water` returns `macchiato` (0.608), `cappuccino`, `espresso`, `coffee`: the drink axis survives the substitution, the specific answer is debatable. And `head` − `foot` + `sock` gives `pants` (0.621), `butt`, `blow`, with `beanie` (the hat one might expect) in sixth place. Analogies are a *qualitative* probe: when they work, they show that some directions in the space are consistent; when they return junk, they show which regularities the corpus did not contain. They are not a proof that the space is linear in meaning, and the standard analogy benchmarks are known to be sensitive to how the search excludes the input words ([Linzen, 2016](#references)).

**Projection to 2-D.** To look at the space, reduce $d$ to 2. **PCA** finds the directions of largest variance: for a centred matrix $X \in \mathbb R^{n \times d}$ of $n$ word vectors it solves $\min_W \|X W W^\top - X\|_F^2$ over $W \in \mathbb R^{d \times 2}$ with orthonormal columns, and the solution is the first two right singular vectors of $X$. The plot is a linear shadow of the space: global structure is preserved, local neighbourhoods are often crushed. **t-SNE** ([van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008](#references)) instead places points so that pairs that are close in $\mathbb R^d$ stay close in the plane, at the price of distorting large distances and depending on a `perplexity` setting; clusters in a t-SNE plot are real, distances between clusters are not. Both are for looking, not for measuring.

### Worked example

Three words in $d = 2$ (illustrative values):

$$
v_{\text{cat}} = (0.9,\ 0.4),\qquad v_{\text{dog}} = (0.8,\ 0.5),\qquad v_{\text{therefore}} = (-0.3,\ 0.9).
$$

$$
\cos(\text{cat}, \text{dog}) = \frac{0.72 + 0.20}{\sqrt{0.97}\,\sqrt{0.89}} = \frac{0.92}{0.929} = 0.990,
\qquad
\cos(\text{cat}, \text{therefore}) = \frac{-0.27 + 0.36}{\sqrt{0.97}\,\sqrt{0.90}} = \frac{0.09}{0.934} = 0.096.
$$

Two animal words point the same way; the connective is nearly orthogonal. Under one-hot encoding all three cosines would be 0.

## From words to sentences, for now

A classifier needs one vector per document, and an embedding table gives one per token. The simplest aggregate is the **mean** of the token vectors (skipping tokens outside the vocabulary, and returning zeros if none remain). With a 100-dimensional table that gives a 100-dimensional document vector, a 300-fold reduction from the 29,350-dimensional TF-IDF vector, and it already supports a nearest-neighbour search: embed a query question, compute its cosine against every stored question, return the top $k$. Averaging discards order and lets frequent words dominate, which is why the [CNN](/notes/nlp/cnn-text-classification) and [RNN](/notes/nlp/recurrent-networks) notes replace it with models that read the sequence.

## Limitations

- **One vector per type.** `bank` has one vector whether the sentence is about rivers or money. The vector is a compromise between senses, and nothing in the representation knows which sense is meant. This is the limitation that *contextual* embeddings remove in the [BERT note](/notes/nlp/bert#from-static-to-contextual-embeddings).
- **Closed vocabulary.** A word not in the table has no vector. Subword tokens from the [tokenization note](/notes/nlp/tokenization) reduce the problem; character n-gram models remove it, at a cost.
- **Similarity is co-occurrence.** Antonyms (`hot`, `cold`) occur in the same contexts and end up close. Whether that is a bug depends on the task.
- **The space reflects the corpus.** Vectors trained on Twitter reproduce Twitter's usage, including its biases and stereotypes, and analogies make those measurable ([Bolukbasi et al., 2016](#references)).
- **Averaging is lossy.** A mean of word vectors is a bag of words in a smaller space; order and negation are still gone.

## What you should remember

- One-hot vectors are $|V|$-dimensional and pairwise orthogonal: no similarity, no transfer.
- An embedding table $E \in \mathbb R^{|V|\times d}$ maps each token to a learned row; `nn.Embedding` is a one-hot matrix product written as a lookup.
- Cosine similarity compares directions; nearest neighbours in a learned space are words used in similar contexts.
- Analogies and 2-D projections are qualitative probes of the geometry, not metrics.
- Static embeddings give one vector per word type regardless of context.
